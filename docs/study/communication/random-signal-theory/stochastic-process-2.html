Stochastic Processes - II

Random Signal Theory — Impulse Stream Sampling and Spectral Replication, Random Sequence, Poisson Process (Intensity, Count Statistics, Arrival Time)

Published

September 28, 2026

1 Overall flow

This document is composed of two branches.

  1. Sampling: Multiplying a continuous-time signal by an impulse train replicates the spectrum in the frequency domain at intervals of \(\omega_s\). When applied to a random process, a band-limited steady-state process is represented as a discrete-time random sequence without loss.
  2. Poisson Process: A point process that models “events whose timing is unknown.” From the small-\(\Delta t\) law, it is derived that the number of events within an interval follows a Poisson distribution.

Both topics have in common that they deal with points on the time axis. In sampling, points are placed on a deterministic lattice \(kT_s\), while in the Poisson process, the positions of the points themselves are random.

2 Impulse Column Sampling

2.1 Definition

impulse train with period \(T_s\)

\[ p_{T_s}(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT_s) \]

If defined as , sampling is the product of the signal and the impulse train.

\[ x_s(t)=x(t)\,p_{T_s}(t)=\sum_{n}x(nT_s)\,\delta(t-nT_s) \]

The second equality comes from \(x(t)\delta(t-t_0)=x(t_0)\delta(t-t_0)\). The sample value \(x(nT_s)\) is stored as the area of each impulse.

2.2 Fourier Series Review

To obtain the Fourier transform of an impulse train, a Fourier series is required.

Orthogonality. When the periods are \(T\) and \(\omega_0=2\pi/T\), the complex exponent \(\{e^{jk\omega_0 t}\}_{k\in\mathbb Z}\) is orthogonal over one period.

\[ \int_{-T/2}^{T/2} e^{jk\omega_0 t}\,\overline{e^{jl\omega_0 t}}\,dt=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} e^{j(k-l)\omega_0 t}\,dt= \begin{cases}T & k=l\\ 0 & k\neq l\end{cases} \]

If \(k\neq l\), the integral is 0 because it rotates an integer number of times during one period, and if \(k=l\), the integrand is 1.

When both sides of Coefficient. \(x(t)=\sum_k c_k e^{jk\omega_0 t}\) are multiplied by \(e^{-jl\omega_0 t}\) and integrated over one period, only the \(k=l\) term remains.

\[ \boxed{c_k=\frac{1}{T}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}x(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\,dt} \]

NoteSource of \(1/T\)

\(1/T\) is the basis norm squared \(\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}|e^{jk\omega_0 t}|^2dt=T\). It has the same structure as the expanded coefficient of an orthogonal basis in finite dimensions, \(c_k=\langle x,e_k\rangle/\langle e_k,e_k\rangle\). \(c_0\) is the one-period average (DC component).

2.3 Fourier transform of complex exponential: \(\mathcal F\{e^{j\omega_0 t}\}=2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)\)

The transformation pair is set to the following convention.

\[ X(j\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t)e^{-j\omega t}dt,\qquad x(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} X(j\omega)e^{j\omega t}d\omega \]

For \(x(t)=e^{j\omega_0 t}\), the defining expression \(\int e^{j(\omega_0-\omega)t}dt\) does not converge in the classical sense because the absolute value of the integrand is 1. Therefore, we consider a candidate and verify it using the inverse transform. Let \(X(j\omega)=A\,\delta(\omega-\omega_0)\).

\[ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int A\,\delta(\omega-\omega_0)e^{j\omega t}d\omega=\frac{A}{2\pi}e^{j\omega_0 t} \;\Longrightarrow\; A=2\pi . \]

\[ \boxed{\mathcal F\{e^{j\omega_0 t}\}=2\pi\,\delta(\omega-\omega_0)} \]

Interpretation. The spectrum of a signal rotating forever at a single frequency exists only at that single point of frequency. The coefficient \(2\pi\) is a value that compensates for the \(1/2\pi\) convention of the inverse transform. In particular, if \(\omega_0=0\), then \(\mathcal F\{1\}=2\pi\delta(\omega)\).

Meaning as a distribution. If the integral is cut at \([-L,L]\)

\[ \int_{-L}^{L}e^{j(\omega_0-\omega)t}dt=\frac{2\sin\big((\omega-\omega_0)L\big)}{\omega-\omega_0}. \]

This function tapers at \(L\to\infty\) with height \(2L\) and width \(\sim 1/L\), and the area is fixed at \(\int\frac{2\sin(uL)}{u}du=2\pi\). Therefore, the limit when integrating over the test function is \(2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_0)\), and the equation should be understood in this sense.

2.4 Fourier transform of an impulse train

① Fourier Series. \(p_{T_s}(t)\) has a period \(T_s\), and within one period \([-T_s/2,T_s/2)\), there is only one impulse \(t=0\). Due to the sifting property

\[ c_k=\frac{1}{T_s}\int_{-T_s/2}^{T_s/2}\delta(t)e^{-jk\omega_s t}dt=\frac{1}{T_s}\quad(\forall k), \qquad p_{T_s}(t)=\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}e^{jk\omega_s t},\quad \omega_s=\frac{2\pi}{T_s}. \]

All harmonic components have the same magnitude and phase, so they simultaneously undergo constructive interference only at \(t=nT_s\) to create an impulse.

② Term-wise transformation. If you use linearity and the above result

\[ \boxed{P(j\omega)=\mathcal F\{p_{T_s}(t)\}=\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_k 2\pi\,\delta(\omega-k\omega_s)=\frac{2\pi}{T_s}\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\delta(\omega-k\omega_s)} \]

In general, if the series coefficient of a periodic signal is \(c_k\), then it is \(X(j\omega)=\sum_k 2\pi c_k\,\delta(\omega-k\omega_0)\). That is, the position of the impulse is \(k\omega_0\), and the area is \(2\pi c_k\).

2.4.1 Coefficient \(2\pi/T_s\) Dissection

Since \(\omega_s=2\pi/T_s\) is already in the formula, the preceding coefficient may appear redundant, but the two have different roles.

Element Role Source
\(\delta(\omega-k\omega_s)\) of \(\omega_s\) Position of impulse Period \(T_s\) → Fundamental frequency
Part of the preceding \(1/T_s\) impulse area Fourier series coefficients \(c_k\)
Part of the preceding \(2\pi\) impulse area \(\omega\) area transformation convention (\(e^{j\omega_0t}\leftrightarrow2\pi\delta\))

Since the area is numerically equal to \(\omega_s\), writing \(P(j\omega)=\omega_s\sum_k\delta(\omega-k\omega_s)\) is equivalent. In this case, the preceding \(\omega_s\) represents the area, not the location.

Assuming there are no coefficients leads to a contradiction. Letting \(P(j\omega)=\sum_k\delta(\omega-k\omega_s)\) and performing the inverse transform, by the Poisson sum formula \(\sum_k e^{jk\omega_s t}=T_s\sum_n\delta(t-nT_s)\)

\[ \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_k e^{jk\omega_s t}=\frac{T_s}{2\pi}\,p_{T_s}(t)\neq p_{T_s}(t). \]

2.4.2 Verification

  • Dimension: The unit of \(p_{T_s}(t)\) is \(1/\mathrm{s}\), and passing through \(\int dt\), the transformation is dimensionless. The right-hand side \(\frac{2\pi}{T_s}[1/\mathrm s]\times\delta(\omega)[\mathrm s]\) is also dimensionless.
  • \(f\) Notation: \(x(t)=\int X(f)e^{j2\pi ft}df\). In the \(x(t)=\int X(f)e^{j2\pi ft}df\) convention, it is \(e^{j2\pi f_0t}\leftrightarrow\delta(f-f_0)\), so \(P(f)=\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_k\delta(f-k/T_s)\). If changed to \(\delta(f)=\delta(\omega/2\pi)=2\pi\delta(\omega)\), it matches the result of the \(\omega\) range. That is, \(2\pi\) is a product of the notation convention, and \(1/T_s\) is the intrinsic scale.

2.5 Spectrum of the sampled signal

The product in the time domain is the convolution in the frequency domain (\(\omega\) is attached in the convention \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\)).

\[ X_s(j\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}X(j\omega)*P(j\omega) =\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{T_s}\sum_k X(j\omega)*\delta(\omega-k\omega_s) =\boxed{\frac{1}{T_s}\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}X\big(j(\omega-k\omega_s)\big)} \]

\(2\pi\) of \(2\pi/T_s\) is canceled out by the \(1/2\pi\) of the convolution, and the remaining \(1/T_s\) becomes the gain of each replica. The original spectrum is replicated at \(\omega_s\) intervals.

2.6 Aliasing and Full Restoration

Let’s call it \(X(j\omega)=0\) for \(|\omega|>\omega_m\).

  • \(\omega_s>2\omega_m\) : The duplicates \([k\omega_s-\omega_m,\,k\omega_s+\omega_m]\) do not overlap. If only the baseband duplicates are selected using an ideal low-pass filter (LPF), \(x(t)\) is restored.
  • \(\omega_s<2\omega_m\) : Adjacent copies overlap (aliasing). Since the original component and the duplicate component are added together at the overlapping part, they cannot be separated by any filter.
TipDetailed conditions of the restoration filter

Since the gain of the replica is \(1/T_s\), the passband gain of the LPF must be \(T_s\) to accurately recover the original signal.

\[ H(j\omega)=\begin{cases}T_s & |\omega|\le\omega_c\\ 0 & |\omega|>\omega_c\end{cases},\qquad \omega_m\le\omega_c\le\omega_s-\omega_m . \]

The cutoff frequency can be placed anywhere in the empty band between the two copies, not just \(\omega_c=\omega_m\). If set to \(\omega_c=\omega_s/2\), it becomes \(h(t)=\mathrm{sinc}(t/T_s)\) (normalized sinc) and yields the interpolation formula \(x(t)=\sum_n x(nT_s)\,\mathrm{sinc}\big((t-nT_s)/T_s\big)\).

3 Sampling of Random Processes and Random Sequences

3.1 Random Sequence

A random sequence is a random process with integer time indices, that is, a discrete-time random process.

\[ \{X(l)\}_{l\in\mathbb Z}\quad(\text{인덱스는 정수, 값은 실수 가능}) \]

Sampling the continuous-time process \(X(t)\) at intervals \(T\) results in a random sequence.

\[ \tilde X(l)\equiv X(lT). \]

3.2 Sampling Theorem for Random Processes

For deterministic signals, we examined whether the Fourier transform \(X(j\omega)\) is band-limited. Since the sample path of a random process usually has infinite energy and therefore no Fourier transform exists, we use the Power Spectral Density (PSD) as the reference.

ImportantCleanup

WSS If the process \(X(t)\) satisfies \(S_X(f)=0\) for \(|f|>W\) and the sample interval is \(T<\dfrac{1}{2W}\), then the discrete-time sequence \(X(lT)\) represents the original process.

Here, “expresses” does not mean point-by-point convergence of individual sample paths, but mean-square in the sense of

\[ \lim_{L\to\infty}E\!\left[\Big|X(t)-\sum_{l=-L}^{L}X(lT)\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\Big(\frac{t-lT}{T}\Big)\Big|^2\right]=0 \]

This means that holds. The difference from the deterministic sampling theorem is that the concept of stochastic convergence is required in random processes.

3.3 Correlation function of a sampled sequence

For the WSS process \(X(t)\), \(R_X(\tau)=E[X(t)X(t+\tau)]\) depends only on the lag \(\tau\). The correlation function of the sampled sequence is

\[ R_{\tilde X}(k)=E\big[\tilde X(l)\tilde X(l+k)\big]=E\big[X(lT)X(lT+kT)\big]=\boxed{R_X(kT)} \]

In the final equality, the WSS property (depending only on the lag \(kT\), independent of \(l\)) was applied. Therefore, \(\tilde X\) is also a WSS sequence, and its correlation function is the continuous correlation function sampled at intervals of \(T\).

3.4 PSD of sampled sequence: Probabilistic version of spectral replication

The PSD of a sequence at normalized frequency \(\nu\) (cycles/sample) is defined as the discrete-time Fourier transform.

\[ S_{\tilde X}(\nu)=\sum_{k}R_{\tilde X}(k)e^{-j2\pi\nu k}. \]

If you substitute \(R_X(kT)=\int S_X(f)e^{j2\pi fkT}df\) and use the Poisson sum formula \(\sum_k e^{j2\pi(fT-\nu)k}=\sum_m\delta(fT-\nu-m)=\frac1T\sum_m\delta\!\big(f-\frac{\nu+m}{T}\big)\)

\[ \boxed{S_{\tilde X}(\nu)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z}S_X\!\left(\frac{\nu+m}{T}\right)} \]

is exactly the same structure as the deterministic signal \(X_s(j\omega)=\frac1{T_s}\sum_kX(j(\omega-k\omega_s))\). \(S_X\) is limited to \(|f|\le W\), and if it is \(1/T>2W\), the copies do not overlap, so \(S_X\) can be read back from \(S_{\tilde X}\). This is the frequency domain meaning of the PSD reference sampling theorem.

4 Poisson Process: Definition and Basic Properties

4.1 Point process and counting process

A point process is a mathematical model of a random event occurring at a specific point in time (or space). Examples: the arrival time of a packet in a network, the position of a star on the celestial sphere.

Taking packet arrival as an example, the arrival time is random because it is impossible to know exactly when the user will send the next request. This is described in terms of two quantities.

  • Counting Process \(N_t\): Start of observation. Number of events that occurred from \(t=0\) to \(t\). \(N_0=0\).
  • Event Time \(W_1<W_2<\cdots\) : The time when the \(i\)-th event occurred. The vector containing event times up to \(t\) is written as \(\mathbf W\in\mathbb R^{N_t}\) (the dimension itself is random).

The interval coefficient is obtained from the difference of the cumulative coefficients.

\[ N_{t_1,t_2}=N_{t_2}-N_{t_1},\qquad N_t=N_{0,t}. \]

4.1.1 interval endpoint convention

Depending on how \(N_t\) is defined, whether the endpoint of the interval counted by \(N_{t_1,t_2}\) is included depends on the definition.

\[ N_t=\#\{i:W_i\le t\}\;\Rightarrow\;N_{t_2}-N_{t_1}=\#\{i:\,t_1<W_i\le t_2\} \]

\[ N_t=\#\{i:W_i< t\}\;\Rightarrow\;N_{t_2}-N_{t_1}=\#\{i:\,t_1\le W_i< t_2\} \]

\(N_t\) Definition Continuity of the sample path Interval counted by \(N_{t_1,t_2}\)
\(\#\{W_i\le t\}\) (“\(t\)”, standard) accidental genus (càdlàg) \((t_1,t_2]\)
\(\#\{W_i< t\}\) Left Continuous \([t_1,t_2)\)

“number of events occurring up to \(t\)” is consistent with the properties of the coincidence, and \((t_1,t_2]\) is the one that matches. Although some formulas in this document are written as \([t_1,t_2)\) by convention, since event times in a Poisson process follow a continuous distribution (\(\Pr(W_i=t_1)=0\)), the distributions of the two conventions are identical. The difference becomes an actual issue in general point processes or discrete-time models where events can be clustered at deterministic times (e.g., slot boundaries). Regardless of the convention, the interval must be standardized to a semi-open interval so that adjacent intervals are partitioned without overlap or omission, as in \((t_0,t_1]\cup(t_1,t_2]\cup\cdots=(t_0,t_n]\).

4.2 Properties of the sample path

The sample path of \(N(t)\) (=\(N_t\)) is a step function and has the following properties.

  • Non-decrease: The number of events only increases or remains the same and does not decrease.
  • Integer value: Since it counts events \(0,1,2,\dots\)
  • Casual: The count is updated to a new value the very moment the event occurs. \(N(W_i)=\lim_{s\downarrow W_i}N(s)\).
  • Unit Jump: \(N(W_i)-N(W_i^-)=1\) at each jump time.
  • Between events, \(N(t)\) is constant.

4.2.1 Why Unit Jumps: Orderliness

“One event increments the count by exactly 1” is merely the definition of the counting process (weight of 1 for each event). The jump size is

\[ \text{점프 크기}=\#\{\text{그 시각에 발생한 사건 수}\}\times1 \]

Since , if two events overlap at the same time, the jump becomes 2. In fact, there exist non-simple point processes that allow batch arrival. Therefore, for a “unit jump” to hold, the property that at most one event at a time is additionally required, and the Poisson process guarantees this under the following condition.

\[ \Pr\big[N_{t,t+h}\ge 2\big]=o(h)\qquad(h\to0) \]

Proof (Probability of simultaneous events = 0). Divide \([0,T]\) into Z\(h=T/n\) subintervals of length \(n\). By the union bound

\[ \Pr\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{\text{소구간 }i\text{ 에 사건 }\ge2\}\Big)\le n\cdot o(T/n)=T\cdot\frac{o(T/n)}{T/n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0. \]

Since two events at the same time fall into the same subinterval in any partition, the probability of this happening is less than this upper bound and is 0. In other words, almost certainly (as) all event times are different.

Another perspective. As shown later, the arrival interval \(W_i-W_{i-1}\) follows an exponential distribution (continuous distribution), so it is \(\Pr(W_i=W_{i-1})=0\).

In models where co-arrival is actually important, the compound Poisson process \(\sum_{i\le N(t)}Y_i\) is used. The arrival times are Poisson, and the jump size of each arrival \(Y_i\) follows a random distribution.

4.3 Intensity and Infinitesimal Interval Law

The probability law of a point process is defined by strength \(\lambda(t,N_t;\mathbf W)\). For a sufficiently small \(\Delta t\)

\[ \boxed{\Pr\big[N_{t,t+\Delta t}=1\;\big|\;N_t;\mathbf W\big]=\lambda(t,N_t;\mathbf W)\,\Delta t+o(\Delta t)} \]

Symbol Meaning
\(N_{t,t+\Delta t}\) Number of events in the infinitesimal interval \((t,t+\Delta t]\)
\(N_t\), \(\mathbf W\) Observations to date (number of events, times of events) = History of the process
\(\lambda(\cdot)\) Instantaneous occurrence rate, unit is events/s
\(o(\Delta t)\) \(\lim_{\Delta t\to0}o(\Delta t)/\Delta t=0\) In Hang

Intuition: Speed × Time. Just as traveling at a speed of \(v\) for a time of \(\Delta t\) results in a distance of \(v\Delta t\), if the occurrence rate is \(\lambda\) for a time of \(\Delta t\), the probability of an event is approximately \(\lambda\Delta t\) (dimensionless). For example, if \(\lambda=2\)/s, \(\Delta t=1\) ms, the probability is approximately \(0.002\).

\(\lambda\) does not determine the number of events. \(\lambda=5\) /s does not mean that exactly 10 events occur in 2 seconds. \(\lambda\) is a quantity that determines the probability in an infinitesimal interval, and the actual number is random (we will show later that the average is \(\lambda T\)).

Read as definition of intensity.

\[ \lambda(t,N_t;\mathbf W)=\lim_{\Delta t\to0}\frac{\Pr[N_{t,t+\Delta t}=1\mid N_t;\mathbf W]}{\Delta t} \]

The idea is similar to giving the probability of a small interval \(f(x)dx\), but since \(\lambda\) is not a density but a rate, it can be greater than 1. \(\Delta t\) must be sufficiently small so that it becomes \(\lambda\Delta t\le1\).

Why is it \(+o(\Delta t)\) and not an equal sign? In the Poisson process of constant intensity \(\lambda\), exactly

\[ \Pr[N_{t,t+\Delta t}=1]=\lambda\Delta t\,e^{-\lambda\Delta t}=\lambda\Delta t-\underbrace{(\lambda\Delta t)^2+\tfrac12(\lambda\Delta t)^3-\cdots}_{o(\Delta t)} \]

. \(\lambda\Delta t\) is a first-order approximation, and the rest are of a higher order than \((\Delta t)^2\) and are ignored compared to \(\Delta t\).

4.3.1 Notation: ; and ,

In \(\lambda(t,N_t;\mathbf W)\), \(\Pr[\,\cdot\mid N_t;\mathbf W]\), ; and , have mathematically the same role (all arguments/conditions), and the only difference is the notational grouping.

  • , : List of factors at the same level
  • ; : The first part is the current status \((t,N_t)\), and the second part is the history \(\mathbf W\), distinguishing the roles.

That is, \(\lambda(t,N_t;\mathbf W)=\lambda(t,N_t,\mathbf W)\) and \(\Pr[\cdot\mid N_t;\mathbf W]=\Pr[\cdot\mid N_t,\mathbf W]\). For reference, since \(N_t=\dim\mathbf W\), \(N_t\) is information determined from \(\mathbf W\).

; conventions in other fields meaning
\(p(x;\theta)\) \(\theta\) is a fixed parameter, not a random variable (frequency caution). \(p(x\mid\theta)\) is \(\theta\), which is a random variable (Bayesian)
\(I(X;Y)\), \(I(X;Y\mid Z)\) Distinguishing two variables in mutual information; \mid is the condition

Here, \(\mathbf W\) is not a fixed parameter but a random history, so it differs from the \(p(x;\theta)\) convention. In point process literature, the same object is often written as the conditional strength \(\lambda^*(t)=\lambda(t\mid\mathcal H_t)\) for the history \(\mathcal H_t\).

4.3.2 Classification of point processes according to intensity function

Strength Process Example
\(\lambda(t,N_t;\mathbf W)\equiv\lambda_0\) Normal (Uniform) Poisson Packet arrival with constant average traffic
\(\lambda(t)\) Abnormal (Non-vehicle) Poisson Traffic change by time of day (Day ↑, Night ↓)
\(\lambda\) depends on \(\mathbf W\) self-excitation process (Hawkes et al.) earthquake aftershock, burst traffic

The infinitesimal interval law is a framework that defines the intensity of not only Poisson processes but the entire point process, and a Poisson process is a case where the intensity does not depend on hysteresis.

4.4 Normal Poisson Process and Abnormal Poisson Process

The Poisson process is memoryless: its strength does not depend on the process history \((N_t,\mathbf W)\).

  • Stationary: \(\lambda(t,N_t;\mathbf W)=\lambda_0\) (Constant). Independent of time and history.
  • nonstationary: \(\lambda(t)\). It changes over time but is still unrelated to history.

Interpretation of Coin Toss. Divide the time axis into \(\Delta t\) squares, and for each square, toss a coin that produces an “event” with probability \(\lambda_0\Delta t\). Each toss is independent of past (and future) results. In the case of anomalies, only the probability changes to \(t\) in the square at time \(\lambda(t)\Delta t\), while the independence remains the same.

WarningDistinguish between “normal” and “independent”

The source of independence between intervals is memorylessness (hysteresis-independence), not stationarity (constant strength). Even in an anomalous Poisson process, the number of events in non-overlapping intervals is independent of each other. This is the reason why the derivation of the count statistics that follows holds true in the general form \(\lambda(t)\).

4.5 Two Laws of Probability

  1. count statistics: The probability that there are \(n\) events in the interval \([t_1,t_2)\) \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]\).
  2. Time of occurrence: Joint distribution of the times of the first \(n\) events within the observation interval \((W_1,\dots,W_n)\).

4.5.1 Why the time of occurrence is important

Information inclusion relationship. The count is a function of time.

\[ N_{t_1,t_2}=\sum_i\mathbf 1\{t_1<W_i\le t_2\} \]

If the joint distribution of event times is known, the count distribution of any interval is determined, but the count distribution alone cannot tell where the events are clustered within the interval. For example, the information that there are “2” in \([0,1)\) cannot distinguish between \(\{0.10,0.15\}\) (burst) and \(\{0.2,0.8\}\) (variance). The time distribution is a complete description of the process, and the count is a summary of it.

Meaning in communication systems.

  • Queueing Delay: Even if the average arrival rate is the same, a queue builds up if arrival times are concentrated.
  • Collision Probability (ALOHA type): Whether two transmissions overlap depends not on the number of times but on the time difference \(|W_i-W_j|\).
  • Estimation/Testing: The likelihood used to estimate the maximum likelihood of \(\lambda\) with the observed event times or to test the Poisson assumption is the joint density of the times.

The reason count statistics are derived first is that they are simpler summaries, not because the time of occurrence is less important. The rule of the time of occurrence is derived from the count results in the distribution of arrival times .

5 Derivation of count statistics

Objective: In a Poisson process (including abnormal) with intensity \(\lambda(t)\)

\[ \Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]=e^{-C}\frac{C^n}{n!},\qquad C=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\lambda(\alpha)\,d\alpha . \]

Strategy: Fix \(t_1\) as the origin and treat \(t_2\) as the variable. Formulate a differential equation for \(t_2\) based on how the probability changes when \(t_2\) is increased by \(\delta\).

5.1 Adjacent interval decomposition

If we consider two adjacent intervals \([t_1,t_2)\), \([t_2,t_2+\delta)\)

\[ N_{t_1,t_2+\delta}=N_{t_1,t_2}+N_{t_2,t_2+\delta}. \tag{1} \]

The goal is to express as the probability of \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2+\delta}=n]\) in two subintervals.

5.2 Total probability decomposition

For the total to be \(n\), there must be exactly \(k\) in the next interval when there are \(n-k\). The possible \((k,n-k)\) is \((0,n),(1,n-1),\dots,(n,0)\), and since the events for different \(k\) are disjoint, the probabilities can be added.

\[ \Pr[N_{t_1,t_2+\delta}=n]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Pr\big[N_{t_1,t_2}=k,\;N_{t_2,t_2+\delta}=n-k\big]. \]

Independence. Due to memorylessness, the intensity of the subsequent section is independent of the history of the preceding section, therefore

\[ \Pr[N_{t_2,t_2+\delta}=j\mid N_{t_1,t_2}=k]=\Pr[N_{t_2,t_2+\delta}=j] \]

, and therefore

\[ \Pr[N_{t_1,t_2+\delta}=n]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=k]\;\Pr[N_{t_2,t_2+\delta}=n-k]. \tag{2} \]

5.3 Three cases of infinitesimal intervals

If \(\delta\) is sufficiently small, there are three possible outcomes in the following interval: “0 / 1 / 2 or more”, and the sum of the probabilities of the three is 1.

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr[N_{t_2,t_2+\delta}=1]&=\lambda(t_2)\,\delta+o(\delta) &&\text{(강도의 정의)}\\ \Pr[N_{t_2,t_2+\delta}\ge2]&=o(\delta) &&\text{(단순성)}\\ \Pr[N_{t_2,t_2+\delta}=0]&=1-\lambda(t_2)\,\delta+o(\delta) &&\text{(합이 1)} \end{aligned} \tag{3} \]

The third equation is a simplified version of \(1-[\lambda\delta+o(\delta)]-o(\delta)\).

Note\(o(\cdot)\) notation arithmetic

\(o(\delta)\) is not a specific function, but a notation meaning “some function satisfying \(o(\delta)/\delta\to0\)”. \(o(\delta)\), appearing twice in the same equation, can be different functions (one being \(\delta^2\), the other \(\delta^3+\delta^{3/2}\), etc.). Therefore, while the exact value of \(-o(\delta)-o(\delta)\) is unknown, we know that the result is again \(o(\delta)\). The rule that holds:

  • \(o(\delta)\pm o(\delta)=o(\delta)\) (finite sum)
  • \(c\cdot o(\delta)=o(\delta)\) (\(c\) is a bounded constant independent of \(\delta\). e.g.: \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n-2]\))
  • \(\delta\cdot O(1)\) is not \(o(\delta)\) — the first term survives.

5.4 From difference equation to differential equation

Substitute (3) into (2). If we divide the cases into \(n-k\)

  • \(k=n\) (\(n-k=0\)): \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]\,(1-\lambda(t_2)\delta+o(\delta))\)
  • \(k=n-1\) (\(n-k=1\)): \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n-1]\,(\lambda(t_2)\delta+o(\delta))\)
  • \(k\le n-2\) (\(n-k\ge2\)): \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=k]\cdot o(\delta)=o(\delta)\) — \(\delta\) Even when multiplied by a probability unrelated to \(o(\delta)\), and since it is the sum of a finite number (\(n-1\)), the total is also \(o(\delta)\)

\[ \Pr[N_{t_1,t_2+\delta}=n]=\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]\big(1-\lambda(t_2)\delta\big)+\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n-1]\,\lambda(t_2)\delta+o(\delta). \tag{4} \]

If we subtract \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]\) from both sides, divide by \(\delta\), and take \(\delta\to0\), the left side becomes the definition of the derivative and is \(o(\delta)/\delta\to0\).

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial t_2}\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]=-\lambda(t_2)\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]+\lambda(t_2)\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n-1],\qquad \Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=-1]\equiv0. \tag{5} \]

Interpretation (Probabilistic Flow). The probability of state \(n\) increases by the incoming flow “\(\lambda\Pr[n-1]\)” when one event occurs in “\(n-1\)”, and decreases by the outgoing flow “\(\lambda\Pr[n]\)” when one event occurs in “\(n\)”. This is the Kolmogorov forward equation for a pure birth process.

  1. is an infinite system of differential equations for \(n=0,1,2,\dots\). Although it can be solved sequentially starting from \(n=0\), it can be solved in one step using a generating function.

5.5 Generator function conversion

Defines the transformation of the count distribution.

\[ P(t_2,z)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]\,z^n. \tag{6} \]

NoteCode convention

This is the Probability Generating Function (PGF) \(E[z^{N}]\) of probability theory. Since the z-transform in signal processing usually uses \(z^{-n}\), there is a difference \(z\leftrightarrow z^{-1}\), but the principle of reading the coefficients is the same. The advantage of this transformation is that it changes an infinite system of equations for \(n\) into a single equation with \(z\) as a parameter. The distribution that was a function of \(n\) becomes a function of \((t_2,z)\).

If we multiply (5) by \(z^n\) and add it for \(n\ge0\),

\[ \frac{\partial P(t_2,z)}{\partial t_2}=-\lambda(t_2)\sum_{n\ge0}\Pr[N=n]z^n+\lambda(t_2)\sum_{n\ge0}\Pr[N=n-1]z^n . \]

If we shift the index to \(m=n-1\) for the second sum (the \(n=0\) term becomes \(\Pr[N=-1]=0\))

\[ \sum_{n\ge0}\Pr[N=n-1]z^n=\sum_{m\ge0}\Pr[N=m]z^{m+1}=z\,P(t_2,z). \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{\partial P(t_2,z)}{\partial t_2}=-\lambda(t_2)\,(1-z)\,P(t_2,z). \tag{7} \]

Boundary condition. If \(t_2=t_1\), the interval length is 0, so there is no event: \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_1}=0]=1\), the rest are 0. Therefore

\[ P(t_1,z)=1. \tag{8} \]

5.6 sun

  1. is a first-order linear ordinary differential equation for \(t_2\) when \(z\) is fixed. Separating variables

\[ \frac{1}{P}\frac{\partial P}{\partial t_2}=-\lambda(t_2)(1-z) \;\Longrightarrow\; \ln P(t_2,z)-\underbrace{\ln P(t_1,z)}_{=\ln1=0}=-(1-z)\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\lambda(\alpha)\,d\alpha . \]

\[ \boxed{P(t_2,z)=\exp\{-(1-z)\,C\},\qquad C\equiv\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\lambda(\alpha)\,d\alpha} \tag{9} \]

\(e^{-(1-z)C}=e^{-C}e^{zC}\) and if we Taylor expand \(e^{zC}\)

\[ P(t_2,z)=e^{-C}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{C^k}{k!}z^k. \tag{10} \]

  1. and (10) \(z^n\) (inverse transformation of the generating function)

\[ \boxed{\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]=e^{-C}\,\frac{C^n}{n!},\qquad C=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\lambda(\alpha)\,d\alpha} \tag{11} \]

In the normal case, if \(\lambda(t)\equiv\lambda_0\), then \(C=\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\) and

\[ \Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]=e^{-\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)}\frac{\big[\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\big]^n}{n!}. \]

In the normal case, the distribution depends only on the length of the interval \(t_2-t_1\) and is independent of location (normal increment). In the abnormal case, since it depends only on the cumulative amount of intensity \(C\), rescaled to the time axis \(\tau(t)=\int_0^t\lambda\) results in a normal process of unit intensity.

5.7 Result Verification

Normalization. \(P(t_2,1)=\sum_n\Pr[N=n]=e^{0}=1\). ✓

Mean and Variance (Derivative of the Generating Function).

\[ E[N]=\partial_zP\big|_{z=1}=C,\qquad E[N(N-1)]=\partial_z^2P\big|_{z=1}=C^2,\qquad \mathrm{Var}[N]=C^2+C-C^2=C . \]

Mean = Variance = \(C\). Since the mean in the normal case is \(\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\), the interpretation that “\(\lambda\) = average number of events per unit time” is justified here. As the standard deviation \(\sqrt C\) decreases to \(1/\sqrt C\) relative to the mean \(C\), the number is relatively concentrated around the mean over long intervals.

Direct assignment. Since \(p_n(C)=e^{-C}C^n/n!\) in \(\frac{dC}{dt_2}=\lambda(t_2)\)

\[ \frac{\partial p_n}{\partial t_2}=\lambda(t_2)\Big(-e^{-C}\frac{C^n}{n!}+e^{-C}\frac{C^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\Big)=-\lambda(t_2)p_n+\lambda(t_2)p_{n-1} \]

satisfies (5). ✓

5.8 Extreme behavior: When the interval and \(n\) grow together

What happens to the individual probability mass when \(C\to\infty\) (the interval is very long or the intensity is high)?

  • \(n\) Fixed: \(e^{-C}C^n/n!\to0\). Exponential decay beats polynomial increase.
  • \(n\) also increases, \(n\approx C\) (near the mode of the distribution): Stirling formula \(n!\approx\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^n\) \[ e^{-C}\frac{C^C}{C!}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi C}}\to0 . \]

That is, for any \(n\), the individual mass approaches 0. Since the possible values spread out widely with a width of \(\sim\sqrt C\), each mass becomes smaller. A properly standardized \((N-C)/\sqrt C\) converges to a standard normal distribution by the central limit theorem.

5.9 Alternative Derivation: Binomial Limit of Coin Toss

Let’s directly derive the normal case using the coin toss interpretation. If we divide length \(T=t_2-t_1\) into \(m=T/\Delta t\) cells and an event occurs in each cell with probability \(p=\lambda_0\Delta t\) (independent, maximum 1 per cell)

\[ \Pr[N=n]=\binom{m}{n}p^n(1-p)^{m-n},\qquad mp=\lambda_0T\ \text{고정}. \]

From \(m\to\infty\)

\[ \binom{m}{n}p^n=\frac{m(m-1)\cdots(m-n+1)}{m^n}\cdot\frac{(mp)^n}{n!}\to\frac{(\lambda_0T)^n}{n!},\qquad (1-p)^{m-n}=\Big(1-\frac{\lambda_0T}{m}\Big)^{m-n}\to e^{-\lambda_0T} \]

Since , we obtain the same result \(e^{-\lambda_0T}(\lambda_0T)^n/n!\). The derivation of the generating function can be seen as performing this binomial limit in the form of differentiation that “increases one by one.”

6 Distribution of arrival times

The occurrence time rule can be derived from count statistics. We handle the normal case (\(\lambda_0\)).

6.1 First arrival time and arrival interval

The fact that the first event occurs after \(t\) is the same as saying that there is no event in \((0,t]\).

\[ \Pr(W_1>t)=\Pr[N_{0,t}=0]=e^{-\lambda_0t} \;\Longrightarrow\; f_{W_1}(t)=\lambda_0e^{-\lambda_0t},\quad t\ge0 . \]

, that is, \(W_1\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_0)\). Due to memorylessness, the interval \(W_{i-1}\) to the next event \(X_i=W_i-W_{i-1}\) is also independent of the past \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_0)\), and the average arrival interval \(1/\lambda_0\). The exponential distribution itself also possesses memorylessness \(\Pr(X>s+t\mid X>s)=\Pr(X>t)\).

6.2 The combined density of the first \(n\) events

For \(0<w_1<\cdots<w_n\), the probability of “one event for each \([w_i,w_i+dw_i)\), and no events in between” is

\[ f_{W_1,\dots,W_n}(w_1,\dots,w_n)\,dw_1\cdots dw_n =\prod_{i=1}^n\big(\lambda_0dw_i\big)\cdot e^{-\lambda_0 w_n} \]

\[ \Longrightarrow\quad f_{W_1,\dots,W_n}(w_1,\dots,w_n)=\lambda_0^n e^{-\lambda_0w_n},\qquad 0<w_1<\cdots<w_n . \]

It is interesting that the density depends only on the last time \(w_n\). This is equivalent to the arrival intervals following an iid exponential distribution (\(\prod_i\lambda_0e^{-\lambda_0x_i}=\lambda_0^ne^{-\lambda_0\sum x_i}\)).

6.3 Conditional distribution when the number is given: Uniform distribution

Under the condition that exactly \(n\) events were observed in the observation interval \([0,T]\), if we multiply the event time density by “\((w_n,T]\) no events” \(e^{-\lambda_0(T-w_n)}\) and divide by \(\Pr[N_{0,T}=n]\)

\[ f(w_1,\dots,w_n\mid N_{0,T}=n)=\frac{\lambda_0^ne^{-\lambda_0T}}{e^{-\lambda_0T}(\lambda_0T)^n/n!}=\boxed{\frac{n!}{T^n}},\qquad 0<w_1<\cdots<w_n\le T . \]

This is equal to the joint density of the order statistic of \(n\) of the uniformly distributed iid samples \([0,T]\). Given the number, the events are scattered within the interval “without any preference.” Since this result does not depend on \(\lambda_0\), the visual information does not provide more information than the number to the \(\lambda_0\) estimation (in normal Poisson, \(N_{0,T}\) is a sufficient statistic for \(\lambda_0\)). Conversely, in abnormal autoexcitation processes, the visual arrangement itself contains information about the intensity.

7 Simulation Verification

Generate a normal Poisson process with an exponent arrival interval and empirically verify the above results.

The abnormal process is generated by thinning: a normal Poisson process of upper limit \(\lambda_{\max}\ge\lambda(t)\) is created, and the event at time \(t\) is maintained with probability \(\lambda(t)/\lambda_{\max}\). Since the probability of the infinitesimal interval of the maintained event becomes \(\lambda_{\max}\delta\cdot\lambda(t)/\lambda_{\max}=\lambda(t)\delta\), it becomes a Poisson process of strength \(\lambda(t)\).

8 Summary

Sampling

  • Sampling is multiplied with the impulse train: \(x_s(t)=x(t)p_{T_s}(t)\).
  • \(\mathcal F\{p_{T_s}\}=\frac{2\pi}{T_s}\sum_k\delta(\omega-k\omega_s)\). \(\omega_s\) is location, \(2\pi/T_s\) is area (\(1/T_s\): series coefficient, \(2\pi\): conversion convention).
  • \(X_s(j\omega)=\frac1{T_s}\sum_kX(j(\omega-k\omega_s))\): Spectrum replicated at \(\omega_s\) intervals, gain \(1/T_s\).
  • No aliasing \(\iff\omega_s>2\omega_m\). Fully restored to an ideal LPF with gain \(T_s\) and cutoff \(\omega_m\le\omega_c\le\omega_s-\omega_m\).

Random Sequence

  • The WSS process \(S_X(f)=0\) for \(|f|>W\) is preserved (in the sense of mean square) when sampled as \(T<1/(2W)\).
  • \(R_{\tilde X}(k)=R_X(kT)\), \(S_{\tilde X}(\nu)=\frac1T\sum_mS_X\big(\frac{\nu+m}{T}\big)\).

Poisson Process

  • \(N(t)\): non-decremental, integer value, random, unit jump(from simplicity \(\Pr[N_{t,t+h}\ge2]=o(h)\)).
  • The infinitesimal interval law \(\Pr[N_{t,t+\Delta t}=1\mid\cdot]=\lambda\Delta t+o(\Delta t)\) defines the point process. Poisson = Strength is independent of history.
  • \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]=e^{-C}C^n/n!\), \(C=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\lambda\). Mean = Variance = \(C\).
  • Normal case: Arrival interval iid \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_0)\), count conditional event time is the order statistic of a uniform distribution.

9 Commonly confused points

  1. Are \(2\pi/T_s\) and \(\omega_s\) duplicates? No. The numerical values are the same, but one is the impulse area and the other is the position. If you subtract the area, the inverse transformation becomes \(T_s/2\pi\) times.
  2. \([t_1,t_2)\) vs \((t_1,t_2]\)? The one that fits \(N_t=\#\{W_i\le t\}\) is \((t_1,t_2]\). There is no difference in distribution during the Poisson process.
  3. Is it impossible for two events to occur at the same time? Possible in a general point process (non-simple). Probability 0 in a Poisson process.
  4. If “+1 for every event”, is it an automatic unit jump? No. The simplicity of “maximum one event at a time” is additionally required.
  5. \(\lambda=5\) If it is 5 per second? No. The average is 5 and the actual number follows Poisson(5).
  6. Is \(\lambda\) a probability? No. It is an occurrence rate (unit 1/s) and can be greater than 1. \(\lambda\Delta t\) is a probability.
  7. What is the difference between ; and ,? They are mathematically the same. ; is a notation that visually distinguishes the current state and history.
  8. Does independence between intervals come from stationarity? No. It comes from memorylessness (hysteresis-independent). It holds true even in abnormal Poisson.
  9. \(o(\delta)-o(\delta)=0\)? No. The two \(o(\delta)\) could be different functions, and the result is again \(o(\delta)\).
