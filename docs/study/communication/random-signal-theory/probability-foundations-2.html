Random Signal Theory — Variable Transformation and Jacobian, Gaussian Random Variable/Vector, Characteristic Function, Central Limit Theorem

Published

1 Overview

This document unfolds around the following single question.

Given the PDF of the random variable (vector) \(\mathbf{X}\), what is the PDF of \(\mathbf{Y} = g(\mathbf{X})\)?

The answer is summarized in a single sentence: “Probability mass is conserved, and volume changes by a factor of \(|\det J|\)”. To accurately understand this sentence, we organize Taylor expansions, Landau notation, Jacobians, the geometric meaning of determinants, and the inverse function theorem in order, and then apply the results to Gaussian random variables and probability vectors. Finally, we conclude with the Central Limit Theorem (CLT), which explains why Gaussians appear so frequently.

2 1D variable transformation

2.1 CDF method

Let \(Y = g(X)\) and assume that \(g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) is strictly monotone and differentiable. It is safer to start with the CDF rather than dealing directly with the PDF. This is because the CDF is probability itself, so we only need to accurately determine the equivalence of events.

In the case of an increasing function. If \(g\) increases, then \(g(X)\le y \iff X\le g^{-1}(y)\), therefore

\[ F_Y(y) = \Pr(g(X)\le y) = \Pr\big(X\le g^{-1}(y)\big) = F_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big). \]

Differentiating both sides with respect to \(y\) (Chain rule)

\[ f_Y(y) = f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\cdot\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y). \]

In the case of a decreasing function. If \(g\) decreases, the direction of the inequality sign is reversed: \(g(X)\le y \iff X\ge g^{-1}(y)\). In a continuous distribution

\[ F_Y(y) = 1 - F_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_Y(y) = -f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\cdot\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y). \]

Since \(\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y) < 0\), the right side is positive.

Integration of the two cases. Using the differentiation formula for the inverse function \(\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y) = 1/g'(g^{-1}(y))\) (justified in the next section), both the increasing and decreasing cases

\[ \boxed{\,f_Y(y) = f_X\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\cdot\frac{1}{\big|g'\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\big|}\,} \]

It can be written as . The absolute value absorbs the negative sign that occurs in a decreasing function, ensuring that the PDF is always non-negative.

2.2 Geometric interpretation: If the slope is large, the density decreases

The smaller interval \([x, x+\Delta x]\) is moved to an interval of length \(\Delta y \approx |g'(x)|\,\Delta x\) via \(g\). The probability masses contained in the two intervals are equal:

\[ f_X(x)\,\Delta x \approx f_Y(y)\,\Delta y = f_Y(y)\,|g'(x)|\,\Delta x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_Y(y) \approx \frac{f_X(x)}{|g'(x)|}. \]

, that is, if \(|g'(x)|\) is large, short sections are stretched out, so the same mass is spread out more widely, resulting in lower density. The PDF is scaled by the reciprocal of the local gradient. This logic of “mass conservation + length (area, volume) scaling” is extended directly to multiple dimensions.

2.3 Differentiation of Inverse Functions and Inverse Function Theorem

2.3.1 The formula itself is the consequence of the chain rule.

Assuming that \(g\) and \(g^{-1}\) are both differentiable, by differentiating both sides of the identity \(g\big(g^{-1}(y)\big) = y\) with respect to \(y\)

\[ g'\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)\cdot\frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y) = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dy}g^{-1}(y) = \frac{1}{g'\big(g^{-1}(y)\big)}. \]

If only the formula is needed, the chain rule is sufficient. However, there are three hidden premises in this calculation.

  1. Does \(g^{-1}\) exist (at least locally)?
  2. Is \(g^{-1}\) differentiable? — If it is not differentiable, differentiating both sides is impossible.
  3. Is the denominator ZZZPH0001448ZZZ \(g'(g^{-1}(y))\neq 0\)?

These three are propositions that require proof, and the Inverse Function Theorem guarantees this.

2.3.2 Accurate statement of theorem

ImportantInverse function theorem

Let \(U\subset\mathbb{R}^n\) be an open set and let \(g:U\to\mathbb{R}^n\) be a function \(C^1\) (where the derivative is continuous). At some point \(\mathbf{x}_0\in U\)

\[ \det Dg(\mathbf{x}_0)\neq 0 \]

If , then there exist open neighborhoods \(U_0\) of \(\mathbf{x}_0\) and open neighborhoods \(V_0\) of \(\mathbf{y}_0 = g(\mathbf{x}_0)\),

  1. \(g: U_0\to V_0\) is a bijector, and
  2. \(g^{-1}: V_0\to U_0\) is \(C^1\),
  3. \(D g^{-1}(\mathbf{y}) = \big[Dg\big(g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\big)\big]^{-1}\), \(\quad\forall\,\mathbf{y}\in V_0\).

The main body of the theorem is numbers 1 and 2, namely existence and smoothness. Formula 3 is a byproduct that follows by the chain rule after numbers 1 and 2 are secured.

2.3.3 Why is it necessary?

The question that Jeong-i answers is as follows.

Can the linear algebraic information (the Jacobian is invertible) at a single point \(\mathbf{x}_0\) guarantee the much stronger property that the nonlinear function \(g\) itself is invertible in its vicinity?

In one dimension, \(g'(x_0)\neq 0\), and if \(g'\) is continuous, then in the vicinity, the sign of \(g'\) is fixed, making \(g\) monotonic, and monotonic functions automatically have inverse functions. Since existence is obtained almost for free, only the formula stands out.

However, in \(n\ge 2\), the concept of “fixed sign” does not exist. \(\det J\neq 0\) merely means that the linear approximation at that moment has not degenerated, and it is not obvious that \(g\) maps to the neighborhood without overlap. Bridging this gap is the Inverse Function Theorem, which serves as the logical foundation for the following.

  • Equivalent to the Implicit Function Theorem: Solving \(g(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{y}\) for \(\mathbf{x}\) is a special form of \(F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}) := g(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}\). One theorem can be derived from the other.
  • Justification of Coordinate Transformation: When integrating by converting to polar coordinates, spherical coordinates, etc., the transformation must be a local diffeomorphism. The sufficient condition for this is the invertibility of the Jacobian.
  • Multidimensional variable transformation formula: \(f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}) = f_{\mathbf{X}}(g^{-1}(\mathbf{y}))/|\det J|\) to be used later is meaningful only if \(g^{-1}\) exists and is differentiable.

2.3.4 The core idea of the proof

Since it cannot be bypassed by monotonism like in 1D, another tool is used.

  1. If we consider ZZZPH0001467ZZZ \(A = Dg(\mathbf{x}_0)\) instead of \(A^{-1}g\), we can set it as \(Dg(\mathbf{x}_0) = I\) without losing generality.
  2. If we let \(h(\mathbf{x}) := \mathbf{x} - g(\mathbf{x})\), then \(Dh(\mathbf{x}_0) = 0\). \(g\in C^1\), then it can be made into \(\|Dh(\mathbf{x})\| \le \tfrac12\) in a sufficiently small vicinity.
  3. The equation \(g(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{y}\) is equivalent to the floating point problem of \(\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}) := \mathbf{y} + h(\mathbf{x})\): \(\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x} \iff g(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{y}\).
  4. By the mean value inequality , \(\|\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})-\phi_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}')\|\le\tfrac12\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\|\), i.e., \(\phi_{\mathbf{y}}\), is a contraction. The Banach Fixed Point Theorem guarantees the existence of a unique fixed point \(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{y}) = g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\).
  5. From the same inequality , the Lipschitz continuity of \(g^{-1}\) is derived, and using this, differentiability and Formula 3 are obtained.

In that the engine of the proof is the fixed-point theorem, it shares the same roots as the logic showing that Newton’s method converges locally.

2.4 In the case of non-injective silk

If \(g\) is not monotonic, multiple inverse \(x_1(y), x_2(y),\dots\) correspond to a single \(y\). Mass conservation can be applied separately near each inverse and then added:

\[ f_Y(y) = \sum_m \frac{f_X\big(x_m(y)\big)}{\big|g'\big(x_m(y)\big)\big|}. \]

Example: \(Y = X^2\). If it is \(y>0\), then the inverse sequences are \(\pm\sqrt{y}\) and \(|g'(\pm\sqrt y)| = 2\sqrt y\), so

\[ f_Y(y) = \frac{f_X(\sqrt y)+f_X(-\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y}\,\mathbb{1}\{y>0\}. \]

3 Required math tools

3.1 Taylor expansion

3.1.1 Derivation of Ideas and Coefficients

Differentiable functions can be approximated by a polynomial in the vicinity of a point. \(x_0\) In the vicinity

\[ f(x) \approx a_0 + a_1(x-x_0) + a_2(x-x_0)^2 + \cdots \]

Let , and determine the coefficients by repeatedly differentiating both sides at \(x_0\).

\[ f(x_0) = a_0,\qquad f'(x_0) = a_1,\qquad f''(x_0) = 2a_2,\qquad f'''(x_0) = 3!\,a_3,\ \dots \]

Differentiating \(n\) times, the \((x-x_0)^n\) term survives \(x=x_0\), and since the coefficient \(n!\) is attached, it becomes \(a_n = f^{(n)}(x_0)/n!\). Therefore

\[ \boxed{\,f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x-x_0)^n\,} \]

(The series converges to \(f\) when \(f\) is an analytic function.) A typical example is

\[ e^x = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n!},\qquad \sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots. \]

3.1.2 1st-order approximation

If you write it as \(x = x_0 + s\)

\[ f(x_0+s) = f(x_0) + s\,f'(x_0) + \frac{s^2}{2}f''(x_0)+\cdots. \]

If \(s = 0.01\), then \(s^2 = 10^{-4}\). Since the contribution of higher-order terms decreases rapidly, if \(s\) is sufficiently small, using only the first-order term results in a good approximation. This is the first-order Taylor approximation (linearization), and it is the approximation used throughout this document.

3.2 Landau notation: \(o(s)\) and \(O(s)\)

\[ f(s) = o(s)\ \ (s\to 0) \iff \lim_{s\to 0}\frac{f(s)}{s} = 0, \qquad f(s) = O(s)\ \ (s\to 0) \iff \frac{|f(s)|}{|s|}\text{ 가 0 근방에서 유계}. \]

Notation Meaning Intuition
\(f(s) = O(s)\) \(\lvert f(s)/s\rvert\) bounded \(s\) at the same speed or lower, decreasing to 0
\(f(s) = o(s)\) \(f(s)/s\to 0\) \(s\) strictly faster than 0

\(o(s)\) is a notation that accurately records only the fact that the specific form of the remaining terms is unknown, but they can be ignored compared to the first term.

NoteDifferentiability itself is \(o(\cdot)\) linearization

If \(f\) is twice differentiable, the remainder is \(\frac{s^2}{2}f''(x_0)+\cdots\), so naturally it is \(o(s)\). However, higher-order derivatives are not needed for the expression \(o(s)\). The definition of differentiation is

\[ f(x_0+s) = f(x_0) + s f'(x_0) + o(s) \iff \lim_{s\to0}\frac{f(x_0+s)-f(x_0)}{s} = f'(x_0) \]

This is because . The (to)differentiability of the multivariable function \(g:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is also defined in the same way:

\[ g(\mathbf{x}_0+\boldsymbol{\Delta}) = g(\mathbf{x}_0) + J(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\boldsymbol{\Delta} + o(\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|). \]

, that is, “differentiable” means “locally approximated by the linear mapping \(J(\mathbf{x}_0)\) and the error is \(o(\|\boldsymbol\Delta\|)\).”

3.3 Jacobian Matrix: Why “Rows = Output, Columns = Input”

Let \(g:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2\), \(\ y_1 = g_1(x_1,x_2)\), and \(y_2 = g_2(x_1,x_2)\). Note that the two inputs \(x_1, x_2\) are passed into both component functions \(g_1, g_2\).

Component-wise first-order Taylor approximation. Since each \(g_i\) is a 2-variable scalar function

\[ g_i(\mathbf{x}_0+\Delta\mathbf{x}) \approx g_i(\mathbf{x}_0) + \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial x_1}\Big|_{\mathbf{x}_0}\Delta x_1 + \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial x_2}\Big|_{\mathbf{x}_0}\Delta x_2, \qquad i = 1,2. \]

If you stack the two expressions vertically

\[ \begin{bmatrix} g_1(\mathbf{x}_0+\Delta\mathbf{x})\\ g_2(\mathbf{x}_0+\Delta\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} g_1(\mathbf{x}_0)\\ g_2(\mathbf{x}_0)\end{bmatrix} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \partial y_1/\partial x_1 & \partial y_1/\partial x_2\\ \partial y_2/\partial x_1 & \partial y_2/\partial x_2 \end{bmatrix}_{\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_0}}_{J(\mathbf{x}_0)} \begin{bmatrix}\Delta x_1\\ \Delta x_2\end{bmatrix}. \]

Index placement is enforced by the definition of matrix multiplication. The matrix-vector product is defined as \((J\Delta\mathbf{x})_i = \sum_j J_{ij}\Delta x_j\). Since the change amount of the \(i\)-th output must be \(\sum_j(\partial g_i/\partial x_j)\Delta x_j\)

\[ J_{ij} = \frac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j} \]

It must be . The row index \(i\) indicates “what kind of output it is,” and the column index \(j\) indicates “in which input direction the derivative was taken.” The \(i\)-th row is the transpose of the gradient of \(g_i\).

Column Perspective: Directional Differential. If we read the same fact as columns, the \(J\)th column of \(j\) is

\[ J(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\mathbf{e}_j = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{g(\mathbf{x}_0+h\mathbf{e}_j)-g(\mathbf{x}_0)}{h} \]

, that is, the direction in which the output vector moves when it moves only slightly in the \(x_j\) direction. This column perspective is used critically in the subsequent area calculation.

If \(p\) dimension, then \(J\) is the \(p\times p\) matrix \(J_{ij} = \partial y_i/\partial x_j\). Also, \(J\) is a function of \(\mathbf{x}\). For example, just as \(y = x^2\) still depends on \(dy/dx = 2x\) for \(x\), the Jacobian also depends on where it is evaluated.

3.4 Cross product, parallelogram, determinant

3.4.1 Definition and Calculation of the Cross Product

The cross product of the 3D vectors \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1,u_2,u_3)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1,v_2,v_3)^\top\) is

\[ \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v} = \det\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{e}_1 & \mathbf{e}_2 & \mathbf{e}_3\\ u_1 & u_2 & u_3\\ v_1 & v_2 & v_3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} u_2v_3 - u_3v_2\\ u_3v_1 - u_1v_3\\ u_1v_2-u_2v_1\end{pmatrix}. \]

(Placing basis vectors in the first row is a formal notation for memory purposes.) The properties are as follows.

  • Direction: \(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\) is perpendicular to both \(\mathbf{u}\)Z and \(\mathbf{v}\). Direct calculation gives \(\mathbf{u}\cdot(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}) = 0\).
  • Size: Lagrange Identity

\[ \|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{u}\|^2\|\mathbf{v}\|^2 - (\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})^2 \]

holds (verified by expanding the components). If we substitute \(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta\)

\[ \|\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{v}\| = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\,\sin\theta. \]

Since \(\|\mathbf{u}\|\) is the base and \(\|\mathbf{v}\|\sin\theta\) is the height, the magnitude of the cross product is the area of the parallelogram formed by the two vectors.

3.4.2 2D: Area \(= |\det[\mathbf{v}_1\ \mathbf{v}_2]|\) (Sign-independent proof)

Let \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (a,b)^\top\), \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (c,d)^\top\). Since the area of a parallelogram is base × height \(= \|\mathbf{v}_1\|\|\mathbf{v}_2\|\sin\theta\),

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Area}^2 &= \|\mathbf{v}_1\|^2\|\mathbf{v}_2\|^2\sin^2\theta = \|\mathbf{v}_1\|^2\|\mathbf{v}_2\|^2 - (\mathbf{v}_1\cdot\mathbf{v}_2)^2\\ &= (a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2) - (ac+bd)^2\\ &= a^2d^2 + b^2c^2 - 2abcd = (ad-bc)^2. \end{aligned} \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{\,\text{Area} = |ad-bc| = \left|\det\begin{bmatrix}a & c\\ b & d\end{bmatrix}\right| = |\det[\mathbf{v}_1\ \mathbf{v}_2]|\,} \]

This derivation makes no assumptions about the signs of the components. (The pictorial proof of subtracting a triangle from a large rectangle is not a general proof because it holds true only in configurations where all components are positive.)

If we look at the same result through the cross product, when we map the 2D vectors \((a,b,0)^\top\) and \((c,d,0)^\top\) to 3D and take the cross product

\[ (a,b,0)^\top\times(c,d,0)^\top = (0,\ 0,\ ad-bc)^\top \]

Since , the magnitude \(|ad-bc|\) is the absolute value of the determinant. In other words, the magnitude of the cross product and the determinant are two ways to calculate the same geometric object (the area of a parallelogram).

3.4.3 Meaning of the sign: orientation

\(\det[\mathbf{v}_2\ \mathbf{v}_1] = -\det[\mathbf{v}_1\ \mathbf{v}_2]\). The area is the same, but the sign is reversed.

  • \(\det>0\): Counterclockwise from \(\mathbf{v}_1\) to \(\mathbf{v}_2\) (direction preserved)
  • \(\det<0\): Clockwise direction (Reverse direction, mirror image)

Since the area cannot be negative, \(|\det|\) is entered into the variable transformation formula.

3.4.4 Why “Determinant = Signed Volume” (Axiomatic View)

If we list the properties that the signed area function \(A(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2)\) must satisfy,

  1. Linear to each factor: If one side is \(c\) times the area, the area is also \(c\) times. The area remains constant because the base and height remain the same even when a multiple of one side is added to one side (shear).
  2. Alternation: \(A(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{v}) = 0\) (flat parallelogram).
  3. Normalization: \(A(\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2) = 1\) (unit square).

The function satisfying these three properties is unique and is the determinant. The same applies in the dimension \(n\), where \(\det[\mathbf{v}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{v}_n]\) is the signed volume of a parallel body formed by \(n\) column vectors. Also, the multiplicative property \(\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B\) is the algebraic expression of the geometric fact that “the volume is \(B\) with a linear mapping \(|\det B|\), followed by \(A\) with \(|\det A|\).”

4 2D Variable Transformation: From Local Linearization to Density Transformation

4.1 Settings

Let \(\mathbf{y} = g(\mathbf{x})\), \(\ \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^2\), and \(g\) be differentiable and locally one-to-one. In \(\mathbf{x}\)-space, take a small rectangle (cell) with \(\mathbf{x}_0 = (x_{01},x_{02})^\top\) as one vertex. The two sides are

\[ \mathbf{e}_1\Delta x_1 = \begin{bmatrix}\Delta x_1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{e}_2\Delta x_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0\\\Delta x_2\end{bmatrix} \]

. The question is this: What do these two edges become in \(g\) after passing through \(\mathbf{y}\)-space?

4.2 Variant Parameterization and Image Curve

The first side (bottom side) is fixed at \(x_2\), and only \(x_1\) is moved. With the scalar parameter \(s\)

\[ \gamma_1(s) = \mathbf{x}_0 + s\,\mathbf{e}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}x_{01}\\x_{02}\end{bmatrix} + s\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix},\qquad 0\le s\le\Delta x_1. \]

If you apply \(g\) to all points on this line segment, the curve in \(\mathbf{y}\)-space

\[ \mathbf{y}(s) = g\big(\gamma_1(s)\big) = g(\mathbf{x}_0 + s\,\mathbf{e}_1) \]

We obtain . \(\mathbf{x}\) was a straight line in space, but since \(g\) is nonlinear, the image is generally a curve.

4.3 Tangent vector: Chain rule

\(\mathbf{y}\) is 2-dimensional, and since \(s\) is a scalar, it is \(d\mathbf{y}/ds = (dy_1/ds,\ dy_2/ds)^\top\). Applying the chain rule component-wise, let \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{x}_0+s\mathbf{e}_1\).

\[ \frac{dy_i}{ds} = \sum_{j=1}^{2}\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial z_j}\,\frac{dz_j}{ds},\qquad i=1,2. \]

Since \(\frac{d\mathbf{z}}{ds} = \mathbf{e}_1\) (\(\mathbf{x}_0\) is a constant for \(s\)), if we group into a matrix

\[ \frac{d\mathbf{y}}{ds} = J(\mathbf{x}_0+s\mathbf{e}_1)\,\mathbf{e}_1, \qquad \mathbf{y}'(0) = J(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\mathbf{e}_1. \]

\(J(\mathbf{x}_0)\mathbf{e}_1\) is the first column of \(J(\mathbf{x}_0)\), and is the directional derivative with respect to the \(x_1\) axis. By the definition of differentiability

\[ \mathbf{y}(s) = g(\mathbf{x}_0 + s\mathbf{e}_1) = g(\mathbf{x}_0) + s\,J(\mathbf{x}_0)\mathbf{e}_1 + o(s). \]

Here, \(o(s)\) is the remainder that vanishes faster than \(s\) (§Landau notation: \(o(s)\) and \(O(s)\)). That is, the upper curve appears like a straight line with a slope of \(J(\mathbf{x}_0)\mathbf{e}_1\) near the starting point.

4.4 Approximation of the side vector

\(\mathbf{y}\)-space The exact displacement of the first side is by the fundamental theorem of calculus

\[ \mathbf{v}_1 = g(\mathbf{x}_0+\Delta x_1\mathbf{e}_1) - g(\mathbf{x}_0) = \int_0^{\Delta x_1} J(\mathbf{x}_0+s\mathbf{e}_1)\,\mathbf{e}_1\,ds. \]

If the variation is very short, becomes \(J(\mathbf{x}_0+s\mathbf{e}_1)\approx J(\mathbf{x}_0)\) over the integration interval, which becomes a constant independent of \(s\).

\[ \mathbf{v}_1 \approx J(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\mathbf{e}_1\,\Delta x_1. \]

\(x_2\) The same applies to the direction side

\[ \mathbf{v}_2 = g(\mathbf{x}_0+\Delta x_2\mathbf{e}_2) - g(\mathbf{x}_0)\approx J(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\mathbf{e}_2\,\Delta x_2. \]

That is, each small side is approximately translated into a straight line vector scaled by \(J(\mathbf{x}_0)\) and \(\Delta x_1\) and \(\Delta x_2\), respectively. Even a curved line is a straight line when viewed on a sufficiently small scale.

4.5 Area of the upper cell

A linear mapping maps a rectangle to a parallelogram. Therefore, the image of the small cell \(R_{\mathbf{y}}\) is approximated by the parallelogram formed by \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2\), and its area is \(|\det[\mathbf{v}_1\ \mathbf{v}_2]|\).

Now calculate the determinant. If we take the scalar \(\Delta x_1,\Delta x_2\) out of the column

\[ [\mathbf{v}_1\ \mathbf{v}_2] = \big[J(\mathbf{x}_0)\mathbf{e}_1\Delta x_1\ \ J(\mathbf{x}_0)\mathbf{e}_2\Delta x_2\big] = J(\mathbf{x}_0)\,\underbrace{[\mathbf{e}_1\ \mathbf{e}_2]}_{I}\, \begin{bmatrix}\Delta x_1 & 0\\ 0 & \Delta x_2\end{bmatrix}. \]

If you apply \(\det(ABC) = \det A\det B\det C\)

\[ \det[\mathbf{v}_1\ \mathbf{v}_2] = \det J(\mathbf{x}_0)\cdot 1\cdot\Delta x_1\Delta x_2, \]

\[ \boxed{\,\text{Area}(R_{\mathbf{y}}) \approx |\det J(\mathbf{x}_0)|\,\Delta x_1\Delta x_2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad dy_1\,dy_2 = |\det J(\mathbf{x}_0)|\,dx_1\,dx_2\,} \]

The absolute value of the Jacobian determinant is the local area scaling ratio by \(g\). The sign of \(\det J\) indicates orientation, and the absolute value is used for the area.

4.6 Density transformation from mass conservation

Just as \(\Pr\{x_0\le X\le x_0+dx\}\approx f_X(x_0)\,dx\) (height × width) in 1 dimension, the probability mass contained in a small cell in 2 dimensions is (density height) × (cell area). Since the same event corresponds to the cell and its surface, the probability mass is the same:

\[ \Pr\{\mathbf{X}\in d\mathbf{x}\}\approx f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}_0)\,dx_1dx_2 = \Pr\{\mathbf{Y}\in d\mathbf{y}\}\approx f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}_0)\,dy_1dy_2, \qquad \mathbf{y}_0 = g(\mathbf{x}_0). \]

If you substitute \(dy_1dy_2 = |\det J(\mathbf{x}_0)|\,dx_1dx_2\) and eliminate \(dx_1dx_2\)

\[ f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}_0)\,|\det J(\mathbf{x}_0)| \approx f_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}_0). \]

If you set the cell size to 0 and substitute \(\mathbf{x} = g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\) to write the right side as an expression using only \(\mathbf{y}\)

\[ \boxed{\,f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}) = \frac{f_{\mathbf{X}}\big(g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\big)}{\big|\det J\big(g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\big)\big|}\,} \]

Interpretation: Convert \(\mathbf{y}\) to \(\mathbf{x} = g^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\) to read the original density, and scale it by the local area factor \(1/|\det J|\). All that has happened is that \(|g'|\) in the 1D formula has been changed to \(|\det J|\). Since the derivative is now a \(2\times 2\) matrix, the determinant acts as the “size” of that matrix.

4.7 Sanity check: linear mapping \(\mathbf{Y} = A\mathbf{X}\)

A Sanity check is a procedure that verifies whether a general formula obtained through complex arguments is consistent by substituting it into the simplest case where the answer can be known independently. Since the derivation above involved several steps, such as Taylor approximation and limits, it is necessary to check for the possibility of errors occurring somewhere.

If \(g(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}\) (\(A\) is reversible \(2\times2\)), then \(J(\mathbf{x})\equiv A\) and \(g^{-1}(\mathbf{y}) = A^{-1}\mathbf{y}\), so the general formula is

\[ f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{y}) = \frac{f_{\mathbf{X}}(A^{-1}\mathbf{y})}{|\det A|}. \]

Meanwhile, this can be verified independently using only linear algebra: \(A\) maps the unit square to a parallelogram whose column vector is the column of \(A\), thereby increasing the area exactly by \(|\det A|\). Since any region can be divided into small squares, the area of all regions becomes \(|\det A|\) (as it is a linear mapping, this is exact, not approximate). For probability mass to be conserved, the density must be \(1/|\det A|\). The answers for the two paths exactly match.

Passing simple cases does not prove a general formula, but it gives confidence that there are no obvious errors. It is a habit worth practicing after a complex derivation.

6 Gaussian random variable

6.1 PDF and parameters

The PDF of \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^2)\) is

\[ p_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),\qquad\sigma^2>0. \]

  • \(m = \mathbb{E}[X]\) (Average): Shifts the center (position) of the curve.
  • \(\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X)\) (Dispersion): Controls spreading. If \(\sigma\) is large, it is a wide and low species; if small, it is a narrow and pointed species.
  • As the distance from the center increases, the density decreases rapidly to \(e^{-(x-m)^2/2\sigma^2}\), so the probability of a large deviation is very small.

6.1.1 Proof of Normalization Constant: Polar Coordinates and Jacobian

If you set it as \(I := \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2/2}dx\)

\[ I^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy. \]

Converts variables to \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\). The Jacobian is

\[ J = \begin{bmatrix}\partial x/\partial r & \partial x/\partial\theta\\ \partial y/\partial r & \partial y/\partial\theta\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det J = r. \]

Therefore, \(dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta\) (as seen in the previous figure, \(r\Delta r\Delta\theta\)) and

\[ I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\Big[-e^{-r^2/2}\Big]_0^\infty = 2\pi, \qquad I = \sqrt{2\pi}. \]

\(u = (x-m)/\sigma\) \(\int p_X(x)dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-u^2/2}du = 1\) is confirmed by substitution (\(dx = \sigma\,du\)).

6.2 Affine transformation and standardization

Affine transformation. If it is \(Y = aX+b\) (\(a\neq 0\)), then in the 1D variable transformation formula, since \(g^{-1}(y) = (y-b)/a\) and \(|g'| = |a|\),

\[ p_Y(y) = \frac{1}{|a|}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\big(\frac{y-b}{a}-m\big)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi a^2\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\big(y-(am+b)\big)^2}{2a^2\sigma^2}\right). \]

, i.e., \(Y\sim\mathcal{N}(am+b,\ a^2\sigma^2)\). The Gaussian is closed under affine transformations.

If you set it to Standardized (Z-converted). \(a = 1/\sigma\), \(b = -m/\sigma\)

\[ Z = \frac{X-m}{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1). \]

If you check directly, \(\mathbb{E}[Z] = (\mathbb{E}[X]-m)/\sigma = 0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Z) = \mathbb{E}[Z^2] = \mathbb{E}[(X-m)^2]/\sigma^2 = 1\).

6.3 CDF and Q-function

PDF and CDF of standard normal \(Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)\)

\[ \varphi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2},\qquad \Phi(z) = \Pr[Z\le z] = \int_{-\infty}^{z}\varphi(t)\,dt \]

It is written as . The CCDF is used particularly frequently in the field of communications, and

\[ Q(z) = \Pr[Z>z] = 1-\Phi(z) = \int_z^\infty\varphi(t)\,dt. \]

Since \(\varphi\) is an even function, symmetry \(\Phi(-z) = 1-\Phi(z) = Q(z)\) holds. The general Gaussian is obtained through standardization

\[ \Pr[X\le x] = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x-m}{\sigma}\right),\qquad \Pr[X>x] = Q\!\left(\frac{x-m}{\sigma}\right). \]

6.4 MGF and characteristic function

6.4.1 Derivation of MGF: Perfect square

\[ M_X(s) = \mathbb{E}[e^{sX}] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\!\left(sx-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)dx. \]

Complete the exponent for \(x\).

\[ \begin{aligned} sx-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2\sigma^2} &= -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[x^2-2(m+\sigma^2s)x+m^2\Big]\\ &= -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[\big(x-(m+\sigma^2 s)\big)^2 - (m+\sigma^2s)^2 + m^2\Big]\\ &= -\frac{\big(x-(m+\sigma^2s)\big)^2}{2\sigma^2} + ms + \frac{\sigma^2s^2}{2}. \end{aligned} \]

The last line utilized \((m+\sigma^2s)^2-m^2 = 2m\sigma^2s+\sigma^4s^2\). If the term irrelevant to \(x\) is removed from the integral, the remaining integral is the total integral \(=1\) of a Gaussian PDF with mean \(m+\sigma^2s\) and variance \(\sigma^2\). Therefore

\[ \boxed{\,M_X(s) = \exp\!\left(ms+\tfrac12\sigma^2s^2\right)\,} \]

6.4.2 characteristic function

\[ \Phi_X(jv) = \mathbb{E}[e^{jvX}] = \exp\!\left(jmv-\tfrac12\sigma^2v^2\right). \]

The derivation of is \(s\to jv\), which is the same as perfect squaring, but in this case, the complex number \(m+j\sigma^2v\) comes in the “mean” position. The integral of a Gaussian shifted by a complex number.

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-c)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)dx = \sqrt{2\pi}\sigma,\qquad c\in\mathbb{C} \]

The fact that still holds is justified by Cauchy’s theorem, which states that since \(e^{-z^2/2\sigma^2}\) is an analytic function and decreases rapidly at \(|\operatorname{Re}z|\to\infty\), the value does not change even if the integration path is moved from the real axis to the horizontal line \(\operatorname{Im}z = \operatorname{Im}c\). (Alternatively, since both \(M_X(s)\) and \(\mathbb{E}[e^{sX}]\) are analytic over the entire complex plane, \(s = jv\) can be substituted as analytic continuity.)

Term Contains parameter Meaning
\(jmv\) Average \(m\) Location
\(-\frac12\sigma^2v^2\) Dispersion \(\sigma^2\) Spread

Since the exponent of the Gaussian PDF is a quadratic expression of \(x\), the remainder after perfect squaring also comes out neatly as a first-order + second-order expression of \(s\) (or \(v\)).

6.4.3 Moment

If we differentiate MGF at 0

\[ M_X'(s) = (m+\sigma^2s)M_X(s),\qquad M_X''(s) = \big[\sigma^2+(m+\sigma^2s)^2\big]M_X(s), \]

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = M_X'(0) = m,\qquad \mathbb{E}[X^2] = M_X''(0) = m^2+\sigma^2,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^2. \]

Also, for any real number \(a\), \(\mathbb{E}[e^{aX}] = M_X(a) = \exp(am+\tfrac12a^2\sigma^2)\).

6.5 Application: \(\mathbb{E}\big[e^{aX+bX^2}\big]\)

When \(X\sim\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^2)\), \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\)

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[e^{aX+bX^2}\big] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\!\left(ax+bx^2-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)dx \]

Calculate . The strategy is the same as MGF: square the exponent to reduce to a standard normal integral.

6.5.1 Terms and Perfect Squares

\[ ax+bx^2-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2\sigma^2} = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big\{(1-2b\sigma^2)x^2-2(m+a\sigma^2)x+m^2\Big\}. \]

Since the coefficients of \(x^2\) are \((1-2b\sigma^2)\), if we factor them out and square them,

\[ = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big\{(1-2b\sigma^2)\big[x-\mu^\star\big]^2 + C\Big\}, \qquad \mu^\star = \frac{m+a\sigma^2}{1-2b\sigma^2}, \qquad C = -\frac{(m+a\sigma^2)^2}{1-2b\sigma^2}+m^2. \]

Since the sum of exponents is the product of exponential functions

\[ \exp\!\left(ax+bx^2-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1-2b\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2}\big[x-\mu^\star\big]^2\right)\cdot \exp\!\left(\boxed{-}\,\frac{C}{2\sigma^2}\right). \]

WarningNote the sign

\(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\) is multiplied to the entire curly brace, so it is also multiplied to the constant term \(C\). The second argument is \(\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{C}{2\sigma^2}\big)\). If written as \(\exp\!\big(+\tfrac{C}{2\sigma^2}\big)\), the sign of the final result is reversed. This is verified again in the special case verification below.

6.5.2 Effective Variance and Variable Substitution

If we take the factor unrelated to \(x\) out of the integral

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[e^{aX+bX^2}\big] = e^{-C/2\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1-2b\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2}\big[x-\mu^\star\big]^2\right)dx. \]

Convergence Condition. For the integral to converge, it must be \(1-2b\sigma^2>0\), i.e., \(b<\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\). If we compare the remaining integrand with the Gaussian kernel \(\exp\!\big(-\frac{(x-\mu^\star)^2}{2\sigma_{\text{eff}}^2}\big)\),

\[ \sigma_{\text{eff}}^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{1-2b\sigma^2} \]

Since , the convergence condition is that the effective variance is positive. If \(b\ge\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\), the exponent does not decrease from \(|x|\to\infty\), so the expected value diverges to \(+\infty\).

Criteria for taking \(\alpha\). The goal is to make the exponent into the standard normal form \(-\alpha^2/2\).

\[ -\frac{1-2b\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2}\big[x-\mu^\star\big]^2 = -\frac{\alpha^2}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = \frac{x-\mu^\star}{\sigma/\sqrt{1-2b\sigma^2}} = \frac{x-\mu^\star}{\sigma_{\text{eff}}}. \]

This has the same structure as the standardized variable of a Gaussian with mean \(\mu^\star\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_{\text{eff}}\). Since \(dx = \sigma_{\text{eff}}\,d\alpha = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{1-2b\sigma^2}}d\alpha\)

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha^2/2}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{1-2b\sigma^2}}\,d\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2b\sigma^2}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-\alpha^2/2}d\alpha}_{=1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2b\sigma^2}}. \]

6.5.3 Result

Substituting \(C\) gives \(-\frac{C}{2\sigma^2} = \frac{(m+a\sigma^2)^2}{2\sigma^2(1-2b\sigma^2)}-\frac{m^2}{2\sigma^2}\), so from \(1-2b\sigma^2>0\)

\[ \boxed{\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{aX+bX^2}\big] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2b\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left\{\frac{(m+a\sigma^2)^2}{2\sigma^2(1-2b\sigma^2)}-\frac{m^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}\,} \]

7 Characteristic function and Fourier transform

7.1 Sign conventions and inverse transform formulas

The standard Fourier pair in signal processing is

\[ \hat f(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\,e^{-j\omega x}\,dx \qquad\longleftrightarrow\qquad f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\hat f(\omega)\,e^{+j\omega x}\,d\omega. \]

The characteristic function has opposite signs for the exponents:

\[ \varphi_X(jv) := \mathbb{E}[e^{jvX}] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty p_X(x)\,e^{+jvx}\,dx = \mathcal{F}\{p_X\}(-v). \]

In other words, the characteristic function is the Fourier transform with the frequency axis flipped. If we substitute \(\omega = -v\) in the standard inverse transform (\(d\omega = -dv\), where the integral limit is flipped and the signs cancel each other out)

\[ p_X(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}\{p_X\}(\omega)\,e^{j\omega x}d\omega = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_X(jv)\,e^{-jvx}\,dv. \]

Exponential Sign Role
Standard Fourier Transform \(e^{-j\omega x}\) \(p_X\to\hat p_X\)
Characteristic Function \(e^{+jvx}\) Frequency Axis Inversion (\(\omega\to -v\))
Inverse characteristic transform \(e^{-jvx}\) Reverse inversion to restore \(p_X\)

Since frequency axis inversion is a one-to-one correspondence, structural properties such as uniqueness and the convolution theorem are preserved. However, when using Fourier pair tables, one must not forget the \(v\to -v\) substitution. Particular care must be taken because, like a Gaussian, if \(p_X\) is symmetric about the origin, the characteristic function becomes a real even function, and the sign difference is not revealed.

7.2 Proof of the Inverse Fourier Transform Theorem

The inverse transform formula is a provable theorem. The most honest path is Gaussian regularization. Let \(f\in L^1\), and attach a damping factor that guarantees convergence

\[ I_\epsilon(x) := \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\hat f(\omega)\,e^{j\omega x}\,e^{-\epsilon\omega^2/2}\,d\omega,\qquad\epsilon>0 \]

Step 1: Swap order. Expand \(\hat f\) into the definition. Since \(\int\!\!\int|f(t)|e^{-\epsilon\omega^2/2}\,dt\,d\omega<\infty\), we can change the order of integration using Fubini’s theorem:

\[ I_\epsilon(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(t)\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{j\omega(x-t)-\epsilon\omega^2/2}\,d\omega\right]dt. \]

Step 2: The inner integral is Gaussian. Completed squares like the MGF/CF calculation above.

\[ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{j\omega u-\frac{\epsilon}{2}\omega^2}\,d\omega = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\epsilon}}\,e^{-u^2/2\epsilon} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\epsilon}}\,e^{-u^2/2\epsilon} =: \phi_\epsilon(u). \]

\(\phi_\epsilon\) is a Gaussian PDF with mean 0 and variance \(\epsilon\).

Step 3: Convolution. Therefore \(I_\epsilon(x) = \int f(t)\,\phi_\epsilon(x-t)\,dt = (f*\phi_\epsilon)(x)\).

Step 4: Limit. If \(\epsilon\to0\), then \(\phi_\epsilon\) is an approximation sequence where the width decreases to 0 and the area remains 1; therefore, at the continuum of \(f\), \((f*\phi_\epsilon)(x)\to f(x)\). Meanwhile, if \(\hat f\in L^1\), then by the governing convergence theorem, \(I_\epsilon(x)\to\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\hat f(\omega)e^{j\omega x}d\omega\). Since the two limits are equal, the inverse transform formula holds. \(\square\)

The key material of this proof is the fact that “the Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian”.

7.3 Dirac Delta

7.3.1 The limitations of the intuition of a “function rising to infinity”

If we define \(\delta(x)\) as “a function where \(x=0\) is \(\infty\) and zero elsewhere,” a contradiction arises. In Lebesgue integration, the value at a single point (a set with measure zero) does not affect the integral; therefore, the integral of such a function is zero and cannot be 1. \(\delta\) does not exist as a function.

7.3.2 Delta as an approximation sequence

A rectangle with width \(\epsilon\) and height \(1/\epsilon\)

\[ \delta_\epsilon(x) = \begin{cases}1/\epsilon, & |x|<\epsilon/2\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \]

is a normal function for all \(\epsilon\) and its area is exactly 1. For \(\epsilon\to 0\), the width goes to 0 and the height goes to \(\infty\), but the area remains 1. The limit itself is not a function, but the limit of the effect applied to the continuous function \(\phi\) is well-defined:

\[ \lim_{\epsilon\to0}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\delta_\epsilon(x)\,\phi(x)\,dx = \phi(0). \]

(\(\int\delta_\epsilon\phi = \phi(\xi_\epsilon)\), \(|\xi_\epsilon|<\epsilon/2\) by the Mean Value Theorem.) The Gaussian \(\phi_\epsilon\) is also an approximation sequence that plays the same role.

7.3.3 Precise definition: Sampling functional

In distribution theory, \(\delta\) is defined as a functional acting on the test function \(\phi\) (a smooth and rapidly decreasing function):

\[ \langle\delta,\phi\rangle := \phi(0),\qquad \int_{-\infty}^\infty\delta(x-t)\,\phi(x)\,dx = \phi(t). \]

From a signal processing perspective, \(\delta(x-t)\) is the operation “sample the signal from \(t\)”.

Level Intuition Accuracy
1 Function rising from \(x=0\) to \(\infty\) Does not exist as a function
2 Width → 0, Height → \(\infty\), Limit of functions with area = 1 Valid as a sequence of approximations
3 \(x=0\) precise definition

When calculating, you can think of a Level 2 diagram, but since a contradiction arises the moment you add the word “function,” the rigorous language is Level 3.

7.3.4 Meaning of integral expression \(\delta(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{j\omega x}d\omega\)

This integral does not converge classically. \(|e^{j\omega x}| = 1\) Subsequently, the integrand does not decrease, and even if a symmetric cut is taken

\[ \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-R}^{R}e^{j\omega x}d\omega = \frac{\sin(Rx)}{\pi x} \]

oscillates from \(x\neq0\) to \(R\to\infty\) and does not converge. Therefore, the equality is an equality as a distribution, that is, for all test functions \(\phi\)

\[ \lim_{\epsilon\to0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{j\omega x}e^{-\epsilon\omega^2/2}d\omega\right]\phi(x)\,dx = \phi(0) \]

8.1 Mean vector and covariance matrix

About \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^n\)

\[ \mathbf{m}_X = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}],\qquad K_X = \mathbb{E}\big[(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{m}_X)(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{m}_X)^\top\big]. \]

\(K_X\) is the matrix \(n\times n\), where the \((i,j)\) component is \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)\). The diagonal elements represent the variance of each component, and the off-diagonal elements represent the covariance between components. Note that this is the expectation of the outer product \(\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^\top\) of the centered vector.

Identity \(K_X = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top]-\mathbf{m}_X\mathbf{m}_X^\top\). If we expand the definition and use the linearity of the expectation value

\[ \begin{aligned} K_X &= \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top-\mathbf{X}\mathbf{m}_X^\top-\mathbf{m}_X\mathbf{X}^\top+\mathbf{m}_X\mathbf{m}_X^\top\big]\\ &= \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top]-\mathbf{m}_X\mathbf{m}_X^\top-\mathbf{m}_X\mathbf{m}_X^\top+\mathbf{m}_X\mathbf{m}_X^\top = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top]-\mathbf{m}_X\mathbf{m}_X^\top. \end{aligned} \]

This is the same structure as Scala’s \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}X)^2\).

8.2 PDF of real Gaussian vectors

The PDF of \(\mathbf{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_X,K_X)\) (\(K_X\) symmetric positive definite value) is

\[ \boxed{\,p_{\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}(\det K_X)^{1/2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac12(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_X)^\top K_X^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m}_X)\right)\,} \]

The exponent part is a scalar \((1\times n)(n\times n)(n\times 1) = 1\times1\) (PDF must be a scalar).

8.6 Complex Gaussian Vector

If \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^n\) is a proper complex Gaussian with mean \(\mathbf{m}_X\) and covariance \(K_X = \mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{m}_X)(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{m}_X)^H]\) (Hermitian positive values),

The square of the magnitude of the complex number \(z = a+jb\) is not \(z^2\), but \(|z|^2 = z^*z = a^2+b^2\). For the diagonal elements of \(K_X\) to have the real variance \(\mathbb{E}|X_i-m_i|^2\), a conjugate is required. The quadratic form \((\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m})^HK^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{m})\) can also be substituted into the exponent because \(K\) is Hermite. Using only \(^\top\) can result in a complex number.

9.1 statement

  • Odd degree: If \(N\) is odd, then \(\mathbb{E}[X_1\cdots X_N] = 0\).

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_1\cdots X_N] = \sum_{\text{모든 짝짓기}}\ \prod_{\{i,j\}\in\text{짝짓기}}\kappa_{ij}. \]

sum is taken for all partitions that divide \(\{1,\dots,N\}\) into unordered pairs \(N/2\).

9.4 Proof 1: Iterative differentiation of MGF

The MGF of the mean 0 Gaussian vector is \(M(\mathbf{t}) = \exp\!\big(\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\kappa_{ij}t_it_j\big)\) and

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_1\cdots X_N] = \frac{\partial^N M}{\partial t_1\cdots\partial t_N}\bigg|_{\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{0}}. \]

Let \(A_i := \sum_j\kappa_{ij}t_j\). Then \(\partial M/\partial t_i = A_iM\), \(\ \partial A_i/\partial t_k = \kappa_{ik}\), and from \(\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{0}\), \(A_i = 0\), \(M = 1\).

\(N = 2\). \(\partial_{t_2}\partial_{t_1}M = \kappa_{12}M+A_1A_2M\), therefore from \(\mathbf{t} = 0\), \(\kappa_{12}\).

\(N = 4\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial_{t_1}M &= A_1M\\ \partial_{t_2}\partial_{t_1}M &= \kappa_{12}M+A_1A_2M\\ \partial_{t_3}\partial_{t_2}\partial_{t_1}M &= \kappa_{12}A_3M+\kappa_{13}A_2M+\kappa_{23}A_1M+A_1A_2A_3M. \end{aligned} \]

If we differentiate once more with respect to \(t_4\) and substitute \(\mathbf{t} = 0\), any term where at least one \(A\) remains becomes 0. Since the only terms that survive are those where each \(A_i\) is “harvested” into \(\kappa_{i4}\),

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_1X_2X_3X_4] = \kappa_{12}\kappa_{34}+\kappa_{13}\kappa_{24}+\kappa_{23}\kappa_{14}. \]

Structure. Two things happen at every differentiation step.

  • One existing \(A_i\) changes to \(\kappa_{ik}\) → Pair \(\{i,k\}\) completed
  • New \(A_k\) added → Waiting for harvest later

9.5 Proof 2: Power Series Expansion (in one go)

If you set it as \(Q(\mathbf{t}) := \frac12\mathbf{t}^\top K\mathbf{t} = \sum_{i<j}\kappa_{ij}t_it_j+\frac12\sum_i\kappa_{ii}t_i^2\)

\[ M(\mathbf{t}) = e^{Q(\mathbf{t})} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{Q(\mathbf{t})^k}{k!}. \]

When \(\partial^N/\partial t_1\cdots\partial t_N\) is evaluated at \(\mathbf{t} = 0\), only the coefficients of the monomial \(t_1t_2\cdots t_N\) remain (since the degree of each variable is 1, the coefficients × 1).

  • Since \(Q\) is a second-order homogeneous formula, \(Q^k\) is \(2k\). To obtain the order \(N\), it must be \(k = N/2\). If \(N\) is odd, there is no such \(k\), so the coefficient is 0.
  • When \(k = N/2\), \(Q^k\) is the product of \(k\) factors. One term is selected from each factor, but the diagonal term \(t_i^2\) is excluded because it violates the degree 1 condition. To select the non-diagonal terms \(\kappa_{ij}t_it_j\) to become \(t_1\cdots t_N\), the selected \(k\) pairs must be coprime and cover the whole. That is, it is a perfect pair.
  • One pair (unordered \(k\) pairs) appears \(k!\) times according to the order assigned to \(k\) arguments, and this is offset by \(1/k!\).

Therefore, the coefficient of \(t_1\cdots t_N\) is \(\sum_{\text{짝짓기}}\prod\kappa_{ij}\). \(\square\)

Essence. Because the MGF (or CF) of a Gaussian is a quadratic exponential function, all higher-order moments are decomposed into the product of second-order moments \(\kappa_{ij}\). This is another expression of the fact that a Gaussian is completely determined by its mean and covariance alone.

10 Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

10.1 statement

Let \(\{X_\ell\}_{\ell\ge1}\) be the iid, and let \(\mathbb{E}[X_\ell] = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_\ell) = \sigma^2\in(0,\infty)\) be the normalized subtotals.

\[ S_L = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}\sum_{\ell=1}^LX_\ell \]

By the Lindeberg–Lévy CLT,

\[ S_L\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\qquad(L\to\infty). \]

Convergence in distribution means that at every point \(F\) where the CDF of the limit distribution \(x\)Z is continuous, \(F_{S_L}(x)\to F(x)\).

The surprising point: There are no assumptions about the distribution of \(X_\ell\). Whether it is Bernoulli, Poisson, or a uniform distribution, as long as it is iid and has a finite variance, the normalized sum converges to a Gaussian. This is why Gaussians are so frequently seen in nature.

10.2 \(1/\sqrt L\) Reason for normalization

The variance of the sum of independent random variables is the sum of their variances. This is because the covariance term becomes zero:

\[ \operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^LX_\ell\Big) = \sum_{\ell=1}^L\operatorname{Var}(X_\ell)+\sum_{\ell\neq\ell'}\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}(X_\ell,X_{\ell'})}_{0} = L\sigma^2. \]

If left as is, the variance diverges in proportion to \(L\), causing the limit distribution to degenerate. Multiplying by \(1/\sqrt L\) fixes the variance at \(\frac{1}{L}\cdot L\sigma^2 = \sigma^2\). For a non-degenerate limit to exist, there must be both centering by subtracting the mean of 0 and scaling by dividing by \(\sqrt L\).

10.3 Standard form (when mean is not 0)

If \(\mathbb{E}[X_\ell] = \mu\), then \(\tilde X_\ell := X_\ell-\mu\) is defined. \(\mathbb{E}[\tilde X_\ell] = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\tilde X_\ell) = \sigma^2\) are present and are still iid, so the above CLT can be applied:

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt L}\sum_{\ell=1}^L(X_\ell-\mu) = \frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^LX_\ell-L\mu}{\sqrt L}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2). \]

Dividing \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\) by \(\sigma\) gives \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), so (Continuity Mapping Theorem)

\[ \boxed{\,\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^LX_\ell-L\mu}{\sigma\sqrt L}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\,} \]

The general form is not a new theorem, but rather the application of mean shifting (\(L\mu\) removal: total mean removal) and scaling (\(\sigma\sqrt L\): variance to 1) to the mean 0 CLT.

10.4 CLT for sample mean

If \(\bar X_L = \frac1L\sum_{\ell=1}^LX_\ell\), divide the numerator and denominator of the general form by \(L\)

\[ \frac{\bar X_L-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt L}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,1). \]

In practical terms, it approximates from a large \(L\) to \(\bar X_L\approx\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2/L)\), serving as the basis for confidence intervals and \(z\)-tests. The error of the sample mean decreases at a \(1/\sqrt L\) rate.

10.5 Proof Sketch: Characteristic Function

A complete proof requires the Levi Continuity Theorem (pointwise convergence of CF ⇒ distributional convergence), which is accepted. The rest can be shown using Taylor expansion and \(o(\cdot)\) notation.

Let \(\varphi(u) := \mathbb{E}[e^{juX_1}]\). If the second moment is finite, \(\varphi\) is twice differentiable at 0, and since \(\varphi(0) = 1\), \(\varphi'(0) = j\mathbb{E}X = 0\), and \(\varphi''(0) = -\mathbb{E}X^2 = -\sigma^2\),

\[ \varphi(u) = 1-\frac{\sigma^2u^2}{2}+o(u^2)\qquad(u\to0). \]

Due to independence, the sum of CFs is the product of CFs

\[ \Phi_{S_L}(v) = \mathbb{E}\Big[e^{jv\sum X_\ell/\sqrt L}\Big] = \Big[\varphi\big(v/\sqrt L\big)\Big]^L = \left[1-\frac{\sigma^2v^2}{2L}+o\!\left(\frac1L\right)\right]^L \ \xrightarrow{L\to\infty}\ e^{-\sigma^2v^2/2}. \]

(The last one is \((1+c/L+o(1/L))^L\to e^c\).) Since the right side is the CF of \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\), by Lévy’s continuity theorem, it becomes \(S_L\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\). The reason the Gaussian appears as a limit is that the second-order Taylor approximation of the CF is universal as \(1-\frac{\sigma^2u^2}{2}\), and the limit of that \(L\) raised to the power of is the Gaussian CF. Details of the third order or higher (shape of the distribution) disappear as \(o(1/L)\).

10.6 Example: Thermal noise

Unless the temperature is 0 K, numerous electrons vibrate randomly near their positions even within a conductor without applied voltage. On average, they do not flow in any direction, but on a very small scale, the voltage between two terminals fluctuates between positive and negative over time. Since this voltage noise is the sum of numerous independent electron motions, it is well modeled as a Gaussian with a mean of 0 by the CLT. This is the physical basis of the AWGN model in communication systems.

10.7 CLT and \(\Delta z\) from the perspective of binomial distribution

\(K\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)\) is the sum of the independent Bernoulli \(B_i\sim\text{Bern}(p)\) \(K = \sum_{i=1}^nB_i\). Since \(\mathbb{E}B_i = p\) and \(\operatorname{Var}B_i = p-p^2 = p(1-p)\)

\[ \mathbb{E}K = np,\qquad\operatorname{Var}K = np(1-p). \]

Standardized variable by CLT

\[ Z = \frac{K-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}} \]

converges from \(n\to\infty\) to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

10.7.1 Fairly comparing discrete PMF and continuous PDF

\(K\) has only integer values, so it represents the PMF (Probability Factor), while \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) has the PDF (Probability/Unit Length). Since the units are different, simply overlaying them as is is meaningless.

Meaning of \(\Delta z\). The interval between neighboring values of \(K\) is 1. When converted to the \(Z\) scale, the interval between the neighboring \(z_k = (k-np)/\sqrt{np(1-p)}\) is

\[ \Delta z = z_{k+1}-z_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}. \]

As \(n\) increases, it becomes \(\Delta z\to0\), and the dots become denser.

Height Adjustment. Set the height so that the width of each stem becomes the probability:

\[ \text{높이}\times\Delta z = \Pr[K=k] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{높이} = \frac{\Pr[K=k]}{\Delta z} = \sqrt{np(1-p)}\,\Pr[K=k]. \]

By doing this, the height of the rod has the same unit as “probability/unit \(z\)-length”, i.e., density, so it can be directly compared with \(\varphi(z)\). This is the same logic as dividing the PDF by \(|g'|\) in the 1D variable transformation: since the “slope” of \(Z = (K-np)/\sqrt{np(1-p)}\) is \(\Delta z\), we divide the mass by \(\Delta z\) to make it density.
