1 Sample Space & Events

1.1 Sample space \(\Omega\)

Sample space \(\Omega\) is the set of all possible outcomes \(\omega_i\) that can occur in an experiment.

  • Coin toss: \(\Omega = \{H, T\}\)
  • Roll Dice: \(\Omega = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\)

1.2 An event is a “set”, not an element.

An event is a collection of sample points \(\omega_i\) and is defined by a set operation that follows the laws of Boolean algebra.

It is essential to distinguish here that the event is a set, not an element. In the case of \(\Omega=\{H,T\}\), the possible events are:

\[\varnothing, \quad \{H\}, \quad \{T\}, \quad \{H,T\}\]

In other words, it is not \(H\) itself, but \(\{H\}\) that is the event. Thanks to this distinction, the question of “probability” naturally holds true.

  • \(\Pr[\varnothing] = 0\) (Probability that nothing happens)
  • \(\Pr[\{H,T\}] = \Pr[\Omega] = 1\) (Probability of heads or tails)

1.3 Set Laws

Regarding event \(A, B\):

1.3.1 Union (Union)

\[A \cup B = \{\omega : \omega \in A \;\text{or}\; \omega \in B\}\]

“\(A\) or \(B\)”, and naturally, \(A \subset A\cup B\) and \(B \subset A\cup B\) are valid.

Example: \(A=\{\text{짝수}\}=\{2,4,6\}\), \(B=\{\text{소수}\}=\{2,3,5\}\), then \(A\cup B = \{2,3,4,5,6\}\).

1.3.2 Intersection

\[A \cap B = \{\omega : \omega \in A \;\text{and}\; \omega \in B\}\]

It is also abbreviated as \(AB\), and refers to a joint event in which two events are simultaneously established.

Example: For \(A,B\) as above, \(A\cap B = \{2\}\).

1.3.3 Complement

\[\bar{A} = \{\omega : \omega \notin A\}\]

The complement of is always defined relative to the sample space \(\Omega\) — it is everything outside \(A\).

Example: If \(A = \{\text{짝수}\}\), then \(\bar{A} = \{1,3,5\}\).

1.3.4 De Morgan’s Law

\[\overline{A \cup B} = \bar{A} \cap \bar{B}\]

The key point is that the operator changes when distributing the complement operation (\(\cup \leftrightarrow \cap\)).

Verification: \(\Omega=\{1,\ldots,6\}\), \(A=\{2,4,6\}\), \(B=\{2,3,5\}\)

  • \(A\cup B = \{2,3,4,5,6\}\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\overline{A\cup B} = \{1\}\)
  • \(\bar{A}=\{1,3,5\}\), \(\bar{B}=\{1,4,6\}\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(\bar{A}\cap\bar{B}=\{1\}\) ✓

1.4 Empty set and mutually exclusive events

  • Empty set (null set) \(\varnothing = \bar{\Omega}\): An event that cannot occur. For example, the event of rolling a die once and getting a 7.
  • Mutually Exclusive: \(A\cap B=\varnothing\). Two events with absolutely no overlapping area. Ex: \(A=\{\text{짝수}\}\), \(B=\{\text{홀수}\}\) in a single dice roll.

1.5 Venn Diagram

The sample space \(\Omega\) is drawn as a rectangle, and the event \(A,B\) is drawn as a circle inside it. It is useful for visually reasoning about union, intersection, and complement operations, and identities such as De Morgan’s laws can also be easily understood just by looking at the diagram.

2 Axioms of Probability

To place a measure called “probability” on top of a set structure called an event, several axioms are required.

2.1 Axiom

  1. Non-negativity: \(\Pr[A_i] \ge 0\) — Probability cannot be negative.
  2. Normalization: \(\Pr[\Omega] = 1\) — The entire sample space is filled with probability mass 1.

2.2 Union Law

2.2.1 Case 1 — Mutually Exclusive Event (\(A\cap B = \varnothing\))

\[\Pr[A\cup B] = \Pr[A] + \Pr[B]\]

Since there is no overlapping area, simply adding them is sufficient.

2.2.2 Case 2 — Overlapping Events (\(A\cap B \neq \varnothing\))

\[\Pr[A\cup B] = \Pr[A] + \Pr[B] - \Pr[A\cap B]\]

If you simply add \(\Pr[A]\) and \(\Pr[B]\), you will count the intersection area twice, so you must subtract it once.

3 Conditional Probability

3.1 Definition

Regarding \(A, B\) of \(\Pr[B]\neq 0\),

\[\Pr[A \mid B] = \frac{\Pr[A\cap B]}{\Pr[B]}\]

3.2 Why is the denominator \(\Pr[B]\) — Reduction of the sample space

The essence of conditional probability is switching the sample space from \(\Omega\) to \(B\).

As an intuitive example, asking “the probability that a person is taller than 160cm” in a group yields completely different values than asking “the probability that a person is taller than 160cm under the condition that they are a male student.”

  • The fact that the condition ZZZPH0000895ZZZ is given \(B\) (male student) means that \(B\) is a new universe. Therefore, the denominator becomes \(\Pr[B]\), not the probability of \(\Omega\) (=1).
  • Since the molecule must be a case where it belongs to \(B\) and is also \(A\), it becomes \(\Pr[A\cap B]\).

4 Statistical Independence

4.1 Definition and Equivalence Condition

If the fact that \(B\) occurred does not change the probability of \(A\) at all, then the two events are statistically independent:

\[\Pr[A\mid B] = \Pr[A]\]

This means “even if we know \(B\), there is no additional information about \(A\).” If we apply the definition of conditional probability…

\[\frac{\Pr[A\cap B]}{\Pr[B]} = \Pr[A]\]

Multiplying both sides by \(\Pr[B]\) yields an equivalence condition in the form of a product rule:

\[\boxed{\Pr[A\cap B] = \Pr[A]\cdot \Pr[B]}\]

Both expressions are used to define independence, and you can use whichever is more convenient depending on the situation.

5 Bayes’ Rule

If you write the definition of conditional probability in two directions:

\[\Pr[A\mid B] = \frac{\Pr[A\cap B]}{\Pr[B]}, \qquad \Pr[B\mid A] = \frac{\Pr[A\cap B]}{\Pr[A]}\]

If we set up the equation by eliminating from both sides \(\Pr[A\cap B]\),

\[\Pr[A\mid B]\cdot\Pr[B] = \Pr[B\mid A]\cdot\Pr[A]\]

Simplifying yields Bayes’ Theorem:

\[\boxed{\Pr[B\mid A] = \frac{\Pr[A\mid B]\cdot\Pr[B]}{\Pr[A]}}\]

Meaning: Allows you to “invert” the direction of conditional probability. That is, it is a tool to update the belief in \(B\) after observing \(A\).

6 Random Variables

6.1 Definition — Mapping from sample space to real numbers

Up to this point, everything was a story within the experimental space (sample space, event). Now, we introduce a mapping that assigns a real number (or complex number) to each sample point:

\[X : \Omega \mapsto \mathbb{R}\]

That is, each result \(\omega\in\Omega\) is mapped to a single number \(X(\omega)\).

  • Example 1 (Dice): \(\Omega=\{1,\ldots,6\}\), \(X(\omega)=\omega\). Since the sample space is already a set of real numbers, it can be written as is.
  • Example 2 (Coin): Since \(\Omega=\{H,T\}\) is not a real number, it must be mapped as \(X(H)=1\) and \(X(T)=0\) to become a random variable.

6.2 Types of random variables

  • Discrete-valued RV: When \(\Omega\) is discrete. Example: dice roll, \(\Omega=\{1,\ldots,6\}\).
  • Continuous-valued RV: When \(\Omega\) is continuous. Ex: Bus waiting time, \(\Omega=[0,\infty)\).

Here, the distinguishing criterion is not the number of elements (cardinality) itself, but whether it is countable. Since the real numbers within the interval \([0,1]\) are uncountable, it becomes a continuous random variable.

The reason this distinction is important is that the tools used are different — PMF for discrete, PDF for continuous.

7 Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF)

7.1 Definition

\[P_X(x) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \Pr[X \le x]\]

Uppercase \(X\) is the name of the random variable, and lowercase \(x\) is the specific value (instance) that the variable takes. \(P_X(x)\) means the “probability that the random variable does not exceed \(x\)”.

7.2 Proof that the CDF is a non-decreasing function

Objective: Show that if \(x_1\le x_2\), then \(P_X(x_2)\ge P_X(x_1)\).

Define two events for \(x_1 \le x_2\):

\[A = \{\omega : X(\omega)\le x_1\}, \qquad B = \{\omega : x_1 < X(\omega) \le x_2\}\]

If you draw it on a vertical line, the two sections do not overlap, so

\[A\cap B = \varnothing, \qquad A\cup B = \{\omega : X(\omega)\le x_2\}\]

If we apply additivity to mutually exclusive events

\[\Pr[A\cup B] = \Pr[A]+\Pr[B]\]

If we substitute each term with the definition

\[P_X(x_2) = P_X(x_1) + \Pr[x_1 < X \le x_2]\]

Conclusion: Since \(\Pr[x_1<X\le x_2]\ge 0\), \(P_X(x_2)\ge P_X(x_1)\). In other words, the CDF is non-decreasing. Intuitively, as \(x\) increases, the included range widens, so the probability is bound to increase.

Also, rearranging the above equation yields a formula for calculating interval probability as the difference of CDFs:

\[\boxed{\Pr[x_1 < X \le x_2] = P_X(x_2)-P_X(x_1)}\]

8 Probability Mass Function and Probability Density Function (PMF vs PDF)

8.1 Discrete case — mass

If \(\Omega=\{1,\ldots,6\}\) and each result has equal probability,

\[P_X(4) = \Pr[X\le 4] = \Pr[X\in\{1,2,3,4\}] = \frac{4}{6}\]

In discrete, we can answer questions such as “What is the probability that”\(X\) is exactly 1?” and “What is the probability that it is exactly 2?”, and this value is called the probability mass function (PMF).

8.2 Case of Continuity — Why Mass Cannot Be Defined

Consider \(X\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\). If asked “the probability that \(X\) is exactly 0.5”?

Applying the logic of discrete numbers directly, this is an expression where the total number of cases is placed in the denominator and 1 in the numerator. However, since there are infinitely many elements inside \([0,1]\), the result is always 0 no matter which specific value is chosen:

\[\Pr[X = 0.5] = \Pr[X=0.1] = \cdots = 0\]

In other words, in continuity, defining the mass of a single point is meaningless. Instead, the probability of an interval can be defined in any way. For example, \(\Pr[0 < X < 0.1]\) occupies 10% of the entire range, so it is 0.1.

8.3 Probability Density Function (PDF)

8.3.1 Definition

\[P_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} p_X(\alpha)\, d\alpha, \qquad p_X(x) = \frac{d}{dx}P_X(x)\]

Differentiating the CDF yields the PDF, and integrating the PDF yields the CDF. \(p_X(x)\) describes how the probability mass is distributed on the real axis.

8.3.2 Probability as Area

\[\Pr[a < X \le b] = \int_a^b p_X(x)\, dx\]

That is, probability is the area under the curve. The probability of a narrow interval \([a, a+\Delta]\) is approximately \(p_X(a)\cdot\Delta\), and the probability of a point converges to 0 when \(\Delta\to 0\). The sum of all these infinitesimal areas is the probability of the interval \([a,b]\).

8.3.3 Density can be greater than 1

The probability mass of a discrete number naturally cannot exceed 1. However, there is no problem at all if the maximum value of the density exceeds 1. This is because what is interpreted as probability is not the density value itself, but the area beneath it.

9 Gaussian Distribution

9.1 PDF

\[p_X(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\]

  • \(\mu\): Mean (central location), \(\sigma^2\): Variance (spread)
  • Standard Normal Distribution is \(\mu=0\) and \(\sigma^2=1\)
  • The CDF is the integral of this PDF, and it becomes a smooth S-curve.

9.2 Interpretation of the shape

  • Symmetry: Since \((x-\mu)^2\) is symmetric with respect to \(\mu\), the contribution of two points equidistant from \(\mu\) is exactly the same.
  • Maximum value location: The exponent part \(-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2\) becomes exactly 0 at \(x=\mu\)Z and reaches a maximum at \(e^0=1\). Otherwise, it is \((x-\mu)^2>0\), and since there is a negative sign in front, the exponential function value becomes less than 1, and converges to 0 as it moves away from \(\mu\).

10 Function of a Random Variable

10.1 Goal

Given that \(Y=f(X)\) and \(f\) is invertible, calculate \(P_Y(y)\) and \(p_Y(y)\). Separately treat the cases where \(f\) is net increasing and net decreasing, and then integrate them into a single formula.

10.2 Case I — \(f\) is strictly increasing

10.2.1 CDF induction

Since \(f\) is an increasing function, it is \(f(X)\le y \iff X \le f^{-1}(y)\). Therefore

\[P_Y(y) = \Pr[f(X)\le y] = \Pr[X\le f^{-1}(y)] = P_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\]

10.2.2 PDF Induction

It starts from \(p_Y = \dfrac{d}{dy}P_Y\). If we set it to \(z := f^{-1}(y)\), it becomes \(P_Y(y)=P_X(z)\), and \(z\) is again a function of \(y\). Therefore, applying the chain rule,

\[p_Y(y) = \frac{dP_X(z)}{dz}\cdot\frac{dz}{dy} = p_X(z)\,\frac{dz}{dy} = \frac{df^{-1}(y)}{dy}\, p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\]

Since the first argument is the derivative of the CDF, it is the PDF of \(X\), and \(f^{-1}(y)\) remains as that argument.

10.2.3 Differentiation of the inverse function — Derived from implicit differentiation

What remains is \(\dfrac{df^{-1}(y)}{dy}\). If we set \(x := f^{-1}(y)\), \(f(x)=y\) holds identically. Differentiating both sides with respect to \(y\), the chain rule applies to the left side, and the right side is \(dy/dy=1\):

\[\frac{d}{dy}f(x) = f'(x)\,\frac{dx}{dy} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{1}{f'(x)}\]

Since \(dx/dy\) on the left side is exactly the \(df^{-1}(y)/dy\) we were trying to find,

\[\frac{df^{-1}(y)}{dy} = \left.\frac{1}{f'(x)}\right|_{x=f^{-1}(y)} > 0\]

\(f'(x)\) is the slope at point \(x\), and since \(f\) is strictly increasing, it is positive. Therefore, in this case, the absolute value is not needed.

Strictly speaking: Just because \(f'>0\) is purely increasing does not mean it is guaranteed at every point (\(f(x)=x^3\) is purely increasing, but \(f'(0)=0\)). At this point, \(1/f'\) diverges, and \(p_Y\) becomes infinite at that one point, but since the measure at that point is 0, it has no effect on any probability calculation. It has the same structure as the discussion of the divergence of \(y=0\) below.

\[\boxed{p_Y(y) = \frac{1}{f'(f^{-1}(y))}\, p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)} \quad (\text{increasing } f)\]

10.3 Case II — \(f\) is strictly decreasing

10.3.1 CDF induction

In decreasing functions, the inequality sign is reversed: \(f(X)\le y \iff X \ge f^{-1}(y)\). Therefore

\[P_Y(y) = \Pr[X\ge f^{-1}(y)] = 1 - P_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\]

10.3.2 CCDF — width of the right tail

Probabilities of the form \(\Pr[X>x]\) are called the Complementary Cumulative Distribution Function (CCDF). Since the total area under the PDF is 1, the area to the right of point \(x\) is equal to the total area of 1 minus the area to the left (=CDF):

\[\bar P_X(x) := \Pr[X>x] = \int_x^\infty p_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha = 1-\int_{-\infty}^x p_X(\alpha)\,d\alpha = 1-P_X(x)\]

In the above equation, \(\Pr[X\ge f^{-1}(y)]\) was changed to \(1-P_X(f^{-1}(y))\), which is allowed because it is a continuous random variable. If it were continuous, it would be \(\Pr[X=x]=0\), so it would be \(\Pr[X\ge x]=\Pr[X>x]\). If it were a discrete random variable, it would be \(\Pr[X\ge x]=1-P_X(x)+\Pr[X=x]\), so one must distinguish whether to include the equality sign.

10.3.3 PDF Induction

\[p_Y(y) = \frac{d}{dy}\left[1-P_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\right] = -\frac{df^{-1}(y)}{dy}\,p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\]

This time it is \(\dfrac{df^{-1}(y)}{dy} = \left.\dfrac{1}{f'(x)}\right|_{x=f^{-1}(y)} < 0\), so it combines with the preceding negative sign to become the absolute value form:

\[\boxed{p_Y(y) = \frac{1}{|f'(f^{-1}(y))|}\, p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)} \quad (\text{decreasing } f)\]

10.4 Unified Result

If you combine the two cases into a single absolute value:

\[\boxed{p_Y(y) = p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\left|\frac{d}{dy}f^{-1}(y)\right|}\]

Here

\[\left|\frac{d}{dy}f^{-1}(y)\right| = \left.\frac{1}{|f'(x)|}\right|_{x=f^{-1}(y)}\]

Interpretation: Revert point \(y\) to \(x=f^{-1}(y)\), then scale by the local stretch/compression factor of that point.

10.5 Points to note — steep / gentle and the direction of density

The description that “if”\(f\) is steep, many \(X\) values are squeezed into a narrow \(Y\) range” is a reversed explanation and is internally contradictory. This is because if many values are squeezed into a narrow range, the density must increase.

Let’s verify the exact relationship with a linear example.

Case 1 — steep \(f\): \(f(x)=kx\), \(k\gg1\). If \(X\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\), then \(Y=kX\sim\text{Uniform}(0,k)\).

  • \(X\) Range width: \(1\) → \(Y\) Range width: \(k\) (Widely spread / stretch)
  • Since the same mass 1 is spread over a wide area, the density decreases to \(1/k\) — Consistent with formula \(p_Y = p_X/f' = 1/k\)

Case 2 — gentle \(f\): \(f(x)=x/k\), \(k\gg1\). \(Y=X/k\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1/k)\).

  • \(X\) Range width: \(1\) → \(Y\) Range width: \(1/k\) (Narrow compression / squeeze)
  • Since mass is concentrated in a narrow region, the density becomes \(k\), increasing (amplified)
\(X\) interval width → \(Y\) interval width density
\(f\) is steep (large \(f'\)) narrow → spreads wide (stretch) becomes lower
\(f\) is gentle (small \(f'\)) wide → narrowly compressed (squeeze) increased

In other words, “squeeze into a small range” is the description that should be applied in gentle cases, while “stretched into a large range” is correct for steep cases.

10.6 Application Procedure — Checklist for Using Formulas

The problem of finding the PDF of \(Y=f(X)\) can be processed in the following order without omissions.

  1. Historical: Save \(x=f^{-1}(y)\)
  2. Historical derivative: Calculate \(\dfrac{d}{dy}f^{-1}(y)\) and take the absolute value.
  3. Support Set: Move the support set of \(X\) to \(f\) to define the range where \(Y\) can take a value. Outside of this range, it is \(p_Y=0\).
  4. Substitution: Substitute into the integrated formula \(p_Y(y)=p_X(f^{-1}(y))\,|df^{-1}/dy|\).
  5. Summary: Remove trivial factors (such as the unit step function) from the support set, and denote the support set separately.
  6. Verification: Checks if it is \(\int p_Y(y)\,dy=1\). This single line catches most errors, such as missing Jacobian and support set errors.

The unified formula is merely the result; the essence is the procedure of “differentiating starting from the CDF”. The reason you must be able to reproduce this derivation yourself rather than memorizing the formula is that the moment the formula’s assumption (reversibility) is broken, the formula becomes unusable, but the CDF method continues to function as before.

10.6.1 Case where reversibility is broken — \(X\) spans the entire real number range \(Y=X^2\)

If the support set of \(X\) is the entire \(\mathbb{R}\), then \(f(x)=x^2\) is not invertible (\(x\)Z and \(-x\)Z go to the same \(y\)). A formula cannot be written, but one can start from the CDF. For \(y>0\)

\[P_Y(y) = \Pr[X^2\le y] = \Pr[-\sqrt y\le X\le\sqrt y] = P_X(\sqrt y)-P_X(-\sqrt y)\]

(Since it is continuous, whether the endpoint is included is irrelevant.) Differentiating with respect to \(y\), by the chain rule

\[p_Y(y) = p_X(\sqrt y)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y} - p_X(-\sqrt y)\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}\right) = \frac{p_X(\sqrt y)+p_X(-\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y}, \quad y>0\]

That is, adds the contribution of all preimages \(x_k\) leading to \(y\). The general form is

\[p_Y(y) = \sum_{k:\,f(x_k)=y}\frac{p_X(x_k)}{|f'(x_k)|}\]

and the reversible case is a special case where there is only one original phase.

  • If \(X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), then \(p_X(\sqrt y)=p_X(-\sqrt y)\), so \(p_Y(y)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}}e^{-y/2}\) (\(y>0\)) — is a chi-square distribution with 1 degree of freedom.
  • As in the example below, if \(X\) follows an exponential distribution, then \(p_X(-\sqrt y)=0\), so only one original image remains, which matches the inversion formula.

10.7 Example — Square of exponential distribution \(Y=X^2\)

10.7.1 Settings

\[p_X(x) = e^{-x}u(x), \qquad u(x)=\begin{cases}1 & x\ge0\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\]

\(Y=f(X)=X^2\), and \(x\ge0\)Z to \(f\) is a net increase.

10.7.2 Historical and its differentiation

\[y=x^2 \;\Rightarrow\; x = f^{-1}(y)=\sqrt{y}\;\;(y\ge0), \qquad \frac{d}{dy}f^{-1}(y) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}\]

10.7.3 support set(support)

\(x\ge0 \Rightarrow y=x^2\ge 0\), therefore \(p_Y(y)=0\) for \(y<0\).

10.7.4 Substitute into the conversion formula

\[p_Y(y) = p_X(\sqrt{y})\left|\frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}\right| = e^{-\sqrt{y}}\,u(\sqrt{y})\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}\]

\(u(\sqrt y)=1\) for \(y\ge0\) since

\[\boxed{p_Y(y) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}e^{-\sqrt{y}},\;\; y>0; \qquad p_Y(y)=0,\;\; y\le 0}\]

10.7.5 Normalization verification

If we replace \(y=x^2\), \(dy=2x\,dx\)

\[\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{2\sqrt y}e^{-\sqrt y}\,dy = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{2x}e^{-x}(2x)\,dx = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}dx = 1 \;\checkmark\]

10.8 Points to note — Divergence in \(y=0\)

If we calculate the limit of \(y\to 0^+\) from the above result

\[\lim_{y\to0^+}\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}e^{-\sqrt y} = \lim_{y\to0^+}\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}\cdot\underbrace{e^{-\sqrt y}}_{\to 1} = +\infty\]

, that is, the density diverges at \(y=0\). The reason this is not a problem, however, is that this is an integrable singularity:

\[\int_0^\epsilon \frac{1}{2\sqrt y}e^{-\sqrt y}dy \;\stackrel{u=\sqrt y}{=}\; \int_0^{\sqrt\epsilon}e^{-u}du = 1-e^{-\sqrt\epsilon} < \infty\]

The requirement for the density function is not that “point-by-point values must be finite,” but that “interval integrals (=probabilities) must be finite.” Furthermore, since \(\Pr[Y=0]=\int_{\{0\}}p_Y\,dy = 0\), setting \(p_Y(0)\) to 0 or \(\infty\) has no effect on any probability calculations. Therefore, the notation “from \(y\le0\) to \(p_Y=0\)” should be understood not as a limit value, but as a convention to neatly divide the domain. (This is in the same context as how the value of the unit step function \(u(0)\) varies depending on the convention.)

time point value
\(y\to0^+\) (Limit) \(+\infty\) (Diverges, but is integrable)
\(y=0\) (Conventional definition) \(0\) (Independent of probability as it is a point with a measure of 0)
\(y<0\) \(0\) (Outside support set)

10.9 Point to Note — Why Mass Is Misassigned Without the Jacobian

The core principle of the transformation is conservation of probability mass:

\[p_X(x)\,dx = p_Y(y)\,dy \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_Y(y) = p_X(x)\frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{p_X(x)}{f'(x)}\]

Jacobian \(1/f'(x)\) is a factor that corrects for the interval width difference between \(dx\) and \(dy\). Let’s directly integrate to see what happens if we ignore this and just leave it as \(\tilde p_Y(y):=p_X(\sqrt y)=e^{-\sqrt y}\):

\[\int_0^\infty e^{-\sqrt y}\,dy \;\stackrel{y=x^2}{=}\; \int_0^\infty e^{-x}\cdot 2x\,dx = 2\Gamma(2) = 2\]

The total integral is not 1 but 2. In other words, the total probability mass is doubled, and this is the exact meaning of the phrase “mass is misallocated.”

Why it is in the direction of inflation: \(f(x)=x^2\) is a function where \(x\) becomes larger as \(f'(x)=2x\) becomes larger; in other words, it is a function that is steeper near the large \(x\). In the steep region, the narrow \(dx\) spreads out into the wide \(dy\), so the density should normally decrease. However, if we do not divide by the Jacobian, it amounts to using the original density value of the \(x\)-space as is. Consequently, probability is overassigned to the region where \(y\) is large, and as it accumulates, the total integral becomes 2.

Correct calculation When omitting Jacobian
Large region (steep) of \(y\) Divide into \(1/(2x)\) to lower density Do not lower → Over-allocate probability
Total integral \(1\) \(2\) (mass excess)

10.10 Extension — Transformation from probability vector to probability vector

Up until now, we have transformed the scalar \(X\) into the scalar \(Y\). Extending the same logic to the transformation from the probability vector \(\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^p\) to \(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^p\), the 1-dimensional interval width ratio \(|dx/dy|\) changes into the volume ratio, that is, the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant:

\[p_{\mathbf Y}(\mathbf y) = p_{\mathbf X}\big(\mathbf g^{-1}(\mathbf y)\big)\,\Big|\det \mathbf J_{\mathbf g^{-1}}(\mathbf y)\Big| = \left.\frac{p_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf x)}{|\det\mathbf J_{\mathbf g}(\mathbf x)|}\right|_{\mathbf x=\mathbf g^{-1}(\mathbf y)}\]

The basis is still the conservation of probability mass \(p_{\mathbf X}(\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf x = p_{\mathbf Y}(\mathbf y)\,d\mathbf y\). \(\mathbf g\) locally behaves like the linear mapping \(\mathbf J_{\mathbf g}(\mathbf x)\), and the linear mapping increases the volume by a factor of \(|\det|\). If \(p=1\), then \(\det\) is simply \(f'(x)\), so we return to the previous integration formula.

11 Expected Value

11.1 Definition

The expected value of the function \(f(\cdot)\) of the random variable \(X\) is

\[\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, p_X(x)\, dx\]

is the weighted average of \(f(X)\), using the probability law of \(X\) as the weight.

11.1.1 Meaning of the integrand

If we look at the case where it is \(f(X)=X\)

\[\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\, p_X(x)\, dx\]

where \(p_X(x)\) is the probability weight, and \(x\cdot p_X(x)\) is the amount that the value \(x\) contributes to the mean.

  • If the value is large and the probability is also high → it means that the value appears frequently, so a large weight is multiplied, contributing significantly to the average.
  • Even if the value is very large, if the probability is very small → it does not occur frequently, so the contribution to the mean is small

11.2 Average, Power, Variance

  • \(f(X)=X\): Mean, \(m_X = \mathbb{E}[X]\)
  • \(f(X)=X^2\): mean squared value, sometimes called “power”
  • \(f(X)=(X-m_X)^2\): Variance

\[\sigma_X^2 = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-m_X)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2\]

The standard deviation is \(\sigma_X=\sqrt{\sigma_X^2}\).

Why square: It is because we want to measure the magnitude of the deviation from the mean. If not squared, positive and negative deviations cancel each other out, and information is lost. In fact, if you calculate the average without squaring, \(\mathbb{E}[X-m_X]=0\) always holds true and provides no information.

11.2.1 Power and dispersion are generally different

If we expand the definition of variance

\[\sigma_X^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2-2m_XX+m_X^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2]-2m_X\mathbb{E}[X]+m_X^2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2]-m_X^2\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[X^2]}_{\text{power}} = \underbrace{\sigma_X^2}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{m_X^2}_{\text{mean}^2}}\]

  • Power \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]\) is the mean square distance from the origin, and variance \(\sigma_X^2\) is the mean square distance from the mean.
  • The two quantities become equal only when \(m_X=0\), and this is a special case.
  • From a signal perspective, this is equivalent to the decomposition of total power = AC power (variance) + DC power (square of the mean). The mixing of “power” and “variance” in zero-mean noise or zero-mean modulated signals is permitted because \(m_X=0\) holds true.

11.3 Example — Mean and Variance of Gaussians

For \(X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), we show directly from the definition that \(\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu\) and \(\text{Var}(X)=\sigma^2\). For convenience,

\[g(t) := \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}\]

If we set , then \(p_X(x)=g(x-\mu)\), \(g\) is an even function, and \(\int g\,dt=1\).

11.3.1 Mean — Symmetry and Odd Functions

If you replace it with \(t=x-\mu\)

\[\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,g(x-\mu)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(t+\mu)\,g(t)\,dt = \underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t\,g(t)\,dt}_{=\,0} + \mu\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(t)\,dt}_{=\,1} = \mu\]

\(t\,g(t)\) is an odd function \(\times\) (even function) \(=\), so the integral over the symmetric interval is 0. This means that since the distribution is symmetric about the center of \(\mu\), the contributions of values located at equal distances to the right and left of \(\mu\) exactly cancel each other out.

Strictly speaking: The statement “the integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval is 0” can be used only when the integral converges absolutely. The Gaussian distribution has no problem because its tails decay at \(e^{-t^2}\), which is \(\int|t|g(t)dt<\infty\). On the other hand, the Cauchy distribution is perfectly symmetric around 0, but since \(\int|x|\frac{1}{\pi(1+x^2)}dx=\infty\), its mean is undefined. The mean cannot be concluded based on symmetry alone.

11.3.2 Variance — Integration by parts

\[\text{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t^2 g(t)\,dt\]

The key observation is \(g'(t) = -\dfrac{t}{\sigma^2}g(t)\), that is, \(t\,g(t) = -\sigma^2 g'(t)\). If we divide by \(t^2g(t) = t\cdot\big(t\,g(t)\big)\) and integrate by parts,

\[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t\cdot\big(-\sigma^2g'(t)\big)dt = -\sigma^2\Big[t\,g(t)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \sigma^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(t)\,dt = 0+\sigma^2 = \sigma^2\]

(The boundary term is 0 because the damping of \(e^{-t^2}\) overwhelms the increase of \(t\).) Therefore, the two parameters of the PDF, \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\), are the mean and variance, as their names suggest.

11.4 Moments

  • \(f(X)=X^n\): \(n\) time (raw moment)

\[\mu_n' = \mathbb{E}[X^n]\]

  • \(f(X)=(X-m_X)^n\): \(n\) Central moment

\[\mu_n = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-m_X)^n\big]\]

11.4.1 Point to Note 1 — The first moment of center is always 0

\[\mu_1 = \mathbb{E}[(X-m_X)^1] = \mathbb{E}[X] - m_X = m_X - m_X = 0\]

It is self-evident by the definition itself, \(m_X=\mathbb{E}[X]\).

11.4.2 Point to note 2 — “\(n=1 \Rightarrow\) mean” is an explanation regarding raw moment.

The list \(n=1 \Rightarrow\) mean, \(n=2 \Rightarrow\) variance, \(n=3 \Rightarrow\) skewness, \(n=4 \Rightarrow\) kurtosis is actually raw moment and central moment compressed into one sentence.

  • \(n=1\) → mean: This must refer to \(\mu_1'=\mathbb{E}[X]=m_X\) (raw moment). As seen above, since the center moment is \(\mu_1=0\), it cannot be the mean.
  • \(n=2\) → variance: This is a central moment. \(\mu_2 = \mathbb{E}[(X-m_X)^2]=\text{Var}(X)\) ✓
  • \(n=3\) → skewness: Strictly speaking, not \(\mu_3\) itself, but the standardized third center moment \(\mu_3/\sigma^3\).
  • \(n=4\) → kurtosis: Likewise, \(\mu_4/\sigma^4\) is closer to the general definition.

11.4.3 Information on primary and secondary statistics and the entire distribution

  • The mean \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) is a first-order statistic.
  • Power \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]\), variance, and covariance between two variables are second-order statistics.

Moments are merely numbers that summarize the distribution. The best case scenario is knowing the entire PDF, which allows one to calculate moments of all orders. The reverse direction generally does not hold.

  • A few moments are not enough: There are infinitely many distributions with the same mean and variance. For example, \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(\text{Uniform}(-\sqrt3,\sqrt3)\) have the same mean of 0 and variance of 1, but they are completely different distributions.
  • Knowing all moments may not be enough: There exist counterexamples, such as the log-normal distribution, where moments of all orders are the same but the distributions are different. For a column of moments to uniquely determine the distribution, additional conditions are required, and a representative sufficient condition, which will be discussed later, is that “MGF exists in the vicinity of \(s=0\)”.
  • Gaussian is an exception: Since \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) has two parameters that are the mean and variance, the distribution is completely determined by only the second-order statistic. This is the reason why dealing only with the covariance matrix under the Gaussian assumption is sufficient in communication and signal processing.

12 Characteristic Function

12.1 Definition

\[\varphi_X(jv) = \mathbb{E}\big[e^{jvX}\big] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p_X(x)\, e^{jvx}\, dx\]

This is essentially the Fourier transform of \(p_X(x)\), where \(j=\sqrt{-1}\) is the imaginary unit and \(v\) is the real variable.

Notation: \(\varphi_X(jv)\) and \(\Phi_X(jv)\) refer to the same object. If the argument is \(jv\) (purely imaginary), it refers to the characteristic function; if it is \(s\) (real), it refers to the MGF, which will be discussed later.

Uses: The characteristic function uniquely determines the distribution of \(X\) and is particularly useful when dealing with the sum of random variables.

12.2 Point to note — Sign convention: Is it “exactly” a Fourier transform?

The standard Fourier transform in signal processing uses a negative sign in the exponent:

\[\mathcal F\{p_X\}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(x)\,e^{-j\omega x}\,dx\]

Since the characteristic function uses the positive sign \(e^{+jvx}\), strictly speaking, it is the frequency axis inverted:

\[\varphi_X(jv) = \mathcal F\{p_X\}(-v) = \overline{\mathcal F\{p_X\}(v)} \qquad (p_X \text{가 실수 함수이므로})\]

In other words, the characteristic function is the conjugate of the Fourier transform according to signal processing conventions, and in terms of form, it is the same as the kernel of the inverse Fourier transform. The sign of the inversion formula is also changed accordingly:

\[p_X(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_X(jv)\,e^{-jvx}\,dv\]

Since the inversion of the frequency axis is a one-to-one correspondence, all structural properties, such as uniqueness and the convolution theorem, are preserved. However, when retrieving results from Fourier transform pair tables commonly used in signal processing, one must not forget the \(v\to-v\) substitution. (Particular caution is required because, like a Gaussian, if \(p_X\) is symmetric with respect to the origin, \(\varphi_X\) becomes a real even function, and the sign difference is not apparent.)

12.3 Why use imaginary numbers that do not physically exist

There are no imaginary numbers in real-world measurements. The inclusion of \(j\) in the characteristic function is due to mathematical generality, not physical reasons.

  • Always exists: \(|e^{jvx}|=1\), so the integral does not diverge for any distribution (compare in detail in the MGF section below).
  • It is a one-to-one correspondence: The PDF and the characteristic function uniquely determine each other. Therefore, there is no loss of information when defining the distribution as the characteristic function.
  • Converting integration to differentiation: To find the 3rd and 4th moments according to the definition, one must calculate \(\int x^n p_X(x)\,dx\) every time. On the other hand, since the characteristic function is in a closed form for major distributions, all orders of moments can be obtained by finding them in the table and simply differentiating. Differentiation can be calculated more mechanically than integration.

12.4 Why the distribution is uniquely determined — Lévy inversion formula

The approach of discussing inverse transformations using delta functions while assuming the existence of a density function does not apply to discrete or singular distributions. The rigorous basis that holds true regardless of the existence of density is the Lévy inversion formula.

Things to prove: If \(\varphi_X=\varphi_Y\), then the two distributions are equal — that is, \(X\mapsto\varphi_X\) is injective.

Define the following integral:

\[I_T := \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{e^{-jva}-e^{-jvb}}{jv}\,\varphi_X(jv)\,dv\]

Step 1 — Substitute definition and swap order. Since the integrand is bounded at \(v\in[-T,T]\), by Fubini

\[I_T = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{e^{jv(X-a)}-e^{jv(X-b)}}{jv}\,dv\right]\]

Step 2 — The inner integral is the Dirichlet integral. For any real number \(c\), \(\cos(vc)/v\) is an odd function, so it cancels out over the symmetric interval, leaving only the imaginary part:

\[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-T}^{T}\frac{e^{jvc}}{jv}dv = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^T \frac{\sin(vc)}{v}dv \;\xrightarrow{T\to\infty}\; \frac{1}{2}\,\text{sgn}(c)\]

(This is the Dirichlet integral \(\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(vc)}{v}dv = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{sgn}(c)\), and is in the same series as the sinc integral in signal processing.)

Step 3 — Assignment and Case Classification.

\[I_\infty = \mathbb{E}\left[\tfrac12\text{sgn}(X-a) - \tfrac12\text{sgn}(X-b)\right]\]

Location of \(X\) \(\text{sgn}(X-a)\) \(\text{sgn}(X-b)\) Parentheses value
\(X<a\) \(-1\) \(-1\) \(0\)
\(X=a\) \(0\) \(-1\) \(1/2\)
\(a<X<b\) \(+1\) \(-1\) \(1\)
\(X=b\) \(+1\) \(0\) \(1/2\)
\(X>b\) \(+1\) \(+1\) \(0\)

, that is, the parentheses are equal to \(\mathbf{1}_{\{a<X<b\}}+\tfrac12\mathbf{1}_{\{X=a\}}+\tfrac12\mathbf{1}_{\{X=b\}}\), and if you take the expected value

\[\boxed{I_\infty = \Pr[a<X<b] + \tfrac12\Pr[X=a] + \tfrac12\Pr[X=b]}\]

Conclusion: The left side is a value calculated solely from \(\varphi_X\), and the right side is an interval probability. Therefore, if \(\varphi_X=\varphi_Y\), then for all \(a<b\), it is \(\Pr[a<X<b]=\Pr[a<Y<b]\), and since the interval family generates a Borel sigma logarithm (\(\pi\) - \(\lambda\) theorem), the two distributions are identical.

12.5 Why it is useful for the sum of random variables — Convolution Theorem

If \(X,Y\) is independent and \(Z=X+Y\), then

\[\varphi_Z(jv) = \mathbb{E}[e^{jv(X+Y)}] = \mathbb{E}[e^{jvX}e^{jvY}] = \mathbb{E}[e^{jvX}]\,\mathbb{E}[e^{jvY}] = \varphi_X(jv)\varphi_Y(jv)\]

To directly calculate the pdf of the original \(Z\), a convolution integral is required:

\[p_Z(z) = (p_X * p_Y)(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(x)p_Y(z-x)\,dx\]

However, in the characteristic function domain, convolution changes to multiplication:

Probability Theory (Value Domain) Characteristic Function (Frequency Domain)
\(p_Z = p_X * p_Y\) (Convolution) \(\varphi_Z = \varphi_X \cdot \varphi_Y\) (Multiplication)

This is exactly the same structure as the time-domain convolution in an LTI system becoming the product of \(H(f)X(f)\) in the frequency domain. If it were the sum of \(n\) independent random variables, the convolution would have to be repeated \(n\) times, but for the characteristic function, it only requires multiplying \(n\). (This multiplication structure is also used critically in the proof of the Central Limit Theorem.)

13 Finding moments from the characteristic function

13.1 General Formula

\[\boxed{\mathbb{E}[X^n] = j^{-n}\left.\frac{d^n}{dv^n}\Phi_X(jv)\right|_{v=0}}\]

13.2 Induction

When differentiating \(\Phi_X(jv)=\mathbb{E}[e^{jvX}]\) with respect to \(v\), \(jX\) is multiplied by one each time:

\[\frac{d}{dv}\Phi_X(jv) = \mathbb{E}[jX\,e^{jvX}], \qquad \frac{d^2}{dv^2}\Phi_X(jv) = \mathbb{E}[(jX)^2 e^{jvX}]\]

If you repeat \(n\) times

\[\frac{d^n}{dv^n}\Phi_X(jv) = \mathbb{E}[(jX)^n e^{jvX}] = j^n\,\mathbb{E}[X^n e^{jvX}]\]

If we substitute \(v=0\), then \(e^{jvX}\to 1\).

\[\left.\frac{d^n}{dv^n}\Phi_X(jv)\right|_{v=0} = j^n\,\mathbb{E}[X^n]\]

Dividing both sides by \(j^n\) yields the formula above.

13.3 Example — Second moment

Differentiate once: \(\dfrac{d}{dv}\Phi_X(jv)=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(x)(jx)e^{jvx}dx\)

Differentiation twice: \(\dfrac{d^2}{dv^2}\Phi_X(jv)=\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(x)(jx)^2 e^{jvx}dx\)

If evaluated at \(v=0\)

\[\left.\frac{d^2}{dv^2}\Phi_X(jv)\right|_{v=0} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(x)(jx)^2dx = -\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^2 p_X(x)dx\]

Applying the formula \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]=j^{-2}\cdot(\cdot)\) results in \(j^{-2}=-1\), so

\[\mathbb{E}[X^2] = (-1)\cdot\left(-\int x^2 p_X(x)dx\right) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^2 p_X(x)\,dx \;\checkmark\]

13.4 Example — All order moments of the exponential distribution

Verify the utility of the characteristic function using \(p_X(x)=e^{-x}u(x)\) used in the previous conversion example.

13.4.1 Characteristic function calculation

\[\varphi_X(jv) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}e^{jvx}\,dx = \int_0^\infty e^{-(1-jv)x}\,dx = \left[\frac{e^{-(1-jv)x}}{-(1-jv)}\right]_0^\infty = \frac{1}{1-jv}\]

Since \(|e^{-(1-jv)x}| = e^{-x}\to0\), the value at the upper bound is 0. This calculation holds for all real numbers \(v\).

13.4.2 \(n\) first derivative

Each time \((1-jv)^{-1}\) is differentiated, the exponent decreases by 1, and by the chain rule, \((-1)\cdot(-j)=j\) is multiplied:

\[\frac{d}{dv}(1-jv)^{-1} = j\,(1-jv)^{-2}, \qquad \frac{d^2}{dv^2}(1-jv)^{-1} = 2\,j^2(1-jv)^{-3}\]

Inductively

\[\frac{d^n}{dv^n}\varphi_X(jv) = n!\,j^n(1-jv)^{-(n+1)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left.\frac{d^n}{dv^n}\varphi_X(jv)\right|_{v=0} = n!\,j^n\]

13.4.3 Moment

\[\mathbb{E}[X^n] = j^{-n}\cdot n!\,j^n = n!\]

Therefore, \(\mathbb{E}[X]=1\), \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]=2\), \(\text{Var}(X)=2-1^2=1\).

If calculated by definition, integration by parts must be repeated \(n\) times to obtain \(\int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x}dx=\Gamma(n+1)=n!\). Using the characteristic function, all orders are obtained at once with a single integration and mechanical differentiation.

Cross-validation: For \(Y=X^2\) from the previous section, directly calculating \(\mathbb{E}[Y]\) as \(p_Y\) yields \(\int_0^\infty y\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y}e^{-\sqrt y}dy \stackrel{y=x^2}{=} \int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x}dx = 2\), which matches \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]=2\). This specifically confirms that the same value is obtained whether \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\) is calculated as \(p_X\)Z or \(p_Y\).

13.5 Point to note — \(j^{-n}\) adjusts the phase, not the magnitude.

Since \(|j|=1\), multiplying by \(j^{-n}\) has no effect on magnitude at all. Since \(j=e^{j\pi/2}\), it is \(j^n=e^{jn\pi/2}\), which is pure phase rotation.

\(n \bmod 4\) \(j^n\) Meaning
\(0\) \(1\) No rotation
\(1\) \(j\) \(90°\) Rotation (Real → Imaginary)
\(2\) \(-1\) \(180°\) Rotation (Sign Inversion)
\(3\) \(-j\) \(270°\) Rotation
  • \(n\) is even: \(j^n=\pm1\), so it is pure sign inversion. The derivative is a real number, and the result is a real number.
  • \(n\) is odd: \(j^n=\pm j\). For example, if \(n=1\), \(\Phi_X'(0)=j\,\mathbb{E}[X]\) becomes the pure imaginary, and by multiplying this by \(j^{-1}=-j\), rotate and return to the real axis:

\[-j\cdot(j\,\mathbb{E}[X]) = -j^2\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X]\;\checkmark\]

Although it appears to be scaling where the “imaginary unit disappears and only the real magnitude remains,” it is actually phase correction that restores the phase by \(-n\pi/2\) while preserving the magnitude. It is exactly the same operation as restoring the phase by multiplying a complex baseband signal by \(e^{-j\theta}\).

14 Moment Generating Function (MGF)

14.1 Definition

\[\Phi_X(s) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \mathbb{E}[e^{sX}] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_X(x)\,e^{sx}\,dx\]

While the characteristic function uses the purely imaginary parameter \(jv\), the MGF uses the real variable \(s\). It is essentially the Laplace transform of \(p_X(x)\) and uniquely determines the distribution if it exists in the vicinity of \(s=0\).

14.2 Note on notation — \(\Phi_X(s)=\varphi_X(-js)\)

In the notation \(\varphi_X(jv)\), the real independent variable is \(v\). Since “\(jv\)” is not an indivisible block of symbols but the result of multiplication “\(j\) multiplied by \(v\)”, assignment must always be made only in the position of the variable \(v\).

\[\varphi_X(-js) = \mathbb{E}[e^{jvX}]\Big|_{v\to-js} = \mathbb{E}\left[e^{j\cdot(-js)\cdot X}\right] = \mathbb{E}[e^{sX}] = \Phi_X(s)\]

(\(j\cdot(-js)=-j^2s=s\).)

Substitution Result
\(v \to -js\) (Exactly assigned to variable) \(\varphi_X(-js)=\Phi_X(s)\) ✓
Replace the entire chunk “\(jv\)” with \(-js\) \(\mathbb{E}[e^{-jsX}]\) (Invalid assignment)

If you check the opposite direction as well, it clearly matches \(\Phi_X(s)|_{s\to jv}=\mathbb{E}[e^{jvX}]=\varphi_X(jv)\).

14.3 Points to Note — Correspondence with Laplace Transform and the Meaning of “More General”

14.3.1 symbol convention

The bilateral Laplace transform uses a negative sign as \(\mathcal L\{p_X\}(s)=\int p_X(x)e^{-sx}dx\), whereas MGF uses \(e^{+sx}\). Therefore

\[\Phi_X(s) = \mathcal L\{p_X\}(-s)\]

It is a sign inversion, similar to the relationship between the characteristic function and the Fourier transform.

14.3.2 Why “Laplace is more common” in signal processing

Replacing \(jv\) with \(s\) is equivalent to transitioning from the Fourier transform to the Laplace transform. The reason the Laplace transform is considered “more general” in signal processing is that even signals for which the Fourier integral does not converge (e.g., \(e^{at}u(t)\), \(a>0\)) can be brought to converge by multiplying by the attenuation factor \(e^{-\sigma t}\). In this case, the real part \(\sigma\) is selected in a direction that helps convergence.

14.3.3 In probability density, the direction is opposite

Applying this logic directly to probability density leads to an incorrect conclusion. Since probability densities are \(p_X\ge0\) and \(\int p_X=1\), they are already absolute integrable, and the Fourier transform (= characteristic function) always exists without a damping factor. Multiplying this by the real exponent \(e^{\sigma x}\) reduces one tail but expands the opposite tail exponentially. In other words, in probability density, \(\sigma\ne0\) does not create new convergence; it can only break convergence.

General signal \(x(t)\) Probability density \(p_X(x)\)
Fourier (Imaginary Axis) May not exist Always exist (\(\lvert\varphi_X\rvert\le1\))
Role of the real part \(\sigma\) Creates convergence through damping Can break convergence by amplifying one tail
“More general” side Laplace In terms of existence characteristic function

Therefore, in probability theory, the statement that the MGF is “more general” is correct only in the sense that the domain extends from the imaginary axis to the complex plane. Conversely, in terms of existence, the characteristic function is more general. This difference is discussed in detail in the following section.

14.4 Why use a separate characteristic function — The problem of existence

When considering only moment extraction, MGF is intuitive and convenient to calculate. Nevertheless, the reason a characteristic function must be written separately is solely due to existence.

14.4.1 MGF can diverge

Since \(s\) is a real number, \(e^{sx}\) expands at \(x\to\pm\infty\). If the tail of \(p_X\) does not overwhelm it, the integral diverges.

Counterexample — Cauchy distribution \(p_X(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^2)}\):

\[\Phi_X(s)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{sx}}{\pi(1+x^2)}dx = \infty \quad (s\neq0)\]

The numerator grows exponentially, but the denominator only grows as \(x^2\), so it diverges. In other words, the Cauchy distribution does not have an MGF. (In the first place, not even \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) exists.)

14.4.2 The characteristic function always exists

Since the exponent part is a purely imaginary number, it is \(|e^{jvx}|=\sqrt{\cos^2(vx)+\sin^2(vx)}=1\), and therefore

\[|\varphi_X(jv)| \le \int p_X(x)\underbrace{|e^{jvx}|}_{=1}dx = \int p_X(x)dx = 1\]

As long as \(p_X\) is a normal density function, it converges absolutely and is bounded in any distribution. The Cauchy distribution also exists neatly as \(\varphi_X(jv)=e^{-|v|}\) without exception.

14.5 The essential difference between the two transformations — two sections of a single complex function

If you define a function extended to \(s=\sigma+j\omega\)

\[\Psi_X(\sigma+j\omega) = \mathbb{E}\left[e^{(\sigma+j\omega)X}\right] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\underbrace{\big[p_X(x)e^{\sigma x}\big]}_{\text{실수 지수로 가중된 밀도}}e^{j\omega x}dx = \mathcal{F}\{p_X(x)e^{\sigma x}\}(\omega)\]

Here, it is revealed that the two functions are two mutually orthogonal axis sections of the same complex function:

  • \(\sigma=0\) Fixed, \(\omega\) Varying (Imaginary axis) → \(\Psi_X(j\omega)=\varphi_X(j\omega)\) = Characteristic function
  • \(\omega=0\) Fixed, \(\sigma\) Changing (Real Contraction) → \(\Psi_X(\sigma)=\Phi_X(\sigma)\) = MGF

14.5.1 Region of Convergence (ROC)

The region where \(\Psi_X(s)\) absolutely converges is generally in the form of a vertical strip \(\{a<\sigma<b\}\) in the complex plane. The key point is:

ROC always includes the entire imaginary axis, which is \(\sigma=0\) (since \(|\Psi_X(j\omega)|\le1\)). That is, \(a\le0\le b\).

The problem is that this width \(b-a\) could be 0. Then ROC shrinks into the imaginary axis itself, and MGF does not exist.

Cauchy: Diverges from \(\sigma\neq0\) → ROC width 0 → No MGF. (CF exists as \(e^{-|\omega|}\))

Exponential Distribution \(p_X(x)=e^{-x}(x>0)\):

\[\Psi_X(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}e^{sx}dx = \int_0^\infty e^{(s-1)x}dx\]

Since it converges at \(\text{Re}(s)<1\), ROC \(=\{s:\text{Re}(s)<1\}\) — and has a positive width, MGF exists.

CF: \(\varphi_X(j\omega)\) MGF: \(\Phi_X(\sigma)\)
Position at \(\Psi_X(s)\) \(s=j\omega\) (Imaginary axis slice) \(s=\sigma\) (Real axis slice)
Role of exponents Phase rotation only, magnitude \(=1\) Exponential damping / growth
Existence condition None — Automatically guaranteed in all distributions ROC needs positive width near \(\sigma=0\)
Cauchy Exists Does not exist
Exponential distribution Existence Existence (ROC \(=(-\infty,1)\))

Summary: The primary design goal of a characteristic function is to “never lose existence” (theoretical foundations such as the uniqueness theorem, inversion formula, and CLT proofs), and the MGF is a practical alternative that is “easier to compute as long as it exists” (it ends up as a real-valued calculation, eliminating the need for phase corrections like \(j^{-n}\), and it is natural to optimize \(s\) within the real range in Chernoff bound or large deviation analysis).

15 MGF and Moment

15.1 Core properties

\[\boxed{\mathbb{E}[X^n] = \left.\frac{d^n}{ds^n}\Phi_X(s)\right|_{s=0}}\]

  • \(n=1\): \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \Phi_X'(0)\) (average)
  • \(n=2\): \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \Phi_X''(0)\) (second moment)

15.2 Why it holds — Taylor expansion

15.2.1 Preparation: Maclaurin series induction of \(e^z\)

When a function expands into \(f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n z^n\), the coefficients are determined by the derivative.

  • \(z=0\) Substitution: \(f(0)=c_0\)
  • After one differentiation \(z=0\): \(f'(z)=c_1+2c_2z+3c_3z^2+\cdots\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f'(0)=c_1\)
  • After two differentiations \(z=0\): \(f''(z)=2c_2+6c_3z+\cdots\) \(\Rightarrow\) \(f''(0)=2!\,c_2\)

In general, differentiating \(n\) times results in the \(z^n\) term becoming \(c_n\cdot n!\), lower orders cancel out, and higher orders cancel out at \(z=0\), leaving \(z\):

\[f^{(n)}(0)=n!\,c_n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{c_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}}\]

Now apply this to \(f(z)=e^z\). The deterministic property of exponential functions is that they are themselves when differentiated:

\[f^{(n)}(z)=e^z \;\;\forall n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f^{(n)}(0)=e^0=1 \;\;\forall n\]

Since the derivatives of all orders are all 1, it is \(c_n = 1/n!\), and

\[\boxed{e^z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n!} = 1+z+\frac{z^2}{2!}+\frac{z^3}{3!}+\cdots}\]

Verification: Differentiating term by term yields \(\dfrac{n\,z^{n-1}}{n!}=\dfrac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\), reproducing the identical form of the original series. In other words, \(\frac{d}{dz}e^z=e^z\) automatically holds true in the series as well.

15.2.2 Apply to MGF

If you substitute \(z=sX\)

\[e^{sX} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(sX)^n}{n!} = 1+sX+\frac{s^2}{2!}X^2+\cdots\]

If we take the expectation of both sides and perform term-wise integration (swapping the order of the series and the expectation),

\[\Phi_X(s) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{s^n}{n!}\mathbb{E}[X^n] = 1 + s\,\mathbb{E}[X] + \frac{s^2}{2!}\mathbb{E}[X^2] + \frac{s^3}{3!}\mathbb{E}[X^3]+\cdots\]

(For this order exchange to be justified, the MGF must converge near \(s=0\) — this is exactly the same requirement as the ROC condition discussed earlier. For distributions without an MGF, such as Cauchy, this expansion itself does not hold.)

, that is, the coefficient of \(s^n/n!\) is exactly \(\mathbb{E}[X^n]\). If we substitute this into the Maclaurin coefficient formula \(c_n=f^{(n)}(0)/n!\),

\[\frac{\mathbb{E}[X^n]}{n!} = \frac{\Phi_X^{(n)}(0)}{n!} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}[X^n]=\Phi_X^{(n)}(0)\]

15.2.3 Check directly

\[\Phi_X'(s)=\mathbb{E}[X]+s\,\mathbb{E}[X^2]+\frac{s^2}{2}\mathbb{E}[X^3]+\cdots \;\Rightarrow\; \Phi_X'(0)=\mathbb{E}[X]\;\checkmark\]

\[\Phi_X''(s)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]+s\,\mathbb{E}[X^3]+\cdots \;\Rightarrow\; \Phi_X''(0)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]\;\checkmark\]

This is precisely why the MGF is called the “moment generating function” — essentially, it is a generating function that takes moments as coefficients.

15.3 Example — MGF of the exponential distribution

About \(p_X(x)=e^{-x}u(x)\)

\[\Phi_X(s) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x}e^{sx}\,dx = \int_0^\infty e^{-(1-s)x}\,dx = \frac{1}{1-s}, \qquad s<1\]

, and the integral diverges at \(s\ge1\). This is the real axis cross-section of ROC \(\{\text{Re}(s)<1\}\) seen earlier.

By differentiation: Since \(\Phi_X^{(n)}(s) = n!\,(1-s)^{-(n+1)}\), \(\Phi_X^{(n)}(0)=n!\).

In series expansion: If we expand \(|s|<1\) into a geometric series

\[\frac{1}{1-s} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty s^n = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{s^n}{n!}\cdot n!\]

Since the coefficient of \(s^n/n!\) is \(n!\), it is also \(\mathbb{E}[X^n]=n!\).

matches the result obtained using the characteristic function, and the calculation is one step simpler in that \(j^{-n}\) does not require phase correction. Since this distribution represents the case where the MGF exists in the vicinity of \(s=0\), both methods can be used.

16 Jointly Distributed Random Variables

16.1 Definition

Two random variables can be defined on the same sample space:

\[X:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}, \qquad Y:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}\]

Combined CDF:

\[P_{X,Y}(x,y) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \Pr[X\le x,\; Y\le y]\]

Combined PDF (if present):

\[p_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x\,\partial y}P_{X,Y}(x,y)\]

Marginal Distribution: Completely integrate one variable to eliminate it.

\[p_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy, \qquad p_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\]

16.2 Points to note 0 — Two random variables from different experiments

The condition that “is defined on the same sample space” does not exclude random variables from different experiments, such as the case where a coin toss produces \(X\) and a die roll produces \(Y\). The two experiments can be viewed as a single compound experiment:

\[\Omega = \Omega_1\times\Omega_2 = \{H,T\}\times\{1,\ldots,6\}, \qquad \omega=(\omega_1,\omega_2)\]

If we define and \(X(\omega):=X_1(\omega_1)\) and \(Y(\omega):=Y_2(\omega_2)\), the two random variables lie on the same \(\Omega\). The point of this definition is that while each experiment is mapped separately to real numbers, a common sample space is required to discuss joint probabilities.

16.3 Events of a joint CDF — Intersection

The comma in the definition of a combined CDF means “and,” that is, that two events occur simultaneously:

\[\Pr[X\le x,\;Y\le y] = \Pr\Big[\{\omega: X(\omega)\le x\}\cap\{\omega: Y(\omega)\le y\}\Big]\]

Since it is the intersection of two events \(\{X\le x\}\) of a 1-variable CDF, it is the most natural extension of the definition of a 1-variable CDF.

16.4 Derivation of Marginal Distribution — Starting from CDF

Since \(\{Y\le\infty\}=\Omega\), if one variable is sent to infinity, the constraint disappears:

\[P_X(x) = \Pr[X\le x,\;Y<\infty] = P_{X,Y}(x,\infty) = \int_{-\infty}^{x}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{X,Y}(u,v)\,dv\right]du\]

When differentiating with respect to \(x\), the inside of the brackets remains unchanged due to the FTC, yielding the surrounding PDF formula. In other words, “integrating over the entire range in the direction of \(y\) to eliminate the influence of \(y\)” is an expression at the density level of the event structure that “no constraints are imposed on \(Y\).”

16.5 Point to Note 1 — Why is it partial differentiation and not just differentiation?

In one dimension, as in \(p_X(x)=\dfrac{d}{dx}P_X(x)\), there is only one variable, so “differentiation” is uniquely defined. However, since \(P_{X,Y}(x,y)\) is a two-variable function with two independent variables from the outset, the concept of “just differentiation” does not exist. It must be specified which variable the differentiation is with respect to, and that is partial differentiation. It is notation that is unavoidable, not a choice.

16.6 Point to Note 2 — Why Mixed Second-Order Partial Derivatives?

This is because the combined CDF is defined as a double integral:

\[P_{X,Y}(x,y) = \int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{y}p_{X,Y}(u,v)\,dv\,du\]

Step 1 — Partial derivative with respect to \(x\) (\(y\) fixed). Applying the Fundamental Theorem of Single-Variable Calculus (FTC) to the outer integral

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}P_{X,Y}(x,y) = \int_{-\infty}^{y}p_{X,Y}(x,v)\,dv\]

Step 2 — Take the partial derivative with respect to \(y\) here (\(x\) fixed). Since the remaining expression is a one-variable integral with \(y\) as the upper bound, apply FTC again:

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{y}p_{X,Y}(x,v)dv\right] = p_{X,Y}(x,y)\]

In other words, to reverse the integration applied once in each direction, differentiation must also be performed twice in total, once in each direction. It is simply applying the 1D FTC twice in sequence.

16.7 Point to Note 3 — Does it hold even if \(X,Y\) is dependent?

Holds. The tools used in the above derivation are (i) the definition of the joint CDF and (ii) the one-variable FTC in each direction—these are the only two, and the assumption of independence was never used.

The role of independence lies in a completely different place. If independent, bond density is decomposed into products:

\[p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y) \qquad (X\perp Y \text{일 때만})\]

This product decomposition breaks in the case of dependency, but the relation \(p_{X,Y}=\partial^2 P_{X,Y}/\partial x\partial y\) is a definitive relation that always holds regardless of dependency or independence.

17 Conditional density, independence, covariance

17.1 Conditional PDF

When \(p_Y(y)\neq0\),

\[p_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{p_{X,Y}(x,y)}{p_Y(y)}\]

is the same shape as the conditional probability of the event \(\Pr[A\mid B]=\Pr[A\cap B]/\Pr[B]\). The numerator is the joint, and the denominator is the marginal distribution of the variable given as a condition.

17.2 Point to note — Why is a conditional event defined when its probability is 0?

For a continuous random variable, ZZZPH0001259ZZZ , we have \(\Pr[Y=y]=0\). Therefore, if we substitute \(B=\{Y=y\}\) directly into the definition of the conditional probability of an event, we get \(0/0\). The conditional PDF solves this problem using the limit of a conditional event with width.

If we set \(B_\Delta := \{y<Y\le y+\Delta\}\), then \(\Pr[B_\Delta]>0\), so the conditional probability of the event can be used as is:

\[\Pr[X\le x\mid B_\Delta] = \frac{\Pr[X\le x,\;y<Y\le y+\Delta]}{\Pr[y<Y\le y+\Delta]} = \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{y}^{y+\Delta}p_{X,Y}(u,v)\,dv\,du}{\int_{y}^{y+\Delta}p_Y(v)\,dv}\]

If \(\Delta\) is smaller, the inner integral of the numerator is \(\approx p_{X,Y}(u,y)\Delta\), and the denominator is \(\approx p_Y(y)\Delta\). If \(\Delta\) cancels out and \(\Delta\to0\) is taken,

\[P_{X|Y}(x|y) = \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{x}p_{X,Y}(u,y)\,du}{p_Y(y)}\]

Differentiating with respect to \(x\) yields the above conditional PDF exactly. Furthermore, since it is \(\int p_{X|Y}(x|y)\,dx = p_Y(y)/p_Y(y)=1\), it is a normal PDF for \(x\) for every fixed \(y\). In other words, the conditional PDF is normalized by the area \(p_Y(y)\) of the cross-section obtained by cutting the combined PDF into \(y\) slices.

17.3 Independent

\[p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y) \iff p_{X|Y}(x|y)=p_X(x)\]

The equivalence of comes directly from the definition of a conditional PDF (where \(p_Y(y)>0\), you divide or multiply both sides by \(p_Y(y)\)). Both expressions translate the event independence \(\Pr[A\cap B]=\Pr[A]\Pr[B]\) and \(\Pr[A\mid B]=\Pr[A]\) into density levels.

17.4 Point to note — The joint distribution cannot be determined from the marginal distributions alone

If independent, the joint PDF is determined by the product of the neighbor PDFs. However, in the case of dependency, even if both neighbor PDFs are known, the joint PDF cannot be determined. Since marginalization is an operation that discards information through integration, information about the dependency structure between two variables is lost.

Counterexample — Bivariate Gaussian. For each \(|\rho|<1\) such that \(\rho\)

\[p_{X,Y}(x,y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^2-2\rho xy+y^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\right)\]

Let . If we take the perfect square for \(x\) in the exponent part,

\[x^2-2\rho xy+y^2 = (x-\rho y)^2 + (1-\rho^2)y^2\]

, therefore

\[p_{X,Y}(x,y) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^2/2}}_{\mathcal N(0,1)\text{ in }y}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(1-\rho^2)}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\rho y)^2}{2(1-\rho^2)}\right)}_{\mathcal N(\rho y,\,1-\rho^2)\text{ in }x}\]

Integrating the second factor over \(x\) yields 1, so it is \(p_Y(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-y^2/2}\), and by symmetry, \(p_X\) is also \(\mathcal N(0,1)\).

  • For all \(\rho\in(-1,1)\), the marginal distribution is the same: \(\mathcal N(0,1)\). However, the joint distribution differs for every \(\rho\). Therefore, it is impossible to reconstruct the joint distribution from the marginal distributions.
  • The combined PDF becomes equal to \(p_X(x)p_Y(y)\) only when it is \(\rho=0\).
  • Additionally, the second factor of the above decomposition is the conditional PDF: \(p_{X|Y}(x|y)=\mathcal N(\rho y,\,1-\rho^2)\). Observing \(Y\) shifts the mean of \(X\) to \(\rho y\) and reduces the variance to \(1-\rho^2\). In other words, the larger \(|\rho|\) is, the more information \(Y\) provides regarding \(X\).

In this example, \(\rho=0\) (uncorrelated) implies independence. However, this is a special property of Gaussians, and generally, uncorrelatedness does not imply independence (see the counterexample in the Uncorrelated vs. Independence section below).

17.5 Covariance

\[\text{cov}[X,Y] = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-m_X)(Y-m_Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]\]

17.6 Correlation Coefficient

\[\rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\text{cov}[X,Y]}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\]

is a standardized covariance quantity that always lies between \(-1\) and \(1\), indicating how strongly \(X\) and \(Y\) are linearly related.

17.7 Why \(-1\le\rho_{X,Y}\le1\) — Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

17.7.1 Step 1 — Variance is not always negative

Define a new random variable for an arbitrary real number \(t\):

\[Z(t) := (X-m_X) - t(Y-m_Y)\]

Since is the expectation of the square of |||, \(\mathbb{E}[Z(t)^2]\ge0\) holds for all \(t\).

17.7.2 Step 2 — Expansion

\[\mathbb{E}[Z(t)^2] = \mathbb{E}[(X-m_X)^2] - 2t\,\mathbb{E}[(X-m_X)(Y-m_Y)] + t^2\mathbb{E}[(Y-m_Y)^2]\]

\[= \sigma_X^2 - 2t\,\text{cov}[X,Y] + t^2\sigma_Y^2 =: g(t)\]

17.7.3 Step 3 — Discriminant Condition

For \(g(t)=At^2+Bt+C\) (\(A=\sigma_Y^2\ge0\), \(B=-2\,\text{cov}[X,Y]\), \(C=\sigma_X^2\)) to be greater than or equal to 0 for all real numbers \(t\), the discriminant must be less than or equal to 0. (If the discriminant is positive, there exist two distinct real roots, and between them, \(g(t)<0\), which leads to a contradiction.)

\[B^2-4AC = 4\,\text{cov}[X,Y]^2 - 4\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2 \le 0\]

\[\Longrightarrow\quad \text{cov}[X,Y]^2 \le \sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2\]

This is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (equivalent to the case where \(\langle U,V\rangle:=\mathbb{E}[UV]\) is set to \(U=X-m_X\) and \(V=Y-m_Y\) in the inner product structure).

17.7.4 Step 4 — Conclusion

\[|\text{cov}[X,Y]|\le\sigma_X\sigma_Y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{-1\le\rho_{X,Y}\le1}\]

17.7.5 Equality condition — Perfect linear relationship

If the discriminant is exactly 0, then ZZZPH0001288ZZZ , since \(g(t)\) has a double root \(t_0\), it becomes \(\mathbb{E}[Z(t_0)^2]=0\). For the expected value of a non-negative random variable to be 0, that variable must be almost certainly 0, so

\[X = m_X + t_0(Y-m_Y) \quad\text{(확률 1로)}\]

, that is, \(|\rho_{X,Y}|=1\) is equivalent to the perfect affine relationship between \(X\) and \(Y\). This is the rigorous basis for the statement that \(\rho\) is a measure of “linear” relevance.

For reference, this derivation stems from the same root as the PSD property of the covariance matrix (\(\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{K}_X\mathbf{a}\ge0\)) that will be discussed later. \(2\times2\) is identical to reproducing \(\det\mathbf{K}=\sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2-\text{cov}[X,Y]^2\ge0\) of the covariance matrix as a special case.

18 Uncorrelated vs Independent

18.1 relationship

\[\text{독립} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{무상관}, \qquad \text{무상관} \;\not\Longrightarrow\; \text{독립}\]

If \(\rho_{X,Y}=0\), then \(\text{cov}[X,Y]=0\), and therefore \(\mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]\).

18.2 Independence \(\Rightarrow\) Proof of non-correlation

Definition of Independence Substitute \(p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)\) into \(\mathbb{E}[XY]\):

\[\mathbb{E}[XY] = \int\int xy\,p_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy = \int\int xy\,p_X(x)p_Y(y)\,dx\,dy\]

The double integral is split because the integrand is completely separated into the product of the parts \(x\) and \(y\). If we look at the inner integral for \(y\) first…

\[\int\left[\int xy\,p_X(x)p_Y(y)\,dy\right]dx = \int x\,p_X(x)\underbrace{\left[\int y\,p_Y(y)\,dy\right]}_{x\text{와 무관한 상수}}dx\]

If you pull the constant out,

\[\mathbb{E}[XY] = \left[\int x\,p_X(x)dx\right]\left[\int y\,p_Y(y)dy\right] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]\]

Therefore

\[\text{cov}[X,Y] = \mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \rho_{X,Y}=0\]

18.3 Counterexample — Unrelated but dependent

Let’s call it \(X\sim\text{Uniform}(-1,1)\), \(Y=X^2\).

\[\mathbb{E}[X]=0, \qquad \mathbb{E}[XY]=\mathbb{E}[X^3]=\int_{-1}^{1}x^3\cdot\frac{1}{2}\,dx = 0\]

(Since \(x^3\) is an odd function, the integral over the symmetric interval is 0.) Therefore

\[\text{cov}[X,Y]=\mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]=0\]

In other words, it is unrelated.

18.3.1 Show non-independence as \(\Pr[A\cap B]\neq\Pr[A]\Pr[B]\)

Rather than relying on the statement that “\(Y\) is a deterministic function of \(X\),” we give a pair of events that directly violates the definition of independence. Since independence requires \(\Pr[A\cap B]=\Pr[A]\Pr[B]\) for every pair of events, a single counterexample is sufficient.

\[A = \left\{X>\tfrac12\right\}, \qquad B = \left\{Y<\tfrac14\right\} = \left\{-\tfrac12<X<\tfrac12\right\}\]

\(p_X(x)=\tfrac12\) on \((-1,1)\) since

\[\Pr[A] = \int_{1/2}^{1}\tfrac12 dx = \tfrac14, \qquad \Pr[B] = \int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\tfrac12 dx = \tfrac12\]

\[\Pr[A]\Pr[B] = \tfrac14\times\tfrac12 = \tfrac18\]

However, since \(A\cap B = \{X>\tfrac12\}\cap\{X<\tfrac12\} = \varnothing\)

\[\Pr[A\cap B] = 0 \;\neq\; \tfrac18 = \Pr[A]\Pr[B]\]

Therefore, they are not independent.

Structural reason: The joint distribution of \((X,Y)\) is a degenerate distribution in which probability mass is carried only on the parabola \(y=x^2\) of the \(xy\)-plane. If they were independent, the joint distribution would have to be spread across the entire rectangular region \((-1,1)\times(0,1)\), so the support set itself is different. Therefore, any number of counterexamples like the one above can be created.

18.4 Why does it hold true in only one direction?

  • Independence(\(p_{X,Y}=p_Xp_Y\)) is a very strong condition that the entire joint density is decomposed into a product, and implies \(\mathbb{E}[g(X)h(Y)]=\mathbb{E}[g(X)]\mathbb{E}[h(Y)]\) for all pairs of functions \(g,h\). The proof above simply made specific choices of \(g(x)=x\) and \(h(y)=y\).
  • No correlation is a much weaker condition stating that the equality holds only for exactly one pair of functions, \(g(x)=x\) and \(h(y)=y\).

\[\text{독립} = \text{"모든" 함수쌍에 대해 분해 가능} \;\supsetneq\; \text{무상관} = \text{"1차 함수쌍" 하나에 대해서만 분해 가능}\]

Since the fact that one special case holds does not mean that the general proposition holds, the converse does not hold.

19 Conditional Expectation

19.1 Definition

\[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,p_{X|Y}(x|y)\,dx\]

19.2 Iterated Expectation (Tower Property)

\[\boxed{\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big] = \mathbb{E}[X]}\]

Interpretation: First, calculate the average of \(X\) within each slice named \(Y=y\), and then average those averages over \(Y\).

19.3 Induction

19.3.1 Preparation — The identity of \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\)

\(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y]\) is a function for \(y\):

\[g(y) := \mathbb{E}[X\mid Y=y] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,p_{X|Y}(x|y)\,dx\]

Therefore, \(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\) is \(g(Y)\), which is obtained by substituting the random variable \(Y\) into this function, and

\[\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big] = \mathbb{E}[g(Y)] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(y)\,p_Y(y)\,dy\]

19.3.2 This induction

Substitute the definition of \(g(y)\) and move \(p_Y(y)\) into the inner integral (\(y\) is independent of \(x\), so it can be moved freely):

\[= \int\int x\,p_{X|Y}(x|y)\,p_Y(y)\,dx\,dy\]

Here, we write the definition of conditional probability:

\[p_{X|Y}(x|y)\,p_Y(y) = p_{X,Y}(x,y)\]

\[= \int\int x\,p_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dx\,dy\]

If we change the order of integration and integrate over \(y\) first (treating \(x\) as a constant), the inner integral becomes marginalized

\[= \int x\underbrace{\left[\int p_{X,Y}(x,y)\,dy\right]}_{=\,p_X(x)}dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,p_X(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}[X] \quad\blacksquare\]

19.3.3 Two key tricks

  1. Conditional Density \(\times\) Marginal Density \(=\) Bond Density
  2. Integral Order Exchange (Fubini) — If we integrate \(y\) first to cancel it out, only the marginal density of \(X\) remains

That is, the structure that “double averaging” is ultimately equivalent to averaging once over the entire joint density is revealed through the exchange of integral orders.

20 Complex vectors and Hermitian transposes

20.1 Conjugate

  • The conjugate of the complex number \(z=a+jb\) is \(\bar z = a-jb\)
  • The conjugate of the complex vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^K\) is the conjugate of each component.

20.2 Conjugate / Hermitian Transpose

\[\mathbf{x}^H = \overline{\mathbf{x}^T}, \qquad \mathbf{A}^H = \overline{\mathbf{A}^T}\]

Notation convention: Here, the conjugate prefix is consistently written as \((\cdot)^H\) instead of \((\cdot)^*\).

21 Properties of the Covariance Matrix (Complex Case)

21.1 Definition

The covariance matrix of the probability vector \(\mathbf{X}\) is

\[\mathbf{K}_X = \mathbb{E}\Big[(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}])(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}])^H\Big]\]

This is an extension of the scalar variance \(\text{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[(X-m_X)^2]\) into a vector; since “squaring” is not defined between vectors, the outer product is used instead. The elements of this matrix \((i,j)\) are \(\text{Cov}(X_i,X_j^*)\), so the covariance information between all pairs of elements is stored in a single matrix. The difference from the case of real numbers is that since it is a complex number, the conjugate transpose is used instead of the transpose.

21.2 Property 1 — Hermitian: \(\mathbf{K}_X=\mathbf{K}_X^H\)

21.2.1 Step 1 — The cross product of a fixed vector is always Hermitian

For any fixed \(\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^K\), let \(\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^H\), then by the conjugate transposition rule \((\mathbf{AB})^H=\mathbf{B}^H\mathbf{A}^H\)

\[\mathbf{A}^H = (\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^H)^H = (\mathbf{z}^H)^H\mathbf{z}^H = \mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}^H = \mathbf{A}\]

(\((\mathbf{z}^H)^H=\mathbf{z}\) — Taking the conjugate transpose twice returns to the original.)

Confirmed by ingredient: \(A_{ij}=z_i z_j^*\)

\[A_{ji}^* = (z_j z_i^*)^* = z_j^* z_i = z_i z_j^* = A_{ij}\]

, i.e., \(A_{ij}=A_{ji}^*\) — satisfies the component-specific definition of the Hermitian.

21.2.2 Step 2 — Preserved even when taking the expected value

If we let \(\mathbf{Z}:=\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]\), then \(\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^H\) is the Hermitian at every sample point. Since the expectation is a component-wise linear operation and can be conjugated or reversed (\(\mathbb{E}[W]^*=\mathbb{E}[W^*]\)),

\[(\mathbf{K}_X)_{ji}^* = \big(\mathbb{E}[Z_jZ_i^*]\big)^* = \mathbb{E}[Z_j^*Z_i] = \mathbb{E}[Z_iZ_j^*] = (\mathbf{K}_X)_{ij}\]

\[\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{K}_X=\mathbf{K}_X^H\]

Key Point: Hermitianity is automatically guaranteed solely by the algebraic fact that “external structure + expectation is a linear operation,” completely independent of the distribution of \(\mathbf{X}\).

21.3 Property 2 — Positive Semidefinite (PSD)

For any \(\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{C}^K\)

\[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{K}_X\mathbf{a} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\mathbf{a}^H(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}])\big|^2\Big] \;\ge\; 0\]

21.3.1 Step 1 — Move the constant \(\mathbf{a}\) into the expectation value

\(\mathbf{a}\) is not a random variable but a fixed constant vector, and since the expectation is a linear operation,

\[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^H]\mathbf{a} = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^H\mathbf{a}\big]\]

21.3.2 Step 2 — Reinterpret as the product of two scalars

If you move the parentheses using the associative property

\[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{Z}^H\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{Z})(\mathbf{Z}^H\mathbf{a})\]

Since \(\mathbf{a}^H\) is \(1\times K\) and \(\mathbf{Z}\) is \(K\times1\), \(W:=\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{Z}\) is a scalar. And

\[\mathbf{Z}^H\mathbf{a} = (\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{Z})^H = W^H = W^*\]

(The transpose of a scalar is just the conjugate.)

21.3.3 Step 3 — \(WW^*=|W|^2\)

If we let \(W=u+jv\), then \(WW^*=(u+jv)(u-jv)=u^2+v^2=|W|^2\). Therefore

\[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{K}_X\mathbf{a} = \mathbb{E}\big[|W|^2\big] = \mathbb{E}\Big[\big|\mathbf{a}^H(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}])\big|^2\Big]\]

21.3.4 Step 4 — Not a sound

Since \(|W|^2\ge0\) holds for every point, and the expected value of a non-negative random variable is not negative,

\[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{K}_X\mathbf{a}\ge0 \quad \forall\,\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{C}^K\]

This is exactly the definition of PSD. In this chain, no assumptions about the distribution of \(\mathbf{X}\) are used anywhere — the single fact that “squares are always non-negative” leads to everything.

21.4 Property 3 — Positive Definite (PD) Condition

When does equality hold? For the expected value of a non-negative random variable to be exactly 0, the variable must be 0 with a probability of 1:

\[\mathbb{E}[|W|^2]=0 \iff W=\mathbf{a}^H(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}])=0 \quad\text{(a.s.)}\]

If \(\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}\), it is self-evident but not of interest. The issue is the case where this equation holds even though it is \(\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{0}\), which means there is a linear dependency between the components of \(\mathbf{X}\).

As an extreme example, if a component \(X_k\) is a constant (variance 0), then if we choose \(\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{e}_k\) (standard basis),

\[\mathbf{a}^H(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]) = X_k-\mathbb{E}[X_k]=0 \quad\text{(항상)}\]

Thus, a counterexample \(\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{0}\) is created. Therefore, the condition “no component has a variance of zero” precisely excludes this degenerate relationship, and in that case

\[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{K}_X\mathbf{a}>0 \quad \forall\,\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{0}\]

, that is, \(\mathbf{K}_X\) becomes positive definite.

21.5 Interpretation — Quadratic forms are variance

\(W=\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{X}]\) Therefore

\[\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{K}_X\mathbf{a} = \text{Var}(\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{X})\]

“Variance can never be negative”, a self-evident fact, when translated into matrix language, becomes the proposition “\(\mathbf{K}_X\) is PSD.” In the context of wireless communication, if \(\mathbf{a}\) is viewed as a beamforming vector, \(\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{K}_X\mathbf{a}\) corresponds to the power of the beamforming output, and the PSD property mathematically guarantees the physical requirement that the power is always not negative.

21.6 Why are these properties important?

21.6.1 (1) Minimum guarantee of validity

If the PSD is broken, it becomes \(\text{Var}(\mathbf{a}^H\mathbf{X})<0\) for any direction \(\mathbf{a}\), which is a physical quantity that cannot exist. In other words, PSD properties are a checklist to verify “whether this matrix qualifies as a genuine covariance matrix.” This is the basis for using PSD projection techniques that clip negative eigenvalues to zero when \(\hat{\mathbf{K}}_X\) estimated due to insufficient sample size or numerical error slightly breaks the PSD in practice.

21.6.2 (2) Eigenvalue decomposition is always clean — Spectral Theorem

\[\mathbf{K}_X = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{U}^H, \qquad \mathbf{\Lambda}=\text{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_K),\;\lambda_i\ge0\]

  • Since it is a Hermitian, it can always be diagonalized into a unitary matrix \(\mathbf{U}\) where the eigenvectors are orthogonal (such decomposition is not guaranteed for general matrices)
  • Since it is a PSD, it is guaranteed that all eigenvalues are non-negative real numbers

This is the basis of “why it always works” MIMO channel eigenmode decomposition, spatial multiplexing, water-filling power allocation, PCA/KL deployment, etc.

21.6.3 (3) Existence of whitening filter

If PD, a square root matrix exists, so if transformed into \(\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{K}_X^{-1/2}\mathbf{X}\)

\[\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^H] = \mathbf{K}_X^{-1/2}\mathbf{K}_X(\mathbf{K}_X^{-1/2})^H = \mathbf{I}\]

This is whitening, a preprocessing step that removes noise correlations to enable the application of an optimal receiver under white noise assumptions. Without PD, this procedure is impossible because \(\mathbf{K}_X^{-1}\) itself does not exist.

21.6.4 (4) Whether PD exists is a determination of “substantial degrees of freedom”

If PSD but not PD (some eigenvalues are 0), it means that the distribution of \(\mathbf{X}\) is confined to a lower-dimensional subspace within the dimension of \(K\). This implies a channel rank deficiency, meaning that the correlation between antennas is high and the actual spatial degrees of freedom are smaller than \(K\), which leads directly to ill-conditioning in the inverse matrix calculation.

Properties Practical Significance
Hermitian Neatly diagonalizable with real eigenvalues + orthogonal eigenvectors
PSD “Variance cannot be negative” is the minimum guarantee of physical validity
PD Inverse matrix exists → whitening, MMSE filter, capacity formula. If broken, rank insufficient

22 Scalar function of probability vector — Maximum value (Maximum)

22.1 Settings

For the probability vector \(\mathbf{X}=(X_1,\ldots,X_L)^T\), we are interested in the following random variable:

\[\max\mathbf{X} = \max\{X_1,\ldots,X_L\}\]

for threshold value \(\mu\)

\[\Pr[\max\mathbf{X}<\mu] = P_{\mathbf{X}}(\mu,\ldots,\mu)\]

Idea: The event \(\max\mathbf{X}<\mu\) occurs only when all components are \(X_i<\mu\).

22.2 In the case of independent components

If \(X_1,\ldots,X_L\) is independent

\[\Pr[\max\mathbf{X}<\mu] = \prod_{i=1}^{L}P_{X_i}(\mu)\]

Differentiate to obtain the PDF (product rule of differentiation):

\[p_{\max\mathbf{X}}(\mu) = \sum_{j=1}^{L}p_{X_j}(\mu)\prod_{i\neq j}P_{X_i}(\mu)\]

Example (\(L=2\)):

\[p_{\max\mathbf{X}}(\mu) = \frac{d}{d\mu}\big[P_{X_1}(\mu)P_{X_2}(\mu)\big] = p_{X_1}(\mu)P_{X_2}(\mu) + p_{X_2}(\mu)P_{X_1}(\mu)\]

22.3 iid case

If \(X_1,\ldots,X_L\) is an iid with common CDF \(P_X(\mu)\) and PDF \(p_X(\mu)\)

\[\Pr[\max\mathbf{X}<\mu] = \big(P_X(\mu)\big)^{L}\]

If you differentiate

\[\boxed{p_{\max\mathbf{X}}(\mu) = L\,p_X(\mu)\big(P_X(\mu)\big)^{L-1}}\]

Interpretation: As \(L\) increases, the density of maximum values is “pulled to the right” — because as the sample size increases, the probability of a larger maximum value increases.

22.4 Point to Note 1 — The Correct Intuitive Conclusion that “the number of samples exceeding \(\mu\) increases”

The observation that “as ‘\(L\) increases, the number of samples exceeding \(\mu\)’ increases” is accurate in itself. However, the conclusion is that the maximum value increases rather than decreases:

There is at least one sample exceeding \(\mu\) \(\Longrightarrow\) Since that sample is a candidate for the maximum value \(\max\mathbf{X}>\mu\)

For the maximum value to decrease, all samples would actually have to stay low, but as the number increases, this becomes increasingly difficult. This is directly reflected in the power structure \(\Pr[\max\mathbf{X}<\mu]=P_X(\mu)^L\).

Check value (\(P_X(\mu)=0.9\) fixed):

\(L\) \(\Pr[\max\mathbf{X}<\mu]=0.9^L\)
\(1\) \(0.9\)
\(10\) \(\approx 0.349\)
\(100\) \(\approx 0.0000266\)

Verify by expected value (\(X\sim\text{Uniform}(0,1)\)): Since \(P_X(\mu)=\mu\) and \(p_X(\mu)=1\), \(p_{\max\mathbf{X}}(\mu)=L\mu^{L-1}\), therefore

\[\mathbb{E}[\max\mathbf{X}] = \int_0^1 \mu\cdot L\mu^{L-1}d\mu = L\int_0^1\mu^L d\mu = \frac{L}{L+1}\]

\(L\) \(\mathbb{E}[\max\mathbf{X}]\)
\(1\) \(0.5\)
\(10\) \(0.909\)
\(100\) \(0.990\)
\(L\to\infty\) \(\to 1\) (Upper bound of distribution)

To use a die as an analogy, the expected value of rolling one die is 3.5, but if you roll 100 dice and look at the maximum value among them, it is almost always close to 6.

22.5 Point to Note 2 — The misconception that “if \(L\) increases, the PDF increases in all \(\mu\)”

First, the terms must be distinguished. “The probability that all components are smaller than \(\mu\)” refers to the CDF, not the PDF:

\[P_{\max\mathbf{X}}(\mu) = \Pr[\max\mathbf{X}<\mu] = \big(P_X(\mu)\big)^L \quad \text{← CDF}\]

PDF \(p_{\max\mathbf{X}}(\mu)\) is the “probability density that the maximum value is within \([\mu,\mu+d\mu]\)”.

And “As \(L\) increases, the PDF always increases” is also not true. Let’s verify this directly with \(p_{\max\mathbf{X}}(\mu)=L\mu^{L-1}\).

\(\mu=0.5\) Fixed:

\(L\) \(L\cdot(0.5)^{L-1}\)
\(1\) \(1\)
\(3\) \(0.75\)
\(10\) \(\approx 0.0195\)
\(50\) \(\approx 4.4\times10^{-14}\)

As \(L\) increases, it actually decreases sharply to 0.

\(\mu=0.99\) Fixed:

\(L\) \(L\cdot(0.99)^{L-1}\)
\(1\) \(1\)
\(10\) \(\approx 9.14\)
\(50\) \(\approx 30.3\)
\(100\) \(\approx 36.6\)
\(200\) \(\approx 26.8\) (decrease again)

Why Is There Such a Difference — The Contest of Two Forces:

\[p_{\max\mathbf{X}}(\mu)=\underbrace{L}_{\text{선형 증가}}\cdot\underbrace{\mu^{L-1}}_{\text{지수적 감소}\;(\mu<1)}\]

As \(\mu\) approaches the upper bound of the distribution, the decrease in \(\mu^{L-1}\) is slow, causing the increase in \(L\) to overwhelm it and the PDF to become larger; conversely, as \(\mu^{L-1}\) turns off much faster, the PDF becomes smaller.

Therefore, the exact meaning of “pushed to the right” is:

\[L\uparrow \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \begin{cases}\mu \text{가 상한 근처} & \Rightarrow \text{PDF 증가}\\[2pt] \mu \text{가 중간/낮은 영역} & \Rightarrow \text{PDF 감소 (0으로 수렴)}\end{cases}\]

The total integral is always preserved as 1, and a redistribution occurs in which it exits from the \(\mu\) region of low probability mass and flocks to the \(\mu\) region of high probability mass. If you read this as “the PDF increases in all \(\mu\),” you immediately encounter a contradiction in the \(\mu=0.5\) counterexample.
