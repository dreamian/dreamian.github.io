1 Introduction

1.1 Two questions addressed by stochastic signal theory

When dealing only with deterministic signals \(\cos t\), \(\sin t\), it was sufficient to analyze the signal itself. However, actual observations are usually

\[ y(t) = s(t) + n(t) \]

It is in the form , and here two questions arise.

  1. Noise \(n(t)\) is a stochastic process. What is a stochastic process, what characteristics (mean, autocorrelation, distribution) describe it, and what tools are used to analyze it?
  2. The signal \(s(t)\) itself can be probabilistic. For example, \(\cos(\omega t + \Theta)\), whose phase follows a uniform distribution, is not a deterministic function but a stochastic process. To estimate parameters based solely on observations \(y(t)\), or to detect whether \(y(t)\) came from the hypothesis \(H_0\) or \(H_1\), the basis of stochastic processes is required.

This document covers the Poisson process among its foundations.

1.2 Sampling Theorem Review (Brief)

When a baseband spectrum with a bandwidth of \(W\) is sampled at a sampling frequency \(f_s\), if \(f_s > 2W\), the duplicated spectra do not overlap, so the original baseband spectrum can be restored using a low-pass filter. Otherwise, aliasing occurs where the duplicated spectra overlap.

The reason is that sampling is a multiplication with an impulse train in the time domain, and since the Fourier transform of an impulse train is an impulse train again, in the frequency domain it becomes a convolution between the original spectrum and the impulse train, i.e., a spectral replication at the \(f_s\) interval.

1.3 Two Perspectives on the Poisson Process

Perspective Question Random Variable Range of Values Description of Distribution
Count statistics How many events occurred in the interval \((t_1,t_2]\)? \(N_{t_1,t_2}\) \(\{0,1,2,\dots\}\) PMF (Probability Mass Function)
Time of occurrence When did the event happen? \(\mathbf W=(W_1,\dots,W_n)\) Ordered real numbers PDF (Probability density function)

If the counting process \(N_t\) is plotted over time, it becomes a staircase that starts at 0 and rises one step at a time for each event. The arrival time \(W_1<W_2<\cdots\) is the point at which each step rises. The only difference is whether the same realization is read as “height” or “corner position,” but the two perspectives describe the same object.

ImportantDo not mix PMF and PDF

Since \(N_{t_1,t_2}\) is an integer value, \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]\) is the probability itself. On the other hand, since \(W_k\) is a continuous real value, it is \(\Pr[W_1=w_1]=0\), and the meaningful quantity is the density \(p_{\mathbf W}(\mathbf w)\). Probability is obtained by multiplying the density by the infinitesimal volume: \(\Pr[\cdots]\approx p_{\mathbf W}(\mathbf w)\,\delta_1\cdots\delta_n\). The sample function density \(p_{N,\mathbf W}(n;\mathbf w)\), which will appear later, is a mixed form that is the PMF for \(n\) and the PDF for \(\mathbf w\).

The first part (Chapters 2–5) deals with count statistics, and the second part (Chapters 6–9) deals with time of occurrence.

2 Definition of Poisson Process and Count Distribution

2.1 Infinitesimal interval axiom

The Poisson coefficient process \(\{N_t : t\ge 0\}\) with intensity (rate) \(\lambda_0>0\) is defined by the following three properties.

Axiom 1 (Independent Increments). Counts of non-overlapping intervals are independent of each other. \[ N_{a,b} \perp\!\!\!\perp N_{c,d}, \qquad (a,b]\cap(c,d]=\varnothing. \]

Axiom 2 (Strength). The probability that exactly 1 event occurs in an interval of length \(h\) is \[ \Pr[N_{t,t+h}=1] = \lambda_0 h + o(h). \]

Axiom 3 (Scarcity, Orderliness). The probability of two or more occurrences in the same interval is \[ \Pr[N_{t,t+h}\ge 2] = o(h). \]

Here, \(o(h)\) represents the term satisfying \(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{o(h)}{h}=0\), i.e., the remainder that approaches zero faster than \(h\). Automatically from Axioms 2 and 3. \[ \Pr[N_{t,t+h}=0] = 1-\lambda_0 h + o(h). \]

Intuition. In very short periods, there is at most one event, and the probability is linearly proportional to the length of time.

2.2 Meaning of Homogeneous

Homogeneous means that the intensity is homogeneous with respect to time, that is, \(\lambda(t)\equiv\lambda_0\), meaning that all points on the time axis are equal.

  • HPP (Homogeneous Poisson Process): \(\lambda(t)\equiv\lambda_0\) (constant)
  • NHPP (Non-Homogeneous Poisson Process): \(\lambda(t)\) is a function of time

“Homogeneity” means that statistical properties do not change even when the time axis is shifted, which corresponds to the stationary increments property that will be discussed later. This is in the same context as a homogeneous Markov chain, which refers to a chain where “transition probabilities do not depend on time.”

2.3 Derivation of PMF: Limit of Binomial Distribution

Let’s divide \([0,t]\) into Z\(\Delta t = t/m\) pieces of length \(m\). By the axioms, the count \(X_k\) of each piece is (approximately) 0 or 1 and \[ \Pr[X_k=1]\approx \frac{\lambda_0 t}{m}, \qquad \Pr[X_k=0]\approx 1-\frac{\lambda_0 t}{m}, \] The pieces are independent of each other. Therefore, \(N_t\approx\sum_k X_k \sim \text{Binomial}(m,\ p=\lambda_0 t/m)\) and \[ \Pr[N_t=n] = \underbrace{\frac{m(m-1)\cdots(m-n+1)}{n!}}_{(\mathrm{i})} \underbrace{\left(\frac{\lambda_0 t}{m}\right)^n}_{(\mathrm{ii})} \underbrace{\left(1-\frac{\lambda_0 t}{m}\right)^{m}}_{(\mathrm{iii})} \underbrace{\left(1-\frac{\lambda_0 t}{m}\right)^{-n}}_{(\mathrm{iv})}. \]

If you fix \(n\) and send it to \(m\to\infty\):

  • (i)·(ii) \(= \dfrac{(\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!}\cdot\dfrac{m}{m}\cdot\dfrac{m-1}{m}\cdots\dfrac{m-n+1}{m} \;\to\; \dfrac{(\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!}\)
    1. \(\to e^{-\lambda_0 t}\) (Standard limit \((1+x/m)^m\to e^x\))
    1. \(\to 1\)

\[ \boxed{\Pr[N_t=n] = \frac{(\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda_0 t}, \qquad n=0,1,2,\dots} \]

This is the law of rare events where “number of trials \(m\to\infty\), probability of success \(p\to 0\), mean \(mp=\lambda_0 t\) fixed”.

Reason why occurrences of 2 or more can be ignored. The sum of the probabilities of “2 or more in any piece” across the entire interval is \[ \sum_{k=1}^m \Pr[X_k\ge 2] = m\cdot o(\Delta t) = t\cdot\frac{o(\Delta t)}{\Delta t}\;\to\;0, \] Since , the approximation “each piece is 0 or 1” becomes accurate in the limit \(m\to\infty\).

2.4 Section Count \(N_{t_1,t_2}\)

\[ N_{t_1,t_2} := N_{t_2}-N_{t_1}, \qquad 0\le t_1<t_2. \]

HPP. Since it is \(N_{t_1,t_2}\overset{d}{=}N_{t_2-t_1}\) due to normal increment, you just need to change \(t\) to \(t_2-t_1\). \[ \Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n] = \frac{\big(\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\big)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)}. \]

Consistency check. In \(N_{t_2}=N_{t_1}+N_{t_1,t_2}\), the two terms are independent, and by Poisson’s additivity, \(\lambda_0 t_1+\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)=\lambda_0 t_2\) becomes exactly the same as \(N_{t_2}\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_0 t_2)\).

Insert cumulative intensity in place of NHPP. \(\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\). \[ \Lambda_{t_1}^{t_2} := \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\lambda(u)\,du, \qquad \Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n] = \frac{\big(\Lambda_{t_1}^{t_2}\big)^n}{n!}e^{-\Lambda_{t_1}^{t_2}}. \]

Why integration? If the interval is divided into infinitesimal pieces \(du\), each piece is an independent Bernoulli \((\lambda(u_k)\Delta u)\), and the average of the sum is \(\sum_k\lambda(u_k)\Delta u\to\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\lambda(u)\,du\). If it is \(\lambda\equiv\lambda_0\), it returns to \(\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\), so HPP is a special case. Also, if we set it to \(\Lambda(t):=\int_0^t\lambda(u)\,du\), it becomes \(\Lambda_{t_1}^{t_2}=\Lambda(t_2)-\Lambda(t_1)\), which has the same structure as the increment of the count \(N_{t_2}-N_{t_1}\).

3 Mean, Variance, Generating Function

3.1 Average: Calculated directly as a series

\[ \mathbb E[N_t] = \sum_{n=0}^\infty n\frac{(\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda_0 t}. \]

  1. The \(n=0\) term is 0 because of the preceding \(n\), so the sum starts from \(n=1\).
  2. Take \(\lambda_0 t\) out and move the exponent to \(m=n-1\). \[ \mathbb E[N_t] = \lambda_0 t\, e^{-\lambda_0 t}\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(\lambda_0 t)^m}{m!}. \]
  3. If you use the exponential series \(\sum_m x^m/m! = e^x\) \[ \boxed{\mathbb E[N_t]=\lambda_0 t}, \qquad \mathbb E[N_{t_1,t_2}] = \lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\ \ (\text{NHPP: } \Lambda_{t_1}^{t_2}). \]

Interpretation. Expected count = Intensity × Time. The larger \(\lambda_0\) is, and the longer the observation time, the more events are observed.

3.2 Probability Generating Function (PGF)

3.2.1 Definition and Three Properties

For a non-negative integer random variable \(X\) \[ G_X(z) := \mathbb E[z^X] = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \Pr[X=n]\,z^n. \] is a power series with probability masses as coefficients and always converges at \(|z|\le 1\).

(1) Contains the entire distribution (restores coefficients). \[ \Pr[X=n] = \frac{G_X^{(n)}(0)}{n!}. \] When \(z^k\) is differentiated \(n\) times, the term \(k<n\) becomes 0, the term \(k=n\) becomes a constant \(n!\), and the term \(k>n\) becomes \(\frac{k!}{(k-n)!}z^{k-n}\). If \(z=0\) is substituted here, the term \(k>n\) also cancels out as \(0^{k-n}=0\), leaving only \(n!\Pr[X=n]\). This is equivalent to comparing the coefficients with the Maclaurin expansion \(f(z)=\sum_n \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}z^n\). In other words, differentiation + substitution of \(z=0\) extracts the compressed probability values one by one.

(2) Factorial moment. If you input \(z=1\), the moment is obtained. \[ G_X'(1)=\mathbb E[X], \qquad G_X''(1)=\mathbb E[X(X-1)]. \] \(\mathbb E[X^2]=\mathbb E[X(X-1)]+\mathbb E[X]\) Therefore \[ \mathrm{Var}[X] = G_X''(1)+G_X'(1)-\big(G_X'(1)\big)^2. \] In Poisson, \(n(n-1)\) cancels out cleanly with \(n!\) more than \(n^2\), making calculations easier.

(3) The PGF of an independent sum is the product of PGFs. If \(X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\) \[ G_{X+Y}(z)=\mathbb E[z^Xz^Y]=\mathbb E[z^X]\,\mathbb E[z^Y]=G_X(z)G_Y(z). \]

3.2.2 PGF of the Poisson process

Method A: From the definition. \[ G_{N_t}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda_0 t}z^n = e^{-\lambda_0 t}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(\lambda_0 tz)^n}{n!} = e^{-\lambda_0 t}e^{\lambda_0 t z} = \exp\big(\lambda_0 t(z-1)\big). \]

Method B: From the structure of the process. If \([0,t]\) is divided into \(m\) pieces, it becomes \(N_t\approx\sum_{k=1}^m X_k\), and by independent increments, \(X_k\) are independent. Using property (3), \[ G_{N_t}(z)\approx\prod_{k=1}^m G_{X_k}(z). \] Since each \(X_k\) is Bernoulli, if you just insert the two terms \(n=0,1\) into the PGF definition \[ G_{X_k}(z)=\Pr[X_k{=}0]\,z^0+\Pr[X_k{=}1]\,z^1=(1-\lambda_0\Delta t)+\lambda_0\Delta t\,z = 1+\lambda_0\Delta t(z-1). \] Since the PGF of all fragments is the same due to normal increments \[ G_{N_t}(z)\approx\left[1+\frac{\lambda_0 t(z-1)}{m}\right]^m \xrightarrow{m\to\infty}\exp\big(\lambda_0 t(z-1)\big). \]

Rearranging to the final form \(1+\lambda_0\Delta t(z-1)\) is an algebraic transformation for the direct application of \((1+x/m)^m\to e^x\). Method B shows that the form \((z-1)\) itself is the limit of a binomial trial where “each infinitesimal interval multiplies \(\lambda_0\Delta t\) by \(z\) or \(1\).”

Return to PMF. Since \(G_{N_t}(z)=\sum_n\left[\frac{(\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda_0 t}\right]z^n\), reading the coefficients of \(z^n\) restores the PMF exactly. The fact that the two paths (from the bottom up from the axioms / restoration from PGF) yield the same answer confirms that the PGF captures distribution information without loss.

3.3 Variance

To PGF. \[ G_{N_t}'(z)=\lambda_0 t\,e^{\lambda_0 t(z-1)},\qquad G_{N_t}''(z)=(\lambda_0 t)^2e^{\lambda_0 t(z-1)} \] Since , \(G'(1)=\lambda_0 t\), \(G''(1)=(\lambda_0 t)^2\) and \[ \mathrm{Var}[N_t] = (\lambda_0 t)^2+\lambda_0 t-(\lambda_0 t)^2 = \lambda_0 t. \] Since the exponential function maintains its form when differentiated, the factorial moment of degree \(k\) is \((\lambda_0 t)^k\) regardless of how many times it is differentiated.

Directly as a series. If split into \(n^2=n(n-1)+n\) \[ \mathbb E[N_t^2]=e^{-\lambda_0 t}\left[\sum_{n=0}^\infty n(n-1)\frac{(\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!}+\sum_{n=0}^\infty n\frac{(\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!}\right]. \] Since the term \(n(n-1)\) is 0 in \(n=0,1\), the sum starts from \(n=2\), \((\lambda_0 t)^2\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{(\lambda_0 t)^{n-2}}{(n-2)!}=(\lambda_0 t)^2e^{\lambda_0 t}\), and the term \(n\) becomes \(\lambda_0 t\,e^{\lambda_0 t}\) in the same way, \[ \mathbb E[N_t^2]=(\lambda_0 t)^2+\lambda_0 t, \qquad \boxed{\mathrm{Var}[N_t]=\lambda_0 t.} \]

Interval and NHPP. \[ \mathbb E[N_{t_1,t_2}]=\mathrm{Var}[N_{t_1,t_2}]=\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\quad(\text{NHPP: }\Lambda_{t_1}^{t_2}). \] It is immediately obtained solely from the fact that \(N_{t_1,t_2}\) follows Poisson. Since the PGF of the Poisson \((\mu)\) is \(\exp(\mu(z-1))\), it is merely a matter of substituting \(t\) with \(t_2-t_1\).

3.4 Meaning of Mean = Variance

Index of dispersion (VMR). \[ \mathrm{VMR}:=\frac{\mathrm{Var}[N_{t_1,t_2}]}{\mathbb E[N_{t_1,t_2}]}=1. \] This is the baseline for complete randomness. From the observed count data

  • VMR \(>1\) (overdispersion): Tendency of events to cluster (burst traffic, contagion effect)
  • VMR \(<1\) (underdispersion): The tendency for events to repel each other (regular intervals like a reservation system)

Since , the Poisson process acts as a null model to determine how clustered the data is.

Intuition. As the intensity increases, not only does the mean increase, but the range of values the count takes also widens (e.g., from \(0\sim10\) to \(0\sim100\)). The same applies when the observation interval is extended. Therefore, \(\lambda_0\) determines the mean and variance simultaneously. This property remains the same in NHPP, with only the common value changing to \(\Lambda_{t_1}^{t_2}\).

3.5 characteristic function

\[ \Phi_{N_t}(j\omega):=\mathbb E[e^{j\omega N_t}] =e^{-\lambda_0 t}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(e^{j\omega}\lambda_0 t)^n}{n!} =\exp\big(\lambda_0 t(e^{j\omega}-1)\big). \] MGF is also \(M_{N_t}(s)=\exp(\lambda_0 t(e^s-1))\) in the same way. This is equivalent to inserting \(z=e^{j\omega}\) (or \(z=e^s\)) into PGF. In intervals and NHPP \[ \Phi_{N_{t_1,t_2}}(j\omega)=\exp\big(\Lambda_{t_1}^{t_2}(e^{j\omega}-1)\big). \]

4 Secondary Statistics: Autocorrelation and Covariance

4.1 Non-overlapping intervals: Product decomposition into independent increments

When \(t_2>t_1\), \([0,t_1]\) and \((t_1,t_2]\) do not overlap, so \[ N_{0,t_1}\perp\!\!\!\perp N_{t_1,t_2},\qquad \mathbb E[N_{0,t_1}N_{t_1,t_2}]=\mathbb E[N_{0,t_1}]\,\mathbb E[N_{t_1,t_2}]=\lambda_0^2t_1(t_2-t_1). \]

ImportantThe basis of independence is the “independent increment”.

The reason \(N_{0,t_1}\) and \(N_{t_1,t_2}\) are independent is that their intervals do not overlap (independent increments), not because the process is stationary. This independence holds true even in NHPPs where stationary increments do not apply. The intuition that “the past does not influence the future” stems from independent increments, not stationarity.

4.2 Overlapping sections: Decomposed into disjoint fragments

\(N_{t_1}\) and \(N_{t_2}\) (\(t_1<t_2\)) are not independent because they share the event of \([0,t_1]\). If \(N_{t_1}\) is large, \(N_{t_2}\), which includes it, also becomes large, so they have a positive correlation. Therefore, it is \(\mathbb E[N_{t_1}N_{t_2}]\ne\mathbb E[N_{t_1}]\mathbb E[N_{t_2}]\).

Processing method. Decompose overlapping variables into “common part + disjoint remainder”, and apply independence only to the disjoint pieces. \[ N_{t_2}=N_{t_1}+N_{t_1,t_2}\ \Longrightarrow\ R_N(t_1,t_2)=\mathbb E[N_{t_1}N_{t_2}]=\mathbb E[N_{t_1}^2]+\mathbb E[N_{t_1}N_{t_1,t_2}]. \] \(\mathbb E[N_{t_1}^2]=\mathrm{Var}[N_{t_1}]+(\mathbb E[N_{t_1}])^2=\lambda_0 t_1+(\lambda_0 t_1)^2\) Therefore \[ R_N(t_1,t_2)=\lambda_0t_1+(\lambda_0t_1)^2+\lambda_0^2t_1(t_2-t_1)=\lambda_0 t_1+\lambda_0^2t_1t_2\qquad(t_2\ge t_1). \] Since this decomposition is only possible when it is \(t_2\ge t_1\), if it is \(t_1>t_2\), the roles are swapped to obtain \(\lambda_0 t_2+\lambda_0^2t_1t_2\). Combining the two yields

\[ \boxed{R_N(t_1,t_2)=\underbrace{\lambda_0^2t_1t_2}_{\text{DC part}}+\underbrace{\lambda_0\min(t_1,t_2)}_{\text{incremental part}}} \]

  • DC part \(\lambda_0^2t_1t_2\): It does not change even if \(t_1,t_2\) is swapped. It is the product of the mean functions \(m_N(t_1)m_N(t_2)\).
  • Incremental part \(\lambda_0\min(t_1,t_2)\): Since it varies depending on the magnitude of \(t_1,t_2\), it is generalized and written as \(\min\).
Situation Processing Method
interval is disjoint immediately as independent increments \(\mathbb E[XY]=\mathbb E[X]\mathbb E[Y]\)
Overlapping segments Decompose into common part + disjoint remainder, then apply independence only to disjoint pieces

4.3 Covariance function \(K_N\)

\[ K_N(t,u):=\mathrm{Cov}(N_t,N_u)=\mathbb E\big[(N_t-\mathbb E N_t)(N_u-\mathbb E N_u)\big]=R_N(t,u)-m_N(t)m_N(u). \] Since \(m_N(t)=\lambda_0 t\), the DC part is exactly canceled out. \[ \boxed{K_N(t,u)=\lambda_0\min(t,u).} \]

4.4 Autocorrelation coefficient

The correlation coefficient is the covariance divided by the product of standard deviations (not the product of variances). \[ \rho_N(t_1,t_2)=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(N_{t_1},N_{t_2})}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}N_{t_1}}\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}N_{t_2}}} =\frac{\lambda_0\min(t_1,t_2)}{\sqrt{\lambda_0t_1}\sqrt{\lambda_0t_2}} =\frac{\min(t_1,t_2)}{\sqrt{t_1t_2}}. \] If we denote it as \(t_1<t_2\) and write the molecule as \(t_1=\sqrt{t_1^2}\), \[ \frac{t_1}{\sqrt{t_1t_2}}=\sqrt{\frac{t_1^2}{t_1t_2}}=\sqrt{\frac{t_1}{t_2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\rho_N(t_1,t_2)=\sqrt{\frac{\min(t_1,t_2)}{\max(t_1,t_2)}}.} \]

Confirm. If it is \(t_1=t_2\), then it is \(\rho_N=1\) (perfectly correlated since it is the same point in time). If it is \(t_1\to 0\) or moves away to \(t_2\to\infty\), then it is \(\rho_N\to 0\). Therefore, it is always within the \([0,1]\) range.

5 Arrival Time Statistics

Now, change the question from “How many?” to “When?“. The goal is the joint distribution of the arrival times \(\mathbf W=(W_1,\dots,W_n)\). It can be derived using only count statistics** and independent increments without any new tools.

5.1 Density × Infinitesimal Volume = Probability

For 2D bond density \(f(x,y)\) \[ \Pr\big[X\in[x,x+\delta_1],\,Y\in[y,y+\delta_2]\big]=\int_x^{x+\delta_1}\!\!\int_y^{y+\delta_2}f(\alpha,\beta)\,d\beta\,d\alpha\approx f(x,y)\,\delta_1\delta_2. \]

Here, \(x\) and \(y\) are the two coordinate axes of the domain, and \(f(x,y)\) is the third value (height) lying above them. Therefore, this probability is not the area under the curve, but the volume of a column erected with a height \(f(x,y)\) on a base area \(\delta_1\delta_2\). In a 2D figure where only the horizontal axis \(x\) and the vertical axis \(y\) are drawn, if the height of the curve is represented by the \(y\) axis, the \(y\) axis simultaneously plays the roles of “coordinates” and “function values,” leading to the misunderstanding that it looks like a 1D density.

5.2 Decomposition of small bin probability

Let the observation start points be \(t_1\) and \(t_1\le w_1<w_2<\cdots<w_n\). Event \[ W_1\in[w_1,w_1+\delta_1),\ \dots,\ W_n\in[w_n,w_n+\delta_n) \] has the exact same meaning as the following on the time axis.

  • Exactly 1 occurrence in each small window (bin) \([w_k,w_k+\delta_k)\)
  • Windows gap between previous and \((t_1,w_1),\ (w_1+\delta_1,w_2),\ \dots,\ (w_{n-1}+\delta_{n-1},w_n)\) 0 occurrences

Simply calling \(W_1\) the “first arrival” does not automatically grant the event that “no event occurred before that.” The probability that \((t_1,w_1)\) is empty has an actual value of \(e^{-\Lambda_{t_1}^{w_1}}\) itself and must be included in the product. The same applies to the gap between them. Otherwise, the preceding event would become the “\(k\)th arrival,” making it impossible for \(W_k\) to be in the designated window.

What is described. It describes the joint distribution of the number of events \(N_{t_1,t_2}\) and the time of the events \(\mathbf W(n)\) on the interval \((t_1,t_2]\), that is, “how many occurred and when” all at once.

5.6.1 Induction

Starts from conditional decomposition. \[ p_{N_{t_1,t_2},\mathbf W(n)}(n;\mathbf w)=\Pr\big[N_{t_1,t_2}=n\,\big|\,\mathbf W(n)=\mathbf w\big]\;p_{\mathbf W(n)}(\mathbf w). \]

The second factor is the joint density of the preceding section (integration interval \(t_1\to w_n\)).

First argument. Given \(\mathbf W(n)=\mathbf w\), for the total count to be exactly \(n\), there must be no additional events from \(w_n\) to \(t_2\). \[ \Pr\big[N_{t_1,t_2}=n\,\big|\,\mathbf W(n)=\mathbf w\big] =\Pr\big[N_{w_n,t_2}=0\,\big|\,\mathbf W(n)=\mathbf w\big] =\Pr[N_{w_n,t_2}=0] =\exp\left(-\int_{w_n}^{t_2}\lambda(\alpha)\,d\alpha\right). \] The reason the condition can be removed from the second equality is that the number of events in \((w_n,t_2]\) is independent of the locations of events within \([t_1,w_n]\) (independent increments).

NoteEven if the condition disappears, the dependency on \(\mathbf w\) remains.

The equation above does not mean that “\(\mathbf w\) is a number with the same probability regardless of what it is.” Although the conditional symbol “\(\mid\mathbf W(n)=\mathbf w\)” has disappeared, the right-hand side \(\exp\left(-\int_{w_n}^{t_2}\lambda\right)\) is still a function of \(\mathbf w\) through \(w_n\). In NHPP, the value actually changes depending on whether \(w_n\) is in a high-intensity or low-intensity time period. It is only in the special case of HPP that it becomes a constant independent of \(w_n\).

Combination. If we multiply the two factors and use the interval additivity of the integral \(\int_{t_1}^{w_n}+\int_{w_n}^{t_2}=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\), \[ \boxed{p_{N_{t_1,t_2},\mathbf W(n)}(n;\mathbf w)=\left(\prod_{k=1}^n\lambda(w_k)\right)\exp\left(-\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\lambda(\alpha)\,d\alpha\right).} \]

Key Point. The density of \(\mathbf W(n)\) alone lacked the “survival probability up to \(t_2\),” and the conditional probability \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n\mid\mathbf W(n)=\mathbf w]\) precisely fills that gap \((w_n,t_2]\), merging into a single integral \(\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\). In other words, the survival interval becomes the entire observation interval.

HPP. \[ p_{N_{t_1,t_2},\mathbf W(n)}(n;\mathbf w)=\lambda_0^n\exp\big(-\lambda_0(t_2-t_1)\big), \] It is independent of the specific location of \(\mathbf w\). This leads to the property that “under the condition that \(n\) events occurred, the arrival times are uniformly scattered across the interval.”

5.6.2 Normalization Check and Uniform Order Statistics

Since the sample function density is of the mixed type, whether the total probability becomes 1 is checked by “summing over \(n\) and integrating over \(\mathbf w\)”. Let \(T:=t_2-t_1\) (HPP). \[ \sum_{n=0}^\infty\int_{t_1\le w_1\le\cdots\le w_n\le t_2}\lambda_0^n e^{-\lambda_0T}\,d\mathbf w =\sum_{n=0}^\infty\lambda_0^n e^{-\lambda_0T}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{T^n}{n!}}_{\text{순서화된 단체의 부피}} =e^{-\lambda_0T}e^{\lambda_0T}=1. \] Since the volume \(T^n\) of \([t_1,t_2]^n\) is divided equally in the order of \(n!\), the volume of the region \(w_1\le\cdots\le w_n\) is \(T^n/n!\).

Here, if we integrate only over \(\mathbf w\), we get \(\Pr[N_{t_1,t_2}=n]=\frac{(\lambda_0T)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda_0T}\), i.e., the Poisson PMF reappears. The link between the two perspectives is that integrating and eliminating the “when” in the sample function density leaves only “a few.” Furthermore, the conditional density is \[ p_{\mathbf W(n)\mid N_{t_1,t_2}=n}(\mathbf w)=\frac{\lambda_0^ne^{-\lambda_0T}}{\frac{(\lambda_0T)^n}{n!}e^{-\lambda_0T}}=\frac{n!}{T^n}, \] , which is exactly the same as the density of the iid uniform distribution \(n\) on [\([t_1,t_2]\) sorted in order of size (order statistic)**. In HPP, this means that “if you know only that the number is \(n\), the location is completely uniformly random,” and it serves as the basis for the method of simulating the Poisson process (selecting the number as Poisson and scattering the location uniformly).

6.2 Independent increments and normal increments

This is the same structure as the mean accumulating as \(\lambda_0 t\) in Poisson.

6.6.1 The reason for saying “Almost surely”

Almost surely(as) means “it holds with a probability of 1.” Logically, there may be cases where it does not hold, but the probability of that set is 0.

  • The standard construction of a stochastic process (Kolmogorov’s extension theorem) determines only finite-dimensional distributions. Therefore, in principle, the possibility that the path is discontinuous at a sample point with a probability of 0 cannot be excluded.
  • If we define \(Y\) as \(\tilde Y(1)=Y(1)+7\) and \(\tilde Y(t)=Y(t)\) for the other two finite-dimensional distributions, then \(\tilde Y\) is the same as \(Y\) for all finite-dimensional distributions, but it is discontinuous at \(t=1\).
  • Kolmogorov’s Continuity Theorem guarantees that if the condition for moments of the increment (e.g., \(\mathbb E|Y(t+h)-Y(t)|^4=3h^2\)) is satisfied, a continuous version exists. By convention, this continuous version is used when referring to a Wiener process.

In other words, “almost surely” does not mean that discontinuities occur meaningfully, but is a convention that honestly indicates the zero-probability loophole left by the strict definition. This is also connected to the reason why white noise \(X=dY/dt\) is defined only as a generalized stochastic process, not as an ordinary function.

6.7 A picture of integrating white noise

Gaussian white noise \(X(t)\) takes a value near the average of 0 at every moment. It is usually a small value, occasionally spikes significantly in the positive and occasionally spikes significantly in the negative. The values at different moments are independent of each other. \(Y(t)=\int_0^tX\) is the accumulation of the area under this erratic signal. Since positive and negative areas alternately accumulate, \(Y(t)\) wanders around 0 (\(\mathbb E[Y(t)]=0\)), and the spread increases as the number of accumulated terms increases (\(\mathrm{Var}[Y(t)]=t\)).

6.8 Wiener process as the limit of a random walk

The reason the Wiener process is called a random walk is that it is the limit of a one-dimensional walk where “you move one step to the left or right by flipping a coin at every moment.”

Discrete walk. Takes an independent step \(\xi_k\) at time intervals \(\Delta\). \[ \xi_k=\begin{cases}+\sqrt\Delta & \text{확률 }1/2\\ -\sqrt\Delta & \text{확률 }1/2\end{cases},\qquad S_\Delta(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor t/\Delta\rfloor}\xi_k . \]

Mean and Variance. \(\mathbb E[\xi_k]=0\), \(\mathrm{Var}[\xi_k]=\Delta\) and since the steps are independent \[ \mathbb E[S_\Delta(t)]=0,\qquad \mathrm{Var}[S_\Delta(t)]=\left\lfloor\frac t\Delta\right\rfloor\Delta\;\xrightarrow{\Delta\to0}\;t. \] The reason for setting the step size to \(\sqrt\Delta\) instead of \(\Delta\) is revealed here. Since the number of steps is \(t/\Delta\), for the variance to converge to a finite value \(t\), the variance of one step must be \(\Delta\), that is, the step size must be \(\sqrt\Delta\). If the step size is set to \(\Delta\), the dispersion becomes \(t\Delta\to0\) and walking stops, and if it is set to \(1\), the dispersion becomes \(t/\Delta\to\infty\) and runs wild.

Properties of Increment.

  • Increments in non-overlapping time intervals are the sum of distinct steps, so they are independent (independent increments).
  • Since the increment of an interval of length \(h\) is the sum of \(h/\Delta\) steps, the distribution is independent of location (\(\text{정상 증분}\)), and by the central limit theorem, it converges from \(\Delta\to0\) to \(\mathcal N(0,h)\).
  • \(t<u\) then \(\mathrm{Cov}(S_\Delta(t),S_\Delta(u))=\mathrm{Var}(S_\Delta(t))\to t=\min(t,u)\).

That is, whatever the distribution of steps (whether coin or Gaussian) is, as long as the mean is 0 and the variance is finite, a Wiener process with a mean of 0, a covariance \(\min(t,u)\), and Gaussian independent increments emerges at the limit (Donsker’s invariant principle). \(\xi_k\) divided by \(\Delta\), \(\xi_k/\Delta\), is a sequence of mutually unrelated values with a variance of \(1/\Delta\to\infty\), which is precisely the discrete approximation of white noise. “Integral of white noise = Wiener process” and “limit of random walk = Wiener process” are two expressions of the same fact.

The absence of drift. Certain realizations may accidentally veer to one side (some paths stay in the negative region for a long time), but since the left and right are symmetrical, statistically they center at 0.

7.1 The count sequence is iid

Divide \((t_1,t_2]\) into bins of width \(\Delta\) \[ X_k:=N_{(k-1)\Delta,\,k\Delta} \] If we define , the continuous-time counting process is changed into a discrete sequence of “how many arrived every \(\Delta\)”.

  • Identically distributed: All bin lengths are \(\Delta\) → Normal increment (HPP only)

The intuition of iid. It is “rolling the same die independently every time”. The die (distribution) is the same every time, and the previous result has no influence on the next result. Here, a “Poisson die with intensity \(\lambda_0\Delta\)” is rolled for every bin.

7.2 Ensemble average and time average

  • Ensemble Mean \(\mathbb E[X[n]]\): The average at a specific point in time after infinitely reproducing the same stochastic process. Theoretically neat, but impossible to calculate in reality.
  • Hour Average \(\hat\mu_X(N)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^NX[n]\): The average of a realization observed over a long period. It is an actual tangible amount.

Mean-ergodic is that the time average converges to the ensemble average. Here, it is defined as mean square (MSE) convergence. \[ \mathbb E\big[(\hat\mu_X(N)-\mathbb E[X])^2\big]\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0. \]

7.2.1 Intuition

  1. Time replaces the ensemble. For averaging 1,000 dice thrown simultaneously and averaging 1 die thrown 1,000 times to reach the same answer (3.5), each trial must be “reset” so that the past does not influence the future. That condition is iid.
  2. Case of breakage. \(X[n]=A\) (If the random variable drawn once is fixed forever), it remains \(\hat\mu_X(N)=A\) forever. This is akin to “copying the first roll of a die 1,000 times,” so no matter how long the observation is made, no new information is added, and one becomes trapped in the initial chance. To be ergodic, the process must visit a sufficiently diverse range of states over time.
  3. Forgetting. Independent increments mean that each bin completely “forgets” the previous bin and starts anew. Then, long observations are effectively equivalent to continuously re-running different independent experiments. Conversely, a process with long memory may not deviate from the color of a specific trajectory, and thus ergodicity may be broken.

7.3 Bias-Variance Decomposition

**This is an identity that holds for an arbitrary estimator, \(\hat\mu\), and a constant, \(\theta\). If we add and subtract \(\mathbb E[\hat\mu]\) between \(\hat\mu-\theta\) \[ \hat\mu-\theta=\big(\hat\mu-\mathbb E[\hat\mu]\big)+\underbrace{\big(\mathbb E[\hat\mu]-\theta\big)}_{\text{Bias (상수)}}. \] If we square and take the expected value, the cross term cancels out as \(2\,\text{Bias}\cdot\mathbb E[\hat\mu-\mathbb E\hat\mu]=0\), so \[ \boxed{\text{MSE}=\mathbb E[(\hat\mu-\theta)^2]=\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu)+\text{Bias}^2.} \]

Apply to iid sample mean.

  • Bias = 0. Due to the linearity of the expectation, \(\mathbb E\left[\frac1N\sum X[n]\right]=\frac1N\sum\mathbb E[X[n]]\), and since it is a uniform distribution, this sum is simplified to \(N\,\mathbb E[X]\), resulting in \(\mathbb E[\hat\mu]=\mathbb E[X]\).
  • Variance \(=\sigma^2/N\). Since they are independent, there is no cross-covariance term \(\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum X[n]\right)=\sum\mathrm{Var}(X[n])=N\sigma^2\), therefore \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat\mu)=\sigma^2/N\).

\[ \text{MSE}(N)=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0. \]

7.5 “If it ultimately has to be \(N\to\infty\), what is the point?”

  1. The key is not the limit itself, but the convergence speed. Behind the sentence “\(N\to\infty\) at 0” lies the quantitative formula for the finite \(N\) in \(\text{MSE}(N)=\sigma^2/N\). If it is \(N=1000\), the standard deviation of the estimation error is exactly \(\sigma/\sqrt{1000}\).
  2. The required observation time can be backcalculated. For target accuracy \(\epsilon\), \(\sigma^2/N\le\epsilon^2\Rightarrow N\ge\sigma^2/\epsilon^2\). If it is Poisson intensity estimation, \(K\Delta\ge\lambda_0/\epsilon^2\).
  3. Serves as the basis for the confidence interval. If the bias is 0 and the variance is known, it can be combined with the central limit theorem to present the “estimate \(\pm\) margin of error”.
  4. Convergence itself is not trivial. There are processes where the time average does not reach the true value, as in the example \(X[n]=A\) above. Ergodicity is a property that requires proof, and in Poisson processes, it is guaranteed thanks to independent and stationary increments. That is, the theoretical strength \(\lambda_0\) can be reliably estimated with only a single long record of observations.

8 Markov properties of arrival times and arrival intervals

8.1 Conditional density: Ratio of bonding densities

The goal is “Given the arrival times so far, when is the next arrival?” \[ p_{W_n\mid W_{n-1},\dots,W_1}(w_n\mid w_{n-1},\dots,w_1),\qquad w_n\ge w_{n-1}. \] Although there is no direct formula for calculating conditional density, the joint density is already known. If we convert the definition of conditional probability \(\Pr[B\mid A]=\Pr[A,B]/\Pr[A]\) into density (Bayes factorization) \[ p_{W_n\mid W_{n-1},\dots,W_1}=\frac{p_{W_n,W_{n-1},\dots,W_1}}{p_{W_{n-1},\dots,W_1}}. \] The numerator is the bond density of all \(n\), and the denominator is the marginal density obtained by integrating \(W_n\) and eliminating it (\(\int_{w_{n-1}}^\infty dw_n\)). Dividing by the denominator is to normalize by the weight of “conditions that have already occurred” and purely extract only the remaining uncertainty (\(W_n\)).

8.2 Simplify

Substitute the joint density (observation start \(t_1\)). \[ \frac{p_{W_1,\dots,W_n}}{p_{W_1,\dots,W_{n-1}}} =\frac{\prod_{k=1}^n\lambda(w_k)\,\exp\left(-\int_{t_1}^{w_n}\lambda\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda(w_k)\,\exp\left(-\int_{t_1}^{w_{n-1}}\lambda\right)}. \]

  • Multiply part: Only the newly added \(\lambda(w_n)\) remains.
  • Exponent part: Since the survival term differs only in \([w_{n-1},w_n]\), only the integral of the last gap remains.

\[ \boxed{p_{W_n\mid W_{n-1},\dots,W_1}(w_n\mid w_{n-1},\dots,w_1)=\lambda(w_n)\exp\left(-\int_{w_{n-1}}^{w_n}\lambda(\alpha)\,d\alpha\right).} \]

The next arrival depends on the past not the entire past, but only through the previous arrival \(w_{n-1}\). This is the Markov form of the arrival time. In HPP \[ p_{W_n\mid W_{n-1}}(w_n\mid w_{n-1})=\lambda_0\exp\big(-\lambda_0(w_n-w_{n-1})\big),\qquad w_n\ge w_{n-1}. \] (In HPP, it is \(\lambda(w_n)\)Z, not \(\lambda_0\).)

Definition of Markov property. It is a property in which the conditional distribution of a current variable depends not on the entire past, but only on the immediately preceding state. \[ p_{W_n\mid W_{n-1},\dots,W_1}=p_{W_n\mid W_{n-1}}. \] \(w_{n-2},w_{n-3},\dots\) does not appear at all in the right-hand side of the equation. Regardless of how many times the event was observed or how those times were arranged, it can be forgotten; only the time of the immediate arrival needs to be remembered. Surprisingly, this property holds not only in HPPs but also in NHPPs. The Markov processes and Markov chains discussed later are all built upon this structure.

Previous arrival = New time origin. In HPP, the conditional density is \(w_n-w_{n-1}\), that is, a function of only the time elapsed since the previous event. Whenever an event occurs, the clock is reset to zero, and only “how much time has passed” from there is needed. As the elapsed time increases, the density decreases exponentially.

WarningIn NHPP, “elapsed time” alone is not enough

The conditional density of ZZZPH0002705ZZZ NHPP \(\lambda(w_n)\exp\left(-\int_{w_{n-1}}^{w_n}\lambda\right)\) is Markov, but it is not a function of \(w_n-w_{n-1}\) alone. It also requires where the previous arrival was on the time axis (absolute position \(w_{n-1}\)). For example, if it is \(\lambda(t)=t\), the conditional density of \(\tau=w_n-w_{n-1}\) is \[ (w_{n-1}+\tau)\exp\left(-w_{n-1}\tau-\tfrac{\tau^2}{2}\right), \] depends on \(w_{n-1}\). If you have just arrived from a high-intensity time zone, the next interval tends to be shorter. “The past is summarized by the single immediate time” (Markov) is true in NHPP, and “only the time elapsed since the immediate time matters” (Time Origin Reset) is true only in HPP.

NoteLower integral limit of joint density \(t_1\) vs \(w_1\)

There are cases where the survival term of the joint density is written as \(\int_{w_1}^{w_n}\). This formula omits the survival term \(e^{-\Lambda_{t_1}^{w_1}}\) for the interval \((t_1,w_1)\), and corresponds to the conditional density of \((W_2,\dots,W_n)\) conditioned on \(W_1=w_1\). As long as the observation start is \(t_1\), \(\int_{t_1}^{w_n}\) must be included in the joint density itself for the first \(n\) arrival times. However, in the above conditional density, since the numerator and denominator cancel out in common regardless of the lower bound, the Markov form result is the same.

There are cases where it is stated that “NHPP intervals are not homodistributed but are uncorrelated,” but this is generally a false claim. \(\tau_{n-1}\) is a part of \(W_{n-1}=\tau_1+\cdots+\tau_{n-1}\), and the conditional distribution of \(\tau_n\) depends on \(W_{n-1}\). Time-change Theorem. In NHPP, what is white is not wall clock time, but the interval of time measured by cumulative intensity. If we denote it as \(\Lambda(t)=\int_{t_1}^t\lambda\) and set it to \(\sigma_n:=\Lambda(W_n)-\Lambda(W_{n-1})\), from the Markov form \[ \Pr[\sigma_n>s\mid W_1,\dots,W_{n-1}] =\Pr\Big[\text{구간 }\big(w_{n-1},\ \Lambda^{-1}(\Lambda(w_{n-1})+s)\big]\text{에 사건 0개}\Big] =e^{-s}. \] Since this conditional distribution does not depend at all on the past, \(\sigma_n\) is independent of the past and \(\text{Exp}(1)\) for all \(n\). That is, \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\dots\) is iid \(\text{Exp}(1)\), and NHPP is an HPP with intensity 1 stretched and shrunk only on the time axis to \(\Lambda^{-1}\). The wall clock intervals only appear shorter because the cumulative intensity clock runs faster during high-intensity time zones. This fact forms the basis for NHPP simulation (converting to \(\Lambda^{-1}\) after creating HPP arrivals) and model fit testing.

The reason it becomes iid in HPP. If it is \(\lambda\equiv\lambda_0\), then \(w_{n-1}\) is completely canceled out \[ p_{\tau_n}(\tau)=\lambda_0e^{-\lambda_0\tau},\qquad\tau\ge0, \] follows \(\text{Exp}(\lambda_0)\) with all intervals being the same and are independent of each other. This is the same logic as the count sequence \(X_k\) being iid only in HPP.

Check directly. Since \(\tau_n\le\tau\) is equal to “at least 1 event in \((w_{n-1},w_{n-1}+\tau]\)”, \[ \Pr[\tau_n\le\tau]=1-\Pr[N_{w_{n-1},w_{n-1}+\tau}=0]=1-e^{-\lambda_0\tau}, \] Differentiating this gives \(\lambda_0e^{-\lambda_0\tau}\).

\(\lambda_0\) determines the rate of exponential decay. \[ \mathbb E[\tau_n]=\int_0^\infty\tau\,\lambda_0e^{-\lambda_0\tau}\,d\tau=\frac1{\lambda_0},\qquad \mathrm{Var}[\tau_n]=\frac1{\lambda_0^2}. \] If \(\lambda_0\) is large, the density decays quickly, and the next event comes early; if \(\lambda_0\) is small, events are rare, and one waits long for the next event. If an average of \(\lambda_0\) events occur per unit time, it is natural that the average interval is \(1/\lambda_0\). \(\mathbb E[N_t]=\lambda_0t\) on the count side and \(\mathbb E[\tau]=1/\lambda_0\) on the interval side represent the same intensity read from two different perspectives.

8.4 Memoryless

\[ \Pr[\tau>s+t\mid\tau>s]=\frac{e^{-\lambda_0(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda_0 s}}=e^{-\lambda_0 t}=\Pr[\tau>t]. \] The fact that “you have already waited \(s\)” provides no information about “how much longer you have to wait.” The distribution of remaining waiting time is the same as if it had just started anew. This is the form in which the “loss of memory” caused by independent increments is most clearly revealed at the level of arrival interval distribution.

Summary in a physical diagram. When an event occurs, that time becomes a new reference point. As time elapses from there, the density of subsequent events decreases exponentially, and when the next event occurs, the reference point changes again. This behavior naturally occurs in situations where numerous independent entities each infrequently trigger events (e.g., arc generation from many terminals, radioactive decay, photon arrival). This is why the Poisson process is used as a fundamental model in so many fields.

Two penetrating principles.

  1. Independent Increment: Non-overlapping intervals are decomposed into products. Overlapping intervals are split into disjoint pieces and then applied. Autocorrelation, covariance, arrival time density, sample function density, and the variance reduction of ergodicity all originate from this.
  2. Normal Increment: The distribution depends only on the interval length. It holds only in HPP and is the basis for the count sequence and arrival interval being identical. In NHPP, the normal increment is restored when the time is recalculated with the cumulative intensity \(\Lambda\) (HPP with intensity 1).
