Optimization Theory: From Stationary Points to Duality

Random Signal Theory — Stationary Points and Convexity, Wirtinger Calculus, Gradients and Level Sets, Steepest Descent, Hessian and Definiteness, Matrix Calculus, Least Squares and Polynomial Fitting, Lagrange Multipliers and LICQ, Second-Order Conditions, Quadratic Minimization (MVDR/LCMV), Complex Constraints, KKT Conditions, Lagrangian Duality, Minimum-Variance Estimation and the Capon Spectrum

Published

November 23, 2026

Optimization is the language in which most estimators, filters, and beamformers in statistical signal processing are derived. This note builds the theory from the scalar case up to Lagrangian duality, derives every result it uses, and checks each idea on small numerical examples. Common misstatements are flagged in warning boxes, and every section ends in a form that can be applied directly to estimation problems.

Optimization is a way to choose model parameters so an estimator, filter, or beamformer performs as well as possible. The same core ideas appear again and again: describe how the objective changes, identify directions in which movement is allowed, and use curvature to tell a minimum from other stationary points.

This article builds those ideas in stages. We begin with one real variable, move to complex variables and vectors, then derive least-squares and constrained optimization methods. Each formula is paired with the question it answers and, where useful, a small example. The aim is not merely to memorize conditions, but to understand what they say about nearby points and feasible movements.

1 Roadmap and Notation

1.1 Four ideas that carry the whole theory

  1. Near a point, the first-order change is linear. For a small change \(dx\), the objective changes by \(df=\nabla f^Tdx\) to first order. The gradient is the vector that tells us how each input coordinate affects the objective.
  2. An optimum has no allowed downhill direction. If we can move a little in a direction that lowers the objective, we are not at a minimum.
  3. Constraints limit which directions are allowed. At a constrained optimum, the objective may still slope downhill in a forbidden direction. Its gradient is balanced by the normals of the constraint boundaries.
  4. A complex variable contains two real coordinates. Write \(z=x+jy\). For real-valued objectives, Wirtinger derivatives package the \(x\) and \(y\) derivatives into convenient complex expressions. The usual choice for descent is \(\partial f/\partial z^*\).

The rest is careful bookkeeping: dimensions, transposes, conjugates, and signs.

2 Unconstrained Optimization: The Scalar Case

2.1 First- and second-order conditions

Suppose \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) is twice differentiable and we are checking whether \(x^\star\) is a local minimum. Let \(h\) be a small change in \(x\). Taylor’s expansion gives

\[ f(x^\star+h) = f(x^\star)+f'(x^\star)h+\frac12 f''(x^\star)h^2+o(h^2). \]

The terms have a useful interpretation:

  • \(f'(x^\star)h\) is the first-order change: slope times step.
  • \(\tfrac12 f''(x^\star)h^2\) is the leading curvature effect.
  • \(o(h^2)\) is an error that becomes small compared with \(h^2\) as \(h\to0\).

At an interior local minimum the slope must vanish. Otherwise, a sufficiently small step in one of the two directions would decrease \(f\). With the slope gone, positive curvature is the usual sign of a minimum.

Necessary condition. If \(x^\star\) is a local minimum, then

\[ f'(x^\star)=0,\qquad f''(x^\star)\ge0. \]

“Necessary” means every local minimum must satisfy these conditions. It does not mean every point satisfying them is a minimum.

Sufficient condition. If

\[ f'(x^\star)=0,\qquad f''(x^\star)>0, \]

then \(x^\star\) is a strict local minimum. The linear term is zero, and the leading remaining term is positive on both sides of \(x^\star\).

WarningPositive second derivative is sufficient, not necessary

For \(f(x)=x^4\), the origin is a strict global minimum, but \(f''(0)=0\). So the test \(f'=0\) and \(f''>0\) guarantees a minimum when it applies, but it does not find every minimum. A zero second derivative means the second-order test needs more information.

2.2 Stationary points and inflection points

2.2.1 Stationary point

A point \(x^\star\) is stationary if

\[ f'(x^\star)=0. \]

The tangent is horizontal there. A stationary point can be a minimum, a maximum, or neither. For example, \(x^2\) has a minimum at zero, \(-x^2\) has a maximum, and \(x^3\) has neither.

In several variables, the corresponding condition is \(\nabla f(x^\star)=0\): every first-order directional change is zero.

2.2.2 Inflection point

An inflection point is a point where the graph changes concavity: it switches from bending upward to bending downward, or vice versa. When the second derivative exists on both sides, this is indicated by a sign change in \(f''\).

The test is a change of sign, not simply \(f''(x^\star)=0\):

  • \(f(x)=x^4\) has \(f''(0)=0\), but it bends upward on both sides, so zero is not an inflection point.
  • \(f(x)=x^3\) has \(f'(0)=0\) and \(f''(x)=6x\) changes sign. Zero is a stationary inflection point.
  • \(f(x)=\sin x\) changes concavity at zero, but \(f'(0)=1\), so that inflection point is not stationary.
  • \(f(x)=x^{1/3}\) changes concavity at zero even though \(f''(0)\) does not exist.

2.2.2.1 Higher-order test

Suppose \(f'(x^\star)=0\), and the first nonzero derivative after it is the \(n\)-th derivative, with \(n\ge2\). If the function is sufficiently smooth for Taylor’s expansion, then

\[ f(x^\star+h)-f(x^\star) = \frac{f^{(n)}(x^\star)}{n!}h^n+o(h^n). \]

The first nonzero term controls whether nearby values lie above or below \(f(x^\star)\):

  • If \(n\) is even, \(h^n\) is positive on both sides. A positive coefficient gives a local minimum; a negative coefficient gives a local maximum.
  • If \(n\) is odd, \(h^n\) changes sign across zero. The point is neither a local minimum nor a local maximum. With the needed smoothness, the concavity also changes, giving a stationary inflection point.

For example, \(x^4\) has an even-order leading term with a positive coefficient, so zero is a minimum. The function \(x^3\) has an odd-order leading term, so it crosses through rather than turning.

NoteDifferentiability alone may not give this classification

Define \(f(x)=x^2\sin(1/x)\) for \(x\ne0\), and \(f(0)=0\). This function is differentiable at zero with \(f'(0)=0\), yet every neighborhood of zero contains points where \(f\) is positive and points where it is negative. So zero is neither a minimum nor a maximum. Its curvature oscillates rather than changing concavity just once. Higher-order tests need stronger smoothness assumptions than differentiability alone.

2.3 Maximization is minimization of the negative

To maximize \(f\), minimize \(-f\). The locations are the same because

\[ f(x)\le f(x^\star) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad -f(x)\ge -f(x^\star). \]

Consequently,

\[ \arg\max_x f(x)=\arg\min_x[-f(x)]. \]

The value changes sign, but the optimizer does not. This is useful because most optimization formulas are written for minimization.

\((-f)'=0\) \(f'=0\): the stationary points are unchanged
\((-f)''>0\) \(f''<0\)

2.4 Convexity, strict convexity, and uniqueness

2.4.1 Convex and strictly convex functions

A function is convex if the graph between any two points lies at or below the straight chord joining their function values. Algebraically,

\[ f(ax_1+(1-a)x_2) \le af(x_1)+(1-a)f(x_2) \]

for all \(x_1,x_2\) and all \(a\in[0,1]\).

It is strictly convex if the inequality is strict whenever the endpoints are distinct and the point is genuinely between them:

\[ f(ax_1+(1-a)x_2) < af(x_1)+(1-a)f(x_2), \quad x_1\ne x_2,\quad 0<a<1. \]

The restrictions matter. If \(x_1=x_2\), or if \(a=0\) or \(a=1\), the middle point is an endpoint and both sides are equal. A strict inequality cannot hold there. Values \(a\) outside \([0,1]\) produce extrapolation rather than a point on the segment.

For \(x_1<x_2\), let \(x_a=ax_1+(1-a)x_2\). The right side is exactly the height of the chord at \(x_a\). Thus strict convexity says the graph sits strictly below every chord between distinct points. The function \(|x|\) is convex but not strictly convex: on the positive half-line it is a straight line.

2.4.2 Why positive curvature implies strict convexity

If \(f''(x)>0\) everywhere, then the slope increases as we move right. This makes the graph bend upward and ensures it lies strictly below each chord.

One way to see it is to expand \(f\) from an interior point \(x_a\) toward each endpoint. The first-order terms cancel when the two endpoint expansions are weighted by \(a\) and \(1-a\), because \(x_a\) is their weighted average. What remains is a positive weighted sum of curvature terms, so

\[ f(x_a)<a f(x_1)+(1-a)f(x_2). \]

The converse is not required: \(x^4\) is strictly convex even though \(f''(0)=0\). In several variables, a positive-definite Hessian at every point is the analogous positive-curvature condition.

2.4.3 Why convexity matters

If \(f\) is convex and differentiable, every stationary point is a global minimum. Intuitively, a convex graph has no hidden valley below a point where every slope is zero.

Strict convexity gives at most one minimizer. If two distinct points both had the minimum value, strict convexity would make every point between them have a smaller value, a contradiction.

WarningAt most one does not mean exactly one

A strictly convex function need not attain a minimum. For example, \(e^x\) is strictly convex but keeps decreasing as \(x\to-\infty\). A condition such as coercivity—\(f(x)\to\infty\) as \(|x|\to\infty\)—can ensure existence. Conversely, a convex function that is not strictly convex can have many minimizers; \(\max(|x|-1,0)\) is minimized at every \(x\in[-1,1]\).

3 Complex Variables and Wirtinger Calculus

3.1 Why the ordinary complex derivative is too restrictive

Write \(z=x+jy\), so the complex plane is a two-dimensional real plane. The ordinary complex derivative is

\[ f'(z_0)=\lim_{\Delta z\to0} \frac{f(z_0+\Delta z)-f(z_0)}{\Delta z}. \]

The step \(\Delta z\) can approach zero from any angle. The quotient must approach the same number from every direction. That requirement is stronger than ordinary real differentiability, which only asks for a good linear approximation in the two real coordinates.

For example, let \(f(z)=z^*\), the complex conjugate. Take a step \(\Delta z=re^{j\theta}\). Then

\[ \frac{f(z_0+\Delta z)-f(z_0)}{\Delta z} = \frac{\Delta z^*}{\Delta z} =e^{-2j\theta}. \]

Approaching along the real axis gives \(1\); approaching along the imaginary axis gives \(-1\). The limit depends on direction, so \(z^*\) has no ordinary complex derivative at any point.

The same fact appears in the Cauchy–Riemann equations. If \(f=u+jv\), those equations include \(u_x=v_y\). For \(f(z)=z^*=x-jy\), we have \(u=x\) and \(v=-y\), so \(u_x=1\) but \(v_y=-1\). The equation fails.

3.1.1 Geometric picture

Multiplication by a complex number \(a+jb\) acts on the real coordinates as

\[ \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} \longmapsto \begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix}. \]

This is a rotation combined with a uniform scaling. In contrast, conjugation maps \((x,y)\) to \((x,-y)\): it reflects the plane across the real axis. A reflection reverses orientation, so it cannot be represented by multiplication by one complex number.

Complex differentiability requires the local linear approximation to have the multiplication form above. That is why the Cauchy–Riemann equations are required.

3.1.2 Why ordinary complex differentiation does not suit real objectives

An objective that we minimize must be real-valued, because the complex numbers have no natural ordering such as “less than.” If a holomorphic function \(f=u+jv\) is real-valued, then \(v=0\). The Cauchy–Riemann equations force both \(u_x=0\) and \(u_y=0\), so \(f\) is constant on a connected open region. Thus the ordinary complex derivative is not a useful general tool for optimizing real-valued functions.

Consider \(f(z)=|z|^2\). At a nonzero point \(z_0\), its difference quotient is

\[ \frac{|z_0+\Delta z|^2-|z_0|^2}{\Delta z} = z_0^*+z_0\frac{\Delta z^*}{\Delta z}+\Delta z^* \longrightarrow z_0^*+z_0e^{-2j\theta}. \]

The limit depends on direction when \(z_0\ne0\). At zero it does have derivative \(0\), but that single point does not make the function holomorphic on any open neighborhood.

NoteDifferentiable, holomorphic, and analytic
  • Real differentiable at a point: a good first-order linear approximation exists there.
  • Holomorphic: ordinarily complex-differentiable at every point of an open set.
  • Analytic: locally represented by a convergent power series.

For a function of one complex variable, holomorphicity and complex analyticity coincide. For real functions, real differentiability is weaker than being infinitely differentiable, and being infinitely differentiable is weaker than being real analytic. For example, \(e^{-1/x^2}\), defined as \(0\) at \(x=0\), is infinitely differentiable there, but its Taylor series at zero is identically zero and does not represent the function away from zero.

As a map from \(\mathbb R^2\) to \(\mathbb R^2\), conjugation is linear and infinitely differentiable. Wirtinger calculus needs real differentiability; it does not require ordinary complex differentiability.

3.2 Wirtinger derivatives

3.2.1 Deriving them from the differential

Let \(f\) be differentiable as a function of the real variables \(x,y\), and set \(z=x+jy\). Then

\[ dz=dx+j\,dy,\qquad dz^*=dx-j\,dy. \]

Solving for the real-coordinate changes gives

\[ dx=\frac{dz+dz^*}{2}, \qquad dy=\frac{dz-dz^*}{2j}. \]

Start from the ordinary real differential \(df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy\) and substitute:

\[ \begin{aligned} df &=f_x\frac{dz+dz^*}{2} +f_y\frac{dz-dz^*}{2j}\\ &=\frac12(f_x-jf_y)\,dz +\frac12(f_x+jf_y)\,dz^*. \end{aligned} \]

This motivates the definitions

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} =\frac12\left(f_x-jf_y\right),\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial z^*} =\frac12\left(f_x+jf_y\right). } \]

They are the coefficients of \(dz\) and \(dz^*\) in the first-order change. The factors of \(\tfrac12\) come from solving \(z=x+jy\) and \(z^*=x-jy\) for \(x\) and \(y\).

The same formulas can be written as operators:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial z} =\frac12\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x} -j\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right), \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial z^*} =\frac12\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x} +j\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right). \]

3.2.2 What “treat \(z\) and \(z^*\) as independent” means

For algebra, these operators act as if \(z\) and \(z^*\) were separate coordinates:

\[ \frac{\partial z}{\partial z}=1,\quad \frac{\partial z^*}{\partial z}=0,\quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial z^*}=0,\quad \frac{\partial z^*}{\partial z^*}=1. \]

This is a calculation rule, not a claim that an actual complex number and its conjugate can be varied independently. On the ordinary complex plane, \(z^*=\overline z\) always. The Wirtinger operators are defined directly by the real partial derivatives above; complexifying the coordinates is one way to understand why the formal coordinate rule is consistent.

If a function is real analytic in \(x,y\), its local power series can be rewritten as a series in \(z,z^*\). For example, \(|z|^2=x^2+y^2=zz^*\). So polynomials in both \(z\) and \(z^*\) are perfectly compatible with ordinary real differentiability, even though they need not be holomorphic in \(z\) alone.

3.2.3 Example: \(|z|^2\)

Write \(f=|z|^2=zz^*=x^2+y^2\). Treating \(z\) and \(z^*\) formally as separate symbols gives

\[ \frac{\partial (zz^*)}{\partial z^*}=z. \]

Using the real-coordinate definition gives the same result:

\[ \frac12(f_x+jf_y) =\frac12(2x+j\,2y) =x+jy=z. \]

3.2.4 Make conjugate dependence explicit

Before differentiating with respect to \(z^*\), rewrite an expression so all appearances of \(z^*\) are visible:

Compact form Expanded form that exposes \(z^*\)
\(|z|^2\) \(zz^*\)
\(\|y-Hz\|^2\) \((y-Hz)^H(y-Hz)\)
\(\operatorname{Re}\{c^Hz\}\) \(\tfrac12(c^Hz+c^Tz^*)\)

The final row is easy to miss: although \(\operatorname{Re}\{c^Hz\}\) appears to contain only \(z\), it also depends on \(z^*\) through the real-part operation. Its derivative with respect to \(z^*\) is \(\tfrac12c\), not zero.

3.2.5 Two pictures of what the derivatives measure

A real-linear first-order change in the plane has four real degrees of freedom. It can be written uniquely as

\[ \Delta f\approx a\,\Delta z+b\,\Delta z^*, \qquad a=\frac{\partial f}{\partial z},\quad b=\frac{\partial f}{\partial z^*}. \]

The \(a\) term multiplies the input change: it rotates and scales it. The \(b\) term conjugates the input change first, which reflects it, then rotates and scales it. Together these terms describe any real-linear map from the plane to itself.

For an ordinary complex derivative, the local change must be multiplication alone, so \(b=0\). For a real-differentiable function, \(b\) need not vanish.

Now let \(\Delta z=re^{j\theta}\). Dividing the local change by \(\Delta z\) gives

\[ \frac{\Delta f}{\Delta z} \approx a+b\frac{\Delta z^*}{\Delta z} =a+be^{-2j\theta}. \]

As the approach direction \(\theta\) changes, these quotient values trace a circle centered at \(a\) with radius \(|b|\). The circle collapses to one point exactly when \(b=0\), the condition for complex differentiability at that point.

For \(f(z)=z^2\), \(b=0\): the quotient approaches one value. For \(f(z)=z^*\), \(a=0\) and \(b=1\): the quotient moves around the unit circle as the direction changes.

3.3 Stationary points via Wirtinger derivatives

For a real-valued, real-differentiable function, the stationary-point test is exactly the ordinary real test expressed in complex notation:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z^*}=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f_x=f_y=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}=0. \]

Why? Because \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) are real, and

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z^*} =\frac12(f_x+jf_y). \]

A complex number is zero only if both its real and imaginary parts are zero. Also, for real \(f\),

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} =\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z^*}\right)^*. \]

So setting either Wirtinger derivative to zero is enough. Setting both to zero is redundant.

More generally, the differential is

\[ df =\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\,dz +\frac{\partial f}{\partial z^*}\,dz^* =2\operatorname{Re}\left\{ \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z^*}\right)^* dz \right\}. \]

If \(g=\partial f/\partial z^*\) is nonzero, choosing a small step \(dz=\varepsilon g\), with \(\varepsilon>0\), gives \(df=2\varepsilon|g|^2>0\). The opposite step decreases \(f\). Thus every direction has zero first-order change only when \(g=0\).

3.3.1 Why differentiate with respect to \(z^*\)?

For real-valued objectives, \(\partial f/\partial z^*\) packages the real gradient into a complex direction. It is the useful direction for steepest ascent; its negative is the steepest-descent direction under the ordinary Euclidean geometry.

For example, \(f(z)=|z|^2\) has \(\partial f/\partial z^*=z\). The descent update is

\[ z_{k+1}=z_k-\alpha z_k=(1-\alpha)z_k. \]

For \(0<\alpha<2\), the magnitude shrinks toward the minimizer \(z=0\).

Using \(\partial f/\partial z=z^*\) as the descent direction instead can move the wrong way. The update \(z_{k+1}=z_k-\alpha z_k^*\) gives

\[ x_{k+1}=(1-\alpha)x_k,\qquad y_{k+1}=(1+\alpha)y_k. \]

For \(\alpha>0\), the imaginary component grows unless it begins at zero. The conjugated derivative is fine for finding stationary points, but it is not the Euclidean descent direction.

3.3.2 Level sets of \(|z|^2\)

A level set at height \(c\) is the set of inputs where the function has the same value:

\[ \{z:f(z)=c\}. \]

For \(f(z)=|z|^2=x^2+y^2\), the level set \(|z|^2=c\) is a circle of radius \(\sqrt c\) centered at zero. At \(c=0\), it is just the origin.

\(c\) Radius \(\sqrt c\) Example point on the circle
1 1 \(1,\ j,\ -1,\ -j\)
4 2 \(2,\ 2j,\ -2\)
9 3 \(3,\ 3j,\ -3\)

The value grows like the square of the radius: circles of radii 1, 2, and 3 have function values 1, 4, and 9.

The function is rotation-invariant because \(|e^{j\phi}z|^2=|z|^2\). Rotating a point about the origin does not change its distance from the origin, so its contours must be circles.

The Wirtinger derivative is \(g=\partial f/\partial z^*=z\), which points radially outward. A tangent direction to the circle at \(z\) is \(jz\), a 90-degree rotation of the radius. The real inner product of two complex directions \(a,b\) is \(\operatorname{Re}(a^*b)\), so

\[ \operatorname{Re}\{z^*(jz)\} =\operatorname{Re}\{j|z|^2\}=0. \]

Thus the outward gradient is perpendicular to the contour’s tangent.

At the origin, the gradient is zero even though the function increases in every direction. The reason is that the increase is second-order: \(|0+\varepsilon d|^2=\varepsilon^2|d|^2\), so the increase divided by the step length tends to zero.

Why some contours are ellipses. A term in \(|z|^2\) treats real and imaginary directions equally. Terms in \(z^2\) and \(z^{*2}\) can treat them differently. For example,

\[ x^2+4y^2 =\frac52|z|^2-\frac32\operatorname{Re}(z^2). \]

Since \(\operatorname{Re}(z^2)=x^2-y^2\), this expression has elliptical contours rather than circles. The same distinction appears in complex statistics: for a zero-mean complex Gaussian, \(E[|z|^2]\) describes overall power, while \(E[z^2]\) (the pseudo-covariance) records directional imbalance. A nonzero pseudo-covariance indicates a preferred orientation.

4 Gradients, Level Sets, and Steepest Descent

4.1 Gradient of a scalar function of a vector

For a real-valued function of a real vector \(x=(x_1,\dots,x_N)^T\), the gradient is the column of all partial derivatives:

\[ \nabla_x f(x) = \begin{bmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1\\ \vdots\\ \partial f/\partial x_N \end{bmatrix}. \]

For example, if \(f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+3x_2\), then

\[ \nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}2x_1\\3\end{bmatrix}. \]

Each component answers a local question: “If I nudge only coordinate \(x_i\), how quickly does \(f\) change?” The gradient gathers those coordinate slopes into one vector.

Why make it a column? Our variable \(x\) is a column, so the update \(x_{\text{new}}=x-\alpha\nabla f\) subtracts two objects of the same shape. Also, the first-order change is

\[ df=\nabla f^T dx. \]

The shapes are \((1\times N)(N\times1)\), so the result is a scalar, as a change in the scalar \(f\) should be.

The gradient as a field of arrows. For \(f=\tfrac12(x_1^2+x_2^2)\), the gradient is \(\nabla f=[x_1,x_2]^T\). At each point, draw the arrow given by the gradient there:

Point Gradient arrow
\((1,0)\) right
\((0,1)\) upward
\((1,1)\) diagonally up and right
\((-1,1)\) diagonally up and left
\((2,0)\) right, twice as long as at \((1,0)\)

The arrow points toward the fastest local increase. Its length measures how steep that increase is. Here arrows point away from the origin because \(f\) is squared distance from the origin.

4.2 Level sets, tangent vectors, and orthogonality

A level set is the collection of points where \(f\) has one fixed value, say \(c\). In two dimensions it is often a curve, or contour. Imagine a person walking along that contour. Their position is \(r(t)=(x(t),y(t))\), and their velocity \(r'(t)\) points along the contour, so it is tangent to it.

Since the walker stays on the same contour, \(f(x(t),y(t))=c\) at every time. A constant has derivative zero. Applying the ordinary chain rule gives

\[ \frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t)) =f_x\frac{dx}{dt}+f_y\frac{dy}{dt} =\nabla f\cdot r'(t)=0. \]

This says that the change from moving in the \(x\) direction is exactly offset by the change from moving in the \(y\) direction. The gradient is perpendicular to the walker’s tangent direction.

For example, take \(f=x^2+y^2\) and walk around the unit circle using \(x=\cos t\), \(y=\sin t\). At \(t=45^\circ\), the two contributions are:

Coordinate Slope of \(f\) Walking speed Contribution to change
\(x\) \(2x=\sqrt2\) \(-\sin t=-1/\sqrt2\) \(-1\)
\(y\) \(2y=\sqrt2\) \(\cos t=1/\sqrt2\) \(+1\)
Total \(0\)

The total is zero because the path stays on a contour. By contrast, along \(x=t,y=0\), the function is \(f=t^2\) and changes at rate \(2t\).

Proposition 1 (The gradient is orthogonal to level sets) At a regular point on a contour, the gradient is perpendicular to the contour’s tangent: \(\nabla f(r(t))\cdot r'(t)=0\).

If the gradient itself is zero, this equation still holds, but it does not identify a unique perpendicular direction. That happens, for example, at the bottom of a smooth bowl.

4.3 Direction of maximum increase

For a small step \(d\), the first-order estimate of the change is

\[ \Delta f\approx \nabla f^T d. \]

This dot product is large when \(d\) points in a direction with a large component along the gradient. If the step length is fixed, then

\[ \nabla f^T d =\|\nabla f\|\,\|d\|\cos\theta, \]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between \(d\) and the gradient. The cosine is largest at \(\theta=0\), meaning the step points in the gradient’s direction. This is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in geometric form.

Proposition 2 (Steepest ascent) Among all steps of the same Euclidean length, the gradient gives the largest first-order increase. Its negative gives the largest first-order decrease. A step tangent to a contour gives zero first-order change.

Here is a numerical check. At \((1,0.5)\), let \(f=\tfrac12(x_1^2+x_2^2)\). The gradient is \((1,0.5)^T\). A step of length \(0.1\) produces approximately:

Step direction Approximate endpoint Actual change in \(f\)
along \(x_1\) \((1.1,0.5)\) \(+0.105\)
along \(x_2\) \((1,0.6)\) \(+0.055\)
\(45^\circ\) diagonal \((1.071,0.571)\) \(+0.111\)
gradient direction \((1.089,0.545)\) \(+0.117\) (largest)
tangent to the contour \((0.955,0.589)\) \(+0.005\) (second-order effect)
opposite the gradient \((0.911,0.455)\) \(-0.107\) (largest decrease)

The slopes along the coordinate axes are 1 and 0.5. A fixed step budget is best spent more in the steeper direction; the gradient combines the slopes in exactly the proportions that maximize the dot product. The first-order prediction for the gradient step is \(\|\nabla f\|\,0.1\approx0.112\). The small extra \(0.005\) comes from curvature, because this is a finite step rather than an infinitesimal one.

WarningReading contour plots

A set of nested contours alone does not tell you whether the center is a minimum or maximum. You need the contour values or a gradient arrow. If the gradient points from inner contours toward outer contours, the values increase outward: the center is a bowl bottom. The descent direction \(-\nabla f\) points inward.

4.4 Steepest descent

To minimize \(f\), take a small step opposite the gradient:

\[ x_k=x_{k-1}-\alpha\nabla f(x_{k-1}),\qquad \alpha>0. \]

The gradient tells us which way is locally uphill, so its negative points locally downhill. The step size \(\alpha\) determines how far we move. Too small can make progress slow; too large can overshoot.

For the quadratic \(f(x)=\tfrac12x^TAx\), with symmetric positive-definite \(A\), the gradient is \(Ax\). The update becomes

\[ x_k=(I-\alpha A)x_{k-1}. \]

In an eigenvector direction of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), this multiplies that component of \(x\) by \(1-\alpha\lambda_i\). It converges to zero exactly when every such multiplier has magnitude below 1, which requires \(0<\alpha<2/\lambda_{\max}\). For a general strictly convex smooth function, a suitable step-size rule is needed for convergence to its unique minimizer.

4.5 Steepest descent versus gradient descent

In the common Euclidean update above, the names are often used interchangeably. Some books distinguish them in finer ways:

  1. How the step size is chosen. Some call exact line search “steepest descent”: choose \(\alpha_k\) to minimize \(f(x_k-\alpha\nabla f(x_k))\) over \(\alpha>0\). They may call a fixed or scheduled step size “gradient descent.” This naming convention is not universal.
  2. Which notion of step length is used. “Steepest” has no meaning until we decide how to measure a step. With Euclidean length, the steepest direction is \(-\nabla f\). With a different norm, the answer changes.

More generally, a unit steepest-descent direction solves

\[ v^\star=\arg\min_{\|v\|\le1}\nabla f^T v. \]

How step length is measured Steepest-descent direction Common name
Euclidean norm \(\ell_2\) \(-\nabla f\) gradient descent
\(\|v\|_P=\sqrt{v^TPv}\), where \(P\succ0\) \(-P^{-1}\nabla f\) preconditioned gradient descent
local Hessian norm, when the Hessian is positive definite \(-(\nabla^2f)^{-1}\nabla f\) Newton’s method
\(\ell_1\) norm move along the coordinate with largest \(|\partial_i f|\), in its downhill sign coordinate descent

For the \(P\)-norm, set \(w=P^{1/2}v\). The constraint becomes \(\|w\|_2\le1\), while the quantity to minimize becomes \((P^{-1/2}\nabla f)^Tw\). Its minimum points opposite \(P^{-1/2}\nabla f\), so converting back gives a direction proportional to \(-P^{-1}\nabla f\). For the \(\ell_1\) norm, a unit step can distribute at most one unit of absolute movement across coordinates. To get the greatest decrease, put that movement on a coordinate with largest absolute partial derivative. A unit direction there is \(-\operatorname{sign}(\partial_i f)e_i\).

Why choose a different norm? Consider \(f=\tfrac12(x_1^2+100x_2^2)\) at \((10,1)\). Its gradient is \((10,100)^T\). The level curves are very elongated ellipses, and the gradient points almost vertically even though the minimum is at the origin. Euclidean gradient descent tends to zigzag across the narrow direction. A geometry that accounts for the curvature, such as the \(A\)-norm for this quadratic, gives direction \(-A^{-1}\nabla f=-x\), which points directly to the minimum. The metric changes what “one unit of movement” means.

Thus, “the gradient points in the direction of greatest increase” assumes Euclidean geometry. The differential \(df\) is a linear rule that evaluates changes; the gradient is the vector that represents that rule after an inner product has been chosen.

  1. Exact versus estimated gradients. In adaptive filtering, steepest descent can refer to using the true gradient of a mean-square error, such as \(\nabla_h E[\varepsilon^2]=2(Rh-p)\). LMS replaces the expectation with an estimate from the current sample, giving a stochastic-gradient method. Machine-learning discussions often use “gradient descent” broadly enough to include stochastic and minibatch versions.

The “method of steepest descent” used in asymptotic integration is a separate technique despite the similar name.

4.6 Gradients with respect to complex vectors

For a real-valued function \(f\) of a complex vector \(z\in\mathbb C^N\), define the two Wirtinger-gradient columns

\[ \nabla_z f= \begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial z_1\\ \vdots\\ \partial f/\partial z_N\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \nabla_{z^*} f= \begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial z_1^*\\ \vdots\\ \partial f/\partial z_N^*\end{bmatrix}. \]

These are compact ways to encode the ordinary real partial derivatives with respect to the real and imaginary parts. They are not saying that a physical complex number and its conjugate can vary independently on the original complex plane.

For one coordinate \(z=x+jy\), the definitions are

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial z} =\frac12\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-j\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right), \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial z^*} =\frac12\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+j\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right). \]

Because \(f\) is real-valued, \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) are real, and hence \(\partial f/\partial z=(\partial f/\partial z^*)^*\). In vector form, \(\nabla_z f=(\nabla_{z^*}f)^*\).

Write the differential first. Let \(g=\nabla_{z^*}f\). A real change in the objective under a small complex displacement \(\delta z\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta f &=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_i}\delta z_i+ \frac{\partial f}{\partial z_i^*}\delta z_i^*\right)\\ &=(\nabla_z f)^T\delta z+(\nabla_{z^*}f)^T\delta z^*\\ &=g^H\delta z+(g^H\delta z)^*\\ &=2\operatorname{Re}\{g^H\delta z\}. \end{aligned} \]

The two terms are conjugates, so their sum is real, as it must be. This is the complex-coordinate version of the real formula \(\delta f=\nabla f^T\delta x\).

Theorem 1 (Direction of maximum increase for complex vectors) For steps with \(\|\delta z\|=\varepsilon\), the largest first-order increase is attained by \[ \delta z=\varepsilon\,\frac{g}{\|g\|},\qquad g=\nabla_{z^*}f, \] when \(g\ne0\). The maximum change per unit step length is \(2\|g\|\).

Indeed, \(2\operatorname{Re}\{g^H\delta z\}\le2|g^H\delta z|\le2\|g\|\|\delta z\|\). Equality requires \(\delta z\) to be a positive real multiple of \(g\). A complex multiple with an arbitrary phase is not necessarily the same direction: if \(\delta z=\varepsilon e^{j\phi}g/\|g\|\), then \(\delta f=2\varepsilon\|g\|\cos\phi\).

Phase \(\phi\) Step direction First-order result
\(0\) \(g\) greatest increase
\(\pi\) \(-g\) greatest decrease
\(\pi/2\) or \(-\pi/2\) \(jg\) or \(-jg\) no first-order change; tangent to the level set

Under the standard identification \(\mathbb C^N\simeq\mathbb R^{2N}\), if \(z=x+jy\), then \(g=\tfrac12(\nabla_x f+j\nabla_y f)\). Thus the stacked real gradient is \(2[\operatorname{Re}g;\operatorname{Im}g]\). The factor of 2 is due to the conventional one-half in the Wirtinger operators. The vector \(g\) points in the same ascent direction, even though its length is half the stacked real-gradient length.

Why differentiate with respect to \(z^*\) for a descent direction? The direction is read from the differential written as an inner product with the proposed displacement: \(\delta f=2\operatorname{Re}\{g^H\delta z\}\). In that form, \(g\) is the vector representing the linear change, so \(-g\) is downhill. Although \(\nabla_z f\) can also be used to test whether the derivative is zero, it is not the vector representing ascent under the usual complex inner product.

For \(f=|z|^2\), the derivatives are \(\partial f/\partial z=z^*\) and \(\partial f/\partial z^*=z\). At \(z=j\), the value of \(g\) is \(j\), so stepping in direction \(+j\) increases distance from the origin, while stepping in direction \(-j\) moves toward it. This makes clear why the conjugate-Wirtinger derivative is the useful one for optimization updates.

5 The Hessian and Second-Order Conditions

5.1 Definition

The gradient records first-order slopes. The Hessian records how those slopes change. For \(x\in\mathbb R^N\),

\[ \nabla_x^2 f(x) = \left[\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}\right]_{i,j=1}^N \equiv H_f(x). \]

The \(j\)th column is the gradient of the \(j\)th component of \(\nabla f\). If \(f\) has continuous second partial derivatives, the mixed partials agree and the Hessian is symmetric.

Along a chosen direction \(d\), consider the one-variable function \(\phi(t)=f(x+td)\). The chain rule gives \(\phi''(0)=d^TH_fd\). So the quadratic form \(d^TH_fd\) tells us whether the graph bends upward or downward along that particular direction.

5.2 Positive definiteness and the symbol \(\succ\)

A symmetric matrix \(A\) is positive definite, written \(A\succ0\), if

\[ d^TAd>0\qquad\text{for every nonzero direction }d. \]

For symmetric \(A\), this is equivalent to saying that all eigenvalues are positive. Positive definite does not mean “all matrix entries are positive”; it means every nonzero direction has positive quadratic curvature.

Matrix Are all entries positive? Positive definite?
\(\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}\) yes no: along \((1,-1)\) the quadratic form is negative
\(\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\) no yes: every nonzero direction gives a positive value

The notation \(A\succ B\) means \(A-B\succ0\). Unlike the ordinary order on real numbers, this matrix order is only partial: a matrix such as \(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) is neither positive nor negative definite.

5.3 Minimum conditions

Theorem 2 (Second-order conditions (unconstrained)) For a twice continuously differentiable function: - Necessary: if \(x^\star\) is a local minimum, then \(\nabla f(x^\star)=0\) and \(\nabla^2 f(x^\star)\succeq0\). - Sufficient: if \(\nabla f(x^\star)=0\) and \(\nabla^2 f(x^\star)\succ0\), then \(x^\star\) is a strict local minimum.

The first condition says there is no nonzero first-order slope at an interior minimum. The Hessian condition then asks how the function bends nearby. Taylor’s formula makes the reason visible:

\[ f(x^\star+h) =f(x^\star)+\frac12h^T\nabla^2f(x^\star)h+o(\|h\|^2). \]

If the Hessian is positive definite, the quadratic term is positive in every direction and dominates the smaller remainder for sufficiently small \(h\). If the Hessian is indefinite, it bends upward in some directions and downward in others, producing a saddle. For example, \(f=x_1^2-x_2^2\) has zero gradient at the origin; it rises along the \(x_1\) axis and falls along the \(x_2\) axis. A semidefinite Hessian is inconclusive by itself: higher-order terms may decide the result.

5.4 A caveat for complex variables

For a real-valued objective written in complex coordinates, checking only the mixed derivative matrix \(\nabla_z(\nabla_{z^*}f)\) can miss curvature in directions that depend on \(z^2\) or \(z^{*2}\). The mixed matrix alone is enough for common circular quadratic forms such as \(z^HAz\), but not for every real-valued function.

For example,

\[ f(z,z^*)=zz^*+\frac{\beta}{2}(z^2+z^{*2}) \]

has mixed derivative \(\partial^2f/\partial z\,\partial z^*=1\). But putting \(z=x+jy\) gives

\[ f=(1+\beta)x^2+(1-\beta)y^2. \]

When \(\beta>1\), the coefficient of \(x^2\) is positive and that of \(y^2\) is negative. The origin is therefore a saddle, even though the mixed derivative is positive. In the general complex case, inspect the full real Hessian, or its equivalent augmented complex Hessian, which includes the pure \(z\) and pure \(z^*\) second derivatives.

6.1 Transpose rules and dimension bookkeeping

Matrix calculus becomes easier when we keep track of both the algebra and each quantity’s shape.

Rule What it says

\((AB)^T=B^TA^T\) transpose reverses product order

For example, \(c^Tx\) and \(x^Tc\) are both the same scalar. But generally \(AB\ne BA\). During a derivation, annotate dimensions such as \(x:N\times1\), \(A:M\times N\). That catches invalid products and tells you whether a result must be scalar, vector, or matrix.

6.2 The perturbation method is the definition of the derivative

The gradient is not merely a list of partials. It is the vector that gives the exact linear part of a small change. A function \(f:\mathbb R^N\to\mathbb R\) is differentiable at \(x\) when there is a vector \(g\) such that

\[ f(x+h)=f(x)+g^Th+r(h), \qquad \frac{r(h)}{\|h\|}\longrightarrow0\quad\text{as }h\to0. \]

The remainder \(r(h)\) becomes negligible relative to the step length. The vector \(g\) is the gradient, \(\nabla f(x)=g\).

This explains the reliable calculation recipe: replace \(x\) by \(x+h\), expand, and keep the terms linear in \(h\). The terms of higher order form the remainder. The expansion itself is exact; the approximation occurs only when we ignore the remainder for a sufficiently small step.

The gradient is unique. If two vectors \(g_1,g_2\) both described the linear part, then \((g_1-g_2)^Th=o(\|h\|)\). Taking a step parallel to \(g_1-g_2\) shows this can hold for arbitrarily small steps only when \(g_1=g_2\). Also, setting \(h=te_k\) recovers the familiar partial derivative in coordinate \(k\).

For a quadratic form the remainder is especially transparent:

\[ (x+h)^TA(x+h) =x^TAx+h^T(A+A^T)x+h^TAh. \]

The first added term is linear in \(h\); the last is quadratic and therefore becomes negligible relative to \(\|h\|\) as \(h\to0\).

WarningThe definition requires differentiability

Having partial derivatives is not always enough to guarantee a gradient linear approximation.

  • At \(x=0\), \(f(x)=|x|\) has no derivative: its change \(|h|\) cannot be represented by one linear expression for positive and negative \(h\).
  • The function \(f(x,y)=x^2y/(x^2+y^2)\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), with \(f(0,0)=0\), has both partial derivatives zero at the origin. But along \(x=y=t\), its value is \(t/2\), which is not a smaller-order remainder compared with the step length. So the partials do not form a valid gradient there.

6.3 Core identities with proofs

1. Gradient of a linear inner product. Let \(y\) be fixed. Since \(x^Ty=\sum_i x_i y_i\), a perturbation gives

\[ (x+h)^Ty=x^Ty+h^Ty. \]

The coefficient vector of the linear change is \(y\), hence \(\nabla_x(x^Ty)=y\).

2. Gradient of a quadratic form. The vector \(x\) appears in both slots of \(x^TAx\), so differentiating produces one contribution from each slot:

\[ \nabla_x(x^TAx)=(A+A^T)x. \]

If \(A\) is symmetric, this is \(2Ax\). For instance, with \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{bmatrix}\), the scalar is \(ax_1^2+2bx_1x_2+dx_2^2\), whose gradient is \((2ax_1+2bx_2,\ 2bx_1+2dx_2)^T\).

Only the symmetric part of \(A\) affects this scalar. Write \(A=\tfrac12(A+A^T)+\tfrac12(A-A^T)\). The second part is skew-symmetric and contributes zero to \(x^TAx\). This is why quadratic objectives can be analyzed with symmetric matrices.

3. Gradient with respect to a matrix. The gradient has the same shape as the matrix being differentiated. For matrices \(X,Y\) of the same size,

\[ \operatorname{tr}(X^TY)=\sum_{i,j}X_{ij}Y_{ij}. \]

This is the Frobenius inner product: multiply corresponding entries and add. Differentiating with respect to one entry \(X_{ab}\) gives \(Y_{ab}\), so

\[ \nabla_X\operatorname{tr}(X^TY)=Y. \]

This is the matrix version of \(\nabla_x(x^Ty)=y\). Similarly, \(\nabla_X\operatorname{tr}(AX)=A^T\) when the dimensions make the trace valid.

\(\nabla_x(x^TAx)=(A+A^T)x\) \(x\) occurs in two slots \((ax^2)'=2ax\)

7 Least Squares

7.1 Model and stacking

Suppose we measured input-output pairs \((x_n,y_n)\) and want to fit a model that is linear in unknown coefficients:

\[ y\approx\sum_{k=1}^K a_k\phi_k(x), \]

where the basis functions \(\phi_k\) are known and the coefficients \(a_k\) are chosen from data. “Stacking” means putting the observations into one vector and all basis values into a matrix.

Symbol Meaning Size Straight-line fit example
\(a\) unknown coefficients \(K\times1\) \([b,m]^T\)
\(Y\) observed outputs \(N\times1\) \([1,3,4]^T\)
\(\Phi\) basis values, \(\Phi_{n,k}=\phi_k(x_n)\) \(N\times K\) \(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\)

For the line \(y=b+mx\) fitted to \((0,1),(1,3),(2,4)\), the product

\[ \Phi a= \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}b\\m\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}b\\b+m\\b+2m\end{bmatrix} \]

contains the model’s prediction at each observed input. The data equations are \(Y\approx\Phi a\).

7.2 Residual and objective

The residual is the observation minus its prediction:

\[ \varepsilon=Y-\Phi a. \]

It contains one error per data point. Positive and negative residuals could cancel if we simply added them, so least squares uses the sum of their squares:

\[ J(a)=\|Y-\Phi a\|_2^2 =(Y-\Phi a)^T(Y-\Phi a) =\sum_n\varepsilon_n^2. \]

This gives one nonnegative score for the fit and makes \(J\) a smooth quadratic in the coefficients. If the measurement errors are independent, zero-mean Gaussian variables with a common variance, minimizing this score is also maximum-likelihood estimation: among all parameter choices, it selects the one under which the observed residuals are most probable.

7.3 Normal equations

The data \(Y\) and design matrix \(\Phi\) are fixed. The coefficients \(a\) are the choices, so \(J\) is a function of \(a\). At an unconstrained minimum, its gradient with respect to \(a\) must be zero.

Expand the squared residual:

\[ \begin{aligned} J(a) &=(Y-\Phi a)^T(Y-\Phi a)\\ &=Y^TY-Y^T\Phi a-a^T\Phi^TY+a^T\Phi^T\Phi a\\ &=Y^TY-2Y^T\Phi a+a^T\Phi^T\Phi a. \end{aligned} \]

The two middle terms are scalars and are transposes of each other, so they are equal. Now differentiate term by term:

Term Why Gradient with respect to \(a\)
\(Y^TY\) does not depend on \(a\) \(0\)
\(-2Y^T\Phi a=-2(\Phi^TY)^Ta\) linear inner-product rule \(-2\Phi^TY\)
\(a^T(\Phi^T\Phi)a\) quadratic-form rule; \(\Phi^T\Phi\) is symmetric \(2\Phi^T\Phi a\)

Therefore

\[ \nabla_aJ=-2\Phi^TY+2\Phi^T\Phi a=0, \]

so

\[ \Phi^T\Phi\,a=\Phi^TY. \]

These are the normal equations. If the columns of \(\Phi\) are linearly independent, then \(\Phi^T\Phi\) is invertible and

\[ a^\star=(\Phi^T\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^TY. \]

The inverse formula describes the solution, though in numerical computation one normally solves the system without forming the inverse.

7.4 Worked example

For the line fit above,

\[ \Phi^T\Phi= \begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Phi^TY= \begin{bmatrix}8\\11\end{bmatrix}. \]

The normal equations for \(a=[b,m]^T\) are

\[ 3b+3m=8,\qquad 3b+5m=11. \]

Subtract the first from the second: \(2m=3\), so \(m=3/2\). Substitution gives \(3b+9/2=8\), hence \(b=7/6\). The fitted line is \(y=7/6+(3/2)x\). Its predictions are \(7/6,\,8/3,\,25/6\), so the residuals are

\[ \left(-\frac16,\frac13,-\frac16\right), \qquad J_{\min}=\frac1{36}+\frac19+\frac1{36}=\frac16. \]

7.5 The orthogonality principle

At the optimum, the normal equations can be written

\[ \Phi^T(Y-\Phi a^\star)=0. \]

The residual is perpendicular to every column of \(\Phi\). In the line example, one column is all ones and the other is the list of input values \(0,1,2\). The residuals sum to zero, and their inner product with the input list is also zero. Geometrically, the fitted vector \(\widehat Y=\Phi a^\star\) is the orthogonal projection of the data vector \(Y\) onto the space of all prediction vectors the model can produce.

This is the geometry behind “least squares”: among all points in that prediction space, the projection is the one at shortest Euclidean distance from the data.

7.6 When \(\Phi^T\Phi\) is singular

The matrix \(\Phi^T\Phi\) is singular exactly when the columns of \(\Phi\) are dependent. This may happen when there are fewer observations than coefficients, when two basis functions repeat the same information, or when the input data do not vary enough to distinguish coefficients.

The least-squares problem still has a minimum, but its coefficient vector may not be unique. For example, observing \((1,2)\) and \((1,4)\) while fitting a line gives

\[ \Phi=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Both inputs equal 1. The data tell us what the fitted line should predict at \(x=1\) (the mean, 3), but they cannot tell us the separate values of intercept \(b\) and slope \(m\). Every pair satisfying \(b+m=3\) has the same predictions and the same objective value \(J=2\).

Why does a solution still exist? The set of possible prediction vectors is the column space of \(\Phi\). There is always a unique closest point in a finite-dimensional subspace, even if many coefficient vectors produce that same point. If \(\Phi v=0\), then adding \(v\) to the coefficients does not change predictions: \(\Phi(a+v)=\Phi a\). The predictions are identifiable, but the coefficients are not.

Common remedies are:

  1. Choose the minimum-length coefficient vector. The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse gives \(a^\star=\Phi^+Y\). It can be computed by singular-value decomposition.
  2. Use ridge regularization. Minimize \(\|Y-\Phi a\|^2+\delta\|a\|^2\), for \(\delta>0\). The solution is \((\Phi^T\Phi+\delta I)^{-1}\Phi^TY\).
  3. Change the model or collect better data. Remove redundant basis functions, reduce model order, or sample a broader range of inputs.

A related issue is near-singularity. If columns are almost dependent, the coefficients technically have a unique solution, but small measurement changes can cause large coefficient changes. With independent Gaussian noise of variance \(\sigma^2\), the coefficient covariance is \(\sigma^2(\Phi^T\Phi)^{-1}\), which becomes large as \(\Phi^T\Phi\) approaches singularity.

7.7 Polynomial approximation and numerical practice

For a polynomial, the basis functions are \(1,x,x^2,\dots\). The matrix of basis values is a Vandermonde matrix. A degree-\(p\) polynomial uses \(p+1\) columns:

\[ \Phi_{n,k}=x_n^k,\quad k=0,\dots,p. \]

The normal equations work on paper, but forming \(\Phi^T\Phi\) can be numerically unstable when columns of \(\Phi\) are nearly parallel. High-degree monomials often have very different scales, which makes this problem common.

There are three ways to calculate the same least-squares answer:

Method What it does
Form \((\Phi^T\Phi)^{-1}\) computes an inverse explicitly; usually the least desirable route
Solve \((\Phi^T\Phi)a=\Phi^TY\) uses elimination or Cholesky without explicitly computing an inverse
Use QR or SVD on \(\Phi\) avoids forming \(\Phi^T\Phi\); generally more stable

The reason is that forming the normal equations squares the condition number: \(\kappa(\Phi^T\Phi)=\kappa(\Phi)^2\). This can magnify roundoff error and destroy accuracy. QR and SVD are usually safer.

For example, on \([0,1]\), the columns \(x^8,x^9,x^{10}\) are very similar: most entries are close to zero, and all rise sharply near 1. Such near-parallel columns make the coefficient estimates sensitive. In a noiseless degree-12 fit with 50 equally spaced points and all true coefficients equal to 1, one illustrative calculation gives:

Rescaling \(x\) to \([-1,1]\) and using orthogonal polynomial bases such as Legendre or Chebyshev can improve conditioning further.

7.8 Complex least squares

For complex data, transpose becomes conjugate transpose. Let \(y\in\mathbb C^M\), \(H\in\mathbb C^{M\times K}\), and \(z\in\mathbb C^K\). The residual objective is

\[ J(z)=\|y-Hz\|^2=(y-Hz)^H(y-Hz). \]

Treating \(z\) and \(z^*\) as Wirtinger variables and differentiating with respect to \(z^*\) gives

\[ \nabla_{z^*}J=-H^H(y-Hz). \]

Setting it to zero yields

\[ H^HHz=H^Hy. \]

If \(H\) has full column rank, then \(H^HH\) is invertible and \(z^\star=(H^HH)^{-1}H^Hy\). The same geometric idea holds: the fitted vector is the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the column space of \(H\), now using the complex inner product.

8 Equality-Constrained Optimization

Now suppose we want to minimize a real-valued objective while obeying equality constraints:

\[ \min_{x\in\mathbb R^n}f(x) \quad\text{subject to}\quad g(x)=0, \qquad g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m. \]

There are \(n\) adjustable coordinates but only some movements are allowed. The central idea is to study how \(f\) changes along the allowed movements.

8.1 Why “find the stationary points of \(f\), then keep the feasible ones” fails

A tempting strategy is to solve the unconstrained condition \(\nabla f=0\) and then discard solutions that violate the constraint. This is generally wrong because a constrained optimum need not be stationary in every direction.

For example, minimize \(x_1^2+x_2^2\) subject to \(x_1+x_2=1\). The unconstrained minimum is \((0,0)\), but it is infeasible. The constrained solution is \((1/2,1/2)\), where

\[ \nabla f=(2x_1,2x_2)=(1,1)\ne0. \]

Why can the gradient be nonzero at the constrained solution? Because the gradient has components pointing in directions we are forbidden to move. At the optimum, only the slope along the constraint must be zero. If the constraint is solved explicitly as \(x_2=1-x_1\), the problem becomes the one-variable function

\[ \varphi(x_1)=x_1^2+(1-x_1)^2. \]

Then \(\varphi'(x_1)=4x_1-2=0\), giving \(x_1=x_2=1/2\). Lagrange multipliers let us do the equivalent calculation without explicitly solving for one variable.

8.2 The Lagrangian

For equality constraints, define

\[ L(x;\lambda)=f(x)+\lambda^Tg(x), \qquad \lambda\in\mathbb R^m. \]

On a feasible point, \(g(x)=0\), so \(L(x;\lambda)=f(x)\) for every \(\lambda\). The extra term does not change the objective values on the allowed set. It does change the slopes off that set. We choose \(\lambda\) so that the added constraint slopes balance the objective slope at the constrained optimum.

The first-order equations are

\[ \nabla_xL(x;\lambda)=0, \qquad \nabla_\lambda L(x;\lambda)=g(x)=0. \]

The second equation enforces feasibility. We are not simply minimizing \(L\) over \(x\) and ignoring constraints: we solve for \(x\) and \(\lambda\) together so that both stationarity and feasibility hold.

For the example, write the constraint as \(g=x_1+x_2-1\) and use

\[ L=x_1^2+x_2^2+\lambda(x_1+x_2-1). \]

For any fixed \(\lambda\), stationarity in \(x\) gives \(x_1=x_2=-\lambda/2\):

Trial \(\lambda\) Stationary point in \(x\) Constraint value \(g(x)\) Feasible?
\(0\) \((0,0)\) \(-1\) no
\(-2\) \((1,1)\) \(1\) no
\(-1\) \((1/2,1/2)\) \(0\) yes

The multiplier acts like a price for violating the constraint. It is not a penalty that is always positive: for an equality constraint, \(g\) may be positive or negative. A penalty method instead adds something like \(\frac{\rho}{2}\|g(x)\|^2\), which is always nonnegative and is a different method.

8.3 First-order condition

The Jacobian of the constraints is the \(m\times n\) matrix

\[ J(x)=\frac{\partial g}{\partial x} = \begin{bmatrix} \nabla g_1(x)^T\\ \vdots\\ \nabla g_m(x)^T \end{bmatrix}. \]

A direction \(d\) that preserves the constraints to first order must satisfy \(Jd=0\). All such directions form the tangent space:

\[ T=\{d:Jd=0\}. \]

At a local constrained minimum, no tangent direction can decrease \(f\). But if \(d\) is tangent, so is \(-d\). Therefore \(\nabla f^Td\) must be zero for every \(d\in T\). In words, the objective gradient is perpendicular to every feasible first-order movement.

The vectors perpendicular to the null space of \(J\) lie in the row space of \(J\), which is spanned by the constraint gradients. So the objective gradient must be a combination of constraint gradients:

\[ \nabla f(x^\star)+\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i^\star\nabla g_i(x^\star)=0, \qquad g(x^\star)=0. \]

These are the Lagrange multiplier equations, and for equality constraints they are also the equality-constrained KKT equations. Geometrically, the normal directions of the constraints balance the normal component of the objective gradient.

8.4 LICQ

The linear independence constraint qualification, abbreviated LICQ, says that the constraint gradients at the point are linearly independent.

Definition 1 (Linear independence constraint qualification) LICQ holds at \(x^\star\) if \(\nabla g_1(x^\star),\dots,\nabla g_m(x^\star)\) are linearly independent.

Situation Meaning of LICQ
One constraint Its gradient is nonzero
Linear constraints \(Cx=c\) The rows of \(C\) are linearly independent
Several nonlinear constraints Each constraint supplies a genuinely different local normal direction

LICQ is a regularity condition: locally, the constraints behave like a smooth surface with the expected number of independent normals.

Theorem 3 (Lagrange multiplier theorem) If \(x^\star\) is a local constrained minimizer and LICQ holds, then there is a unique multiplier vector \(\lambda^\star\) such that \(\nabla_xL(x^\star;\lambda^\star)=0\) and \(g(x^\star)=0\).

Why is a condition like LICQ needed? Consider

\[ g_1=x_2-x_1^2=0,\qquad g_2=x_2+x_1^2=0,\qquad f=x_1. \]

The two constraints can both hold only at \((0,0)\), so that point is the only feasible point and is automatically a minimizer. But at the origin, both constraint gradients equal \((0,1)^T\). They are dependent. The multiplier stationarity equation would be

\[ (1,0)^T+(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)(0,1)^T=0, \]

which has no solution: the left-hand side always has first component 1. The linearized constraints suggest horizontal motion is allowed, even though the exact feasible set contains only one point. LICQ rules out this kind of degeneracy. When it holds, the multipliers are unique.

8.5 Geometry: normals in balance

At a regular constrained optimum:

  • The gradient \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to the feasible surface. Otherwise, it would have a component along the surface and we could move along the surface to decrease \(f\).
  • Each constraint gradient \(\nabla g_i\) is a normal to one constraint surface.
  • The multipliers scale those normals so they balance the objective gradient: \(\nabla f=-\sum_i\lambda_i\nabla g_i\).
  • Equivalently, a contour of the objective just touches the constraint surface at the optimum. If it crossed the constraint surface, there would still be a nearby feasible point on a lower contour.

A physical picture is a bead on a wire. The objective can be thought of as potential energy, with force \(-\nabla f\). The wire prevents motion across itself and supplies a normal reaction force. At equilibrium, that reaction balances the component of the objective force that would otherwise move the bead. The multiplier describes the size of that constraint force.

8.6 One multiplier per constraint, not per variable

There is one multiplier for each independent constraint, not one for each coordinate. In \(\min x^TAx\) subject to \(c^Tx=1\), there is one scalar equation, so \(\lambda\) is one scalar. At the solution, \(\nabla f\) must be parallel to the one normal vector \(c\).

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\) and \(c=(1,1)^T\), compare:

Point \(\nabla f=2Ax\) One scalar \(\lambda\) can make \(\nabla f=-\lambda c\)?
\((0.8,0.2)\) \((1.6,1.6)\) yes
\((0.5,0.5)\) \((1,4)\) no; it is not parallel to \((1,1)\)

If we allowed a different multiplier for each coordinate, then almost any gradient could be matched component by component, and stationarity would stop telling us whether the gradient is normal to the constraint. With \(m\) independent constraints we have \(m\) multipliers, one scale per constraint normal. The multiplier is an unknown solved together with \(x\); it is not a function of \(x\).

8.7 Sensitivity: what the multiplier measures

The multiplier also tells us how much the best achievable objective changes when we move the constraint slightly. Replace \(g(x)=0\) by \(g(x)=\varepsilon\) and let \(p^\star(\varepsilon)\) be the minimum value. Differentiating along the optimal solution path gives

\[ \frac{dp^\star}{d\varepsilon} =\nabla f^T\frac{dx^\star}{d\varepsilon} =-\lambda^T\nabla g^T\frac{dx^\star}{d\varepsilon} =-\lambda^T\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}g(x^\star(\varepsilon)) =-\lambda. \]

For multiple constraints, this relation is componentwise. Thus the multiplier measures the marginal change in optimal cost when the right-hand side of a constraint changes. In economics it is called a shadow price.

Check the earlier example with constraint \(x_1+x_2=1+\varepsilon\). Its minimum value is \(p^\star(\varepsilon)=(1+\varepsilon)^2/2\), whose derivative at zero is 1. The optimal multiplier at \(\varepsilon=0\) is \(-1\), so indeed \(dp^\star/d\varepsilon=-\lambda=1\).

8.8 Second-order conditions

First-order stationarity says the objective has no slope along allowed directions. It does not say whether the point is a minimum, maximum, or saddle along those directions. For that, examine curvature along the tangent space.

Theorem 4 (Second-order conditions (equality constraints)) Suppose \(x^\star\) satisfies the first-order conditions, LICQ holds, and \(T=\{d:J(x^\star)d=0\}\).

  • Necessary: at a local minimum, \(d^T\nabla_x^2L(x^\star;\lambda^\star)d\ge0\) for every \(d\in T\).
  • Sufficient: if this quantity is strictly positive for every nonzero \(d\in T\), then \(x^\star\) is a strict local minimum.

There are two important details: use the Hessian of the Lagrangian, and test it only in tangent directions. The Lagrangian Hessian includes curvature caused by curved constraints. Tangent directions are the only directions we can actually move while staying feasible.

The non-strict condition is only necessary. For example, minimize \(-x_2^4\) subject to \(x_1=0\). At the origin the Lagrangian Hessian is zero in every direction, so it passes the non-strict test. But on the constraint, the function is \(-x_2^4\le0\), so the origin is a local maximum, not a minimum. Strictly positive tangent curvature is sufficient; merely nonnegative curvature may leave the answer to higher-order terms.

Why use \(\nabla^2L\) rather than only \(\nabla^2f\)? Consider minimizing \(-x_2\) on the unit circle \(x_1^2+x_2^2=1\). The objective is linear and has zero Hessian, but the constraint bends. Write \(g=x_1^2+x_2^2-1\) and \(L=-x_2+\lambda g\). At the top \((0,1)\), stationarity gives \(\lambda=1/2\), and \(\nabla^2L=I\), so tangent curvature is positive. At the bottom \((0,-1)\), \(\lambda=-1/2\), and \(\nabla^2L=-I\), so tangent curvature is negative. The Hessian of \(L\) correctly distinguishes the minimum from the maximum because it includes the constraint’s curvature.

Why test only tangent directions? Minimize \(x_1^2-x_2^2\) subject to \(x_2=0\). Its unconstrained Hessian is indefinite, but the constraint forbids movement in the \(x_2\) direction. Along the feasible line, the objective is just \(x_1^2\), which has a strict minimum at zero. The right second-order test is therefore positivity only on directions satisfying the linearized constraint.

8.9 When is the constrained solution unique?

A standard sufficient condition for a unique global minimizer is: the objective is strictly convex, the feasible set is convex, and a minimizer exists. If there were two different minimizers, strict convexity would make the objective at their midpoint strictly smaller. But the midpoint is feasible when the feasible set is convex, a contradiction.

For equality constraints, the feasible set is convex when the constraints are affine, such as \(Cx=c\). A nonlinear equality surface need not be convex as a set. For example, minimizing \(x_1^2+2x_2^2\) on the unit circle has two minimizers \((1,0)\) and \((-1,0)\), even though the objective is strictly convex. The circle is not a convex feasible set: the midpoint of those two feasible points is the infeasible origin.

For inequalities \(h_i(x)\le0\), convex functions \(h_i\) define convex sublevel sets, so convexity of all constraints produces a convex feasible region. A strictly convex feasible region can also make a linear objective’s minimizer unique. For example, a nonzero linear objective over a Euclidean ball has one minimizing point; over a box, it may minimize along an entire edge.

9 Worked Example: Quadratic Minimization with One Linear Constraint

Consider the quadratic problem

\[ \min_{x\in\mathbb R^n}x^TAx \quad\text{subject to}\quad c^Tx=1, \qquad A=A^T\succ0. \]

Since \(A\) is positive definite, \(x^TAx\) is a bowl with a unique unconstrained minimum at \(x=0\). But zero does not satisfy \(c^Tx=1\), so the constraint forces us to choose the least-cost point on a hyperplane. The one constraint has nonzero gradient \(c\), so LICQ holds.

9.1 Stationarity, multiplier, and solution

Form the Lagrangian

\[ L(x;\lambda)=x^TAx+\lambda(c^Tx-1). \]

Using \(\nabla_x(x^TAx)=2Ax\) for symmetric \(A\),

\[ \nabla_xL=2Ax+\lambda c=0. \]

Multiply on the left by \(A^{-1}\) to solve for \(x\):

\[ x(\lambda)=-\frac{\lambda}{2}A^{-1}c. \]

Now impose the constraint:

\[ c^Tx(\lambda) =-\frac{\lambda}{2}c^TA^{-1}c=1. \]

Because \(A^{-1}\) is positive definite, \(c^TA^{-1}c>0\). Therefore

\[ \lambda^\star=-\frac{2}{c^TA^{-1}c}, \qquad x^\star=\frac{A^{-1}c}{c^TA^{-1}c}. \]

The solution is feasible by construction. Strict convexity of the objective and the affine constraint make it the unique global minimizer.

9.2 Optimal value: two derivations

Let \(s=c^TA^{-1}c>0\), so \(x^\star=A^{-1}c/s\). Substitute into the objective:

\[ \begin{aligned} x^{\star T}Ax^\star &=\frac{1}{s^2}c^T(A^{-1})^TA A^{-1}c\\ &=\frac{1}{s^2}c^TA^{-1}c\\ &=\frac1s =\frac{1}{c^TA^{-1}c}. \end{aligned} \]

We used that \(A^{-1}\) is symmetric because \(A\) is symmetric. There is also a shorter derivation. From stationarity, \(2Ax^\star=-\lambda^\star c\). Multiply by \(x^{\star T}\) and use \(x^{\star T}c=1\):

\[ 2x^{\star T}Ax^\star=-\lambda^\star, \qquad x^{\star T}Ax^\star=-\frac{\lambda^\star}{2} =\frac{1}{c^TA^{-1}c}. \]

So the best objective value is directly related to the multiplier.

9.3 Numerical check

Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\) and \(c=(1,1)^T\). This is the problem

\[ \min x_1^2+4x_2^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad x_1+x_2=1. \]

Here \(A^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(1,1/4)\) and \(s=1+1/4=5/4\). Therefore

\[ x^\star=\frac{(1,1/4)^T}{5/4}=(0.8,0.2)^T, \qquad \lambda^\star=-\frac{2}{5/4}=-1.6. \]

The objective is \(0.8^2+4(0.2)^2=0.8=1/s\).

9.4 Why \(A^{-1}\) cannot be canceled

The solution is

\[ x^\star=\frac{A^{-1}c}{c^TA^{-1}c}. \]

The numerator is a vector and the denominator is a scalar. The \(A^{-1}\) in the numerator transforms the direction \(c\); the one in the denominator determines the scale needed to meet the constraint. They cannot be canceled as if they were common scalar factors. Matrix multiplication does not generally commute.

For the numerical example, the correct solution is \((0.8,0.2)\). If one incorrectly “cancels” \(A^{-1}\), one gets \((0.5,0.5)\). Both points satisfy \(x_1+x_2=1\), but the second has objective \(1.25\), larger than \(0.8\). The simplification is valid only in special cases, such as when \(c\) is an eigenvector of \(A\).

Interpretation in signal processing. This is the minimum-variance distortionless-response (MVDR) beamformer. The matrix \(A\) represents noise covariance, and \(c\) represents the desired signal direction. With white noise, all directions have equal cost and the solution is the normalized vector \(c\). With colored noise, \(A^{-1}\) accounts for which directions are noisy before the normalization is applied.

10 Complex Variables with Constraints

10.1 The Lagrangian must be real

Wirtinger derivatives are designed for real-valued objectives. Therefore, when optimizing over complex variables, the Lagrangian must also be real-valued.

One complex equality \(g(z)=0\) represents two real equalities:

\[ \operatorname{Re}g(z)=0,\qquad \operatorname{Im}g(z)=0. \]

It therefore needs two real multipliers. We can combine them into one complex multiplier \(\lambda=\mu+j\nu\), because

\[ \mu\operatorname{Re}g+\nu\operatorname{Im}g =\operatorname{Re}\{\lambda^*g\}. \]

For a vector constraint \(Cz=c\), a real Lagrangian can be written

\[ L=z^HAz+\lambda^H(Cz-c)+\lambda^T(C^*z^*-c^*) =z^HAz+2\operatorname{Re}\{\lambda^H(Cz-c)\}. \]

The factor 2 can be absorbed into the multiplier’s definition. A constraint whose right side is real, such as \(c^Hz=1\), is still a complex equation: its imaginary part must also be zero.

Why not use the seemingly natural \(L=z^HAz+\lambda^H(Cz-c)\) alone? It is generally complex-valued. Then its two Wirtinger derivatives need not be conjugates, and setting just one to zero can give a false condition. Including the conjugate term makes \(L\) real, restoring the usual real-valued optimization logic.

10.2 Example: multiple complex linear constraints (LCMV)

Consider

\[ \min_z z^HAz \quad\text{subject to}\quad Cz=c, \]

where \(z\in\mathbb C^N\), \(A\) is Hermitian positive definite, and \(C\) has \(M\) rows. Each row of \(C\) is one complex constraint, and \(c\) contains its required value. There is one complex multiplier per complex constraint.

For example, the requirements \(z_1+z_2+z_3=1\) and \(z_1-z_2=0\) correspond to

\[ C=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&-1&0\end{bmatrix}, \qquad c=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}. \]

Use the real Lagrangian

\[ L=z^HAz+\lambda^H(Cz-c)+\lambda^T(C^*z^*-c^*). \]

It is real: \(z^HAz\) is real because \(A=A^H\), and the last two terms are conjugates. Differentiate with respect to \(z^*\), holding \(z\) fixed:

Term Derivative with respect to \(z^*\)
\(z^HAz\) \(Az\)
\(\lambda^H(Cz-c)\) \(0\)
\(\lambda^T(C^*z^*-c^*)\) \(C^H\lambda\)

Thus stationarity is

\[ Az+C^H\lambda=0. \]

Solve this equation for \(z\):

\[ z=-A^{-1}C^H\lambda. \]

Now require the constraint \(Cz=c\):

\[ -CA^{-1}C^H\lambda=c. \]

If the rows of \(C\) are linearly independent, then \(CA^{-1}C^H\) is positive definite and invertible. So

\[ \lambda^\star=-(CA^{-1}C^H)^{-1}c, \]

and therefore

\[ z^\star=A^{-1}C^H(CA^{-1}C^H)^{-1}c. \]

The order of these matrix factors matters; the inverse remains between \(C^H\) and \(c\). A quick check confirms feasibility:

\[ Cz^\star=CA^{-1}C^H(CA^{-1}C^H)^{-1}c=c. \]

Why does independent rows guarantee invertibility? For any nonzero \(u\),

\[ u^HCA^{-1}C^Hu=(C^Hu)^HA^{-1}(C^Hu)>0 \]

provided \(C^Hu\ne0\). That holds exactly when the rows of \(C\) are independent.

If \(A=I\), this becomes \(z^\star=C^H(CC^H)^{-1}c\), the minimum-norm solution of the constraints. In array processing, this is the linearly constrained minimum-variance (LCMV) beamformer: constraints can preserve a desired response in one direction while forcing zero response, or a “null,” in interference directions.

11 Inequality Constraints and the KKT Conditions

An equality constraint says where we must stay. An inequality constraint says which side of a boundary is allowed. We use one consistent convention:

Constraint type Standard form Multiplier
Equality \(h_j(x)=0\) \(\lambda_j\) is unrestricted
Inequality \(g_i(x)\le0\) \(\mu_i\ge0\)

For minimization, the Lagrangian is \[ L(x,\lambda,\mu)=f(x)+\sum_j\lambda_jh_j(x)+\sum_i\mu_i g_i(x). \]

11.1 Why the standard form is “minimize, with \(g(x)\le0\)”

Any inequality can be rewritten this way: \(a(x)\ge b\) is the same as \(b-a(x)\le0\). Fixing one convention lets us use the same sign rule, \(\mu\ge0\), for every inequality.

At a boundary point where \(g(x)=0\), the feasible side has \(g(x)<0\), and the outside has \(g(x)>0\). Thus \(\nabla g\) points outward. At a constrained minimum, the downhill direction \(-\nabla f\) must be blocked by the boundary; it points outward too:

\[ -\nabla f=\mu\nabla g,\qquad\mu\ge0, \]

or \(\nabla f+\mu\nabla g=0\). Equality multipliers are unrestricted because an equality can block movement from either side.

The sign convention also makes the Lagrangian a lower bound on feasible objective values: at a feasible point, \(\mu g(x)\le0\), hence \(L(x,\lambda,\mu)\le f(x)\). For a sign check, minimize \(x^2\) subject to \(x\ge1\). Write \(g=1-x\le0\). At \(x=1\), stationarity \(2x-\mu=0\) gives \(\mu=2\ge0\). If we kept \(x-1\ge0\) but used the \(g\le0\) rule, the multiplier would have the wrong sign and reject the true solution. Convert maximization to minimization of \(-f\) before using this convention.

11.2 Active and inactive constraints

An inequality is active at a boundary point, \(g_i(x)=0\), and inactive when it has slack, \(g_i(x)<0\). Imagine a fenced yard: in the middle, the fence does not limit small steps; against the fence, outward movement is blocked.

  • An inactive constraint is locally irrelevant, so its multiplier is zero.
  • An active constraint can balance the objective gradient, with a nonnegative multiplier.
Problem Unconstrained minimizer Constrained solution Status
\(\min(x-1)^2\) subject to \(x\le2\) \(1\) \(1\) inactive, \(\mu=0\)
\(\min(x-3)^2\) subject to \(x\le2\) \(3\) \(2\) active, \(\mu=2\)

11.3 Geometry of the boundary case

For \(g(x)\le0\), \(\nabla g\) points outward. A first-order allowed direction \(d\) at the boundary satisfies \(\nabla g(x)^Td\le0\): zero means tangent, negative means inward, and positive means forbidden outward motion.

At a minimum, none of the allowed directions can decrease \(f\). The downhill direction must point exactly outward, with no inward or tangential component. Hence \(-\nabla f=\mu\nabla g\) for \(\mu\ge0\). If the downhill direction pointed inward, we could descend; if it had a tangent component, we could slide along the boundary and descend. Rescaling \(g\) by a positive constant leaves the feasible set unchanged and only rescales \(\mu\).

11.4 The KKT conditions

For differentiable minimization with equalities \(h_j(x)=0\) and inequalities \(g_i(x)\le0\), the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions are:

Theorem 5 (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions) At a local minimum satisfying a suitable constraint qualification, multipliers \(\lambda^\star,\mu^\star\) exist such that - Stationarity: \(\nabla f(x^\star)+\sum_j\lambda_j^\star\nabla h_j(x^\star)+\sum_i\mu_i^\star\nabla g_i(x^\star)=0\). - Primal feasibility: \(h_j(x^\star)=0\) and \(g_i(x^\star)\le0\). - Dual feasibility: \(\mu_i^\star\ge0\). - Complementary slackness: \(\mu_i^\star g_i(x^\star)=0\) for each inequality.

The Lagrangian can be written \(L=f+\lambda^Th+\mu^Tg\). Each condition has its own job:

Condition Meaning
Stationarity The objective slope is balanced by constraint normals
Primal feasibility The point obeys the original constraints
Dual feasibility Inequality multipliers have the permitted sign
Complementary slackness An inequality cannot have both slack and a nonzero multiplier

A common condition guaranteeing multipliers exist is LICQ: the equality gradients and active inequality gradients are linearly independent. Without a constraint qualification, KKT can fail even at a true optimum.

11.5 Complementary slackness

For a feasible point, the constraint slack is \(s_i=-g_i(x)\ge0\). Since \(\mu_i\ge0\), the product condition \(\mu_i s_i=0\) means at least one must be zero:

  • If \(g_i<0\), the constraint has room and \(\mu_i=0\): it exerts no force.
  • If \(\mu_i>0\), then \(g_i=0\): the boundary is tight.
  • Both can be zero: the point lies on the boundary, but the constraint need not push. This is weak activity.

For multiple constraints, \(\sum_i\mu_i g_i=0\) is equivalent to each product being zero because every product is nonpositive. One way to derive the product condition is to introduce a slack variable and write \(g_i(x)+s_i^2=0\). Stationarity in \(s_i\) gives \(2\mu_i s_i=0\).

11.6 Solving KKT by enumerating active sets

For a few inequalities, guess which are active, solve each case, and reject candidates that fail a KKT condition. For \(\min(x-3)^2\) subject to \(x\le2\), write \(g=x-2\le0\):

  1. Inactive: \(\mu=0\) gives \(x=3\), but then \(g=1>0\), so reject.
  2. Active: \(x=2\). Stationarity \(2(x-3)+\mu=0\) gives \(\mu=2\ge0\), so accept.

For \[ \min (x_1-2)^2+(x_2-2)^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad x_1+x_2-2\le0, \] the inactive candidate \((2,2)\) violates the constraint. In the active case, stationarity gives \[ 2(x_1-2)+\mu=0,\qquad2(x_2-2)+\mu=0. \] Thus \(x_1=x_2\), and \(x_1+x_2=2\) gives \(x^\star=(1,1)\) and \(\mu^\star=2\). It is feasible, the multiplier is nonnegative, and \(\mu g=0\). Its objective value is 2. With \(m\) inequalities there can be \(2^m\) active patterns, so hand enumeration is useful only for small problems.

11.7 Necessity and sufficiency

With a suitable constraint qualification, KKT is necessary at a local optimum. In a nonconvex problem it is not generally sufficient; a KKT point can be a maximum or saddle.

For a convex problem—convex objective, convex inequality constraints, affine equalities—KKT is sufficient for global optimality. Under a condition such as Slater’s condition, which requires a strictly feasible point for the inequalities, KKT is also necessary.

12 Lagrangian Duality

12.1 Dual function and dual problem

For fixed multipliers, the dual function is the infimum of the Lagrangian over all \(x\), with no primal constraints imposed:

\[ q(\lambda,\mu)=\inf_{x\in\mathbb R^n}L(x,\lambda,\mu). \]

The dual problem chooses multipliers to make that lower bound as large as possible:

\[ d^\star=\sup_{\lambda,\ \mu\ge0}q(\lambda,\mu). \]

The original constrained problem is the primal, with optimum value \(p^\star\). Weak duality says \(d^\star\le p^\star\). The primal constraints are folded into the Lagrangian, not imposed on the inner minimization defining \(q\).

12.2 Weak duality

Theorem 6 (Weak duality) For any feasible \(x\) and any multipliers with \(\mu\ge0\), \(q(\lambda,\mu)\le f(x)\). Therefore \(d^\star\le p^\star\).

The reason is: at a feasible point, \(h(x)=0\), \(g_i(x)\le0\), and \(\mu_i\ge0\), so \(L(x,\lambda,\mu)\le f(x)\). The infimum of \(L\) over all \(x\) is no larger than its value at this point. Thus every dual value is a lower bound on every feasible objective value.

12.3 Example

Consider \(\min x^2\) subject to \(x\ge1\). Use \(g=1-x\le0\). The primal optimum is \(p^\star=1\) at \(x^\star=1\). The Lagrangian is \(L=x^2+\mu(1-x)\), with \(\mu\ge0\). For fixed \(\mu\), minimize over unrestricted \(x\) to get \(x=\mu/2\). Substitution gives

\[ q(\mu)=\frac{\mu^2}{4}+\mu\left(1-\frac{\mu}{2}\right) =\mu-\frac{\mu^2}{4}. \]

\(\mu\) Inner minimizer \(x=\mu/2\) \(q(\mu)\)
0 0, primal-infeasible 0
1 0.5 0.75
2 1 1
3 1.5 0.75
4 2 0

The dual values never exceed 1. Their maximum is 1 at \(\mu=2\), matching the primal optimum. This is strong duality. At the best multiplier, the unrestricted inner minimizer happens to satisfy the primal boundary.

12.4 Properties

  • The dual function is always concave. For each fixed \(x\), the Lagrangian is affine in its multipliers, and a pointwise infimum of affine functions is concave.
  • Maximizing a concave function over \(\mu\ge0\) is a convex optimization problem, even when the primal is not convex.
  • Strong duality, \(d^\star=p^\star\), often holds for convex problems under mild assumptions such as Slater’s condition.
  • For a feasible \(x\), the gap \(f(x)-q(\lambda,\mu)\) bounds how far the candidate could be from optimality.
  • At a primal-dual optimum with zero gap, the primal point minimizes the Lagrangian, giving stationarity; the inequality contribution must vanish, giving complementary slackness.

Dual feasibility means \(\mu\ge0\), while equality multipliers are unrestricted. Complementary slackness links optimal primal and dual solutions; it is not an extra constraint in the dual maximization itself.

13 Dual Example: Quadratic Objective with a Perturbed Constraint Vector

Suppose the vector in an equality constraint is only approximately known. We may choose a perturbation \(\delta\), but its squared length is limited:

\[ \min_{x,\delta}x^TAx \quad\text{subject to}\quad (c_0+\delta)^Tx=1,\qquad\|\delta\|^2\le\varepsilon,\qquad A\succ0. \]

Both \(x\) and \(\delta\) are decision variables. Introduce an unrestricted multiplier \(\lambda\) for the equality and \(\mu\ge0\) for the inequality:

\[ L=x^TAx+\lambda\big((c_0+\delta)^Tx-1\big) +\mu(\delta^T\delta-\varepsilon). \]

If the original constraint were \(\|\delta\|\le\varepsilon\), its squared form would instead be \(\|\delta\|^2\le\varepsilon^2\).

13.1 Stationarity

Differentiate with respect to each choice:

Term Gradient in \(x\) Gradient in \(\delta\)
\(x^TAx\) \(2Ax\) \(0\)
\(\lambda(c_0+\delta)^Tx\) \(\lambda(c_0+\delta)\) \(\lambda x\)
\(\mu(\delta^T\delta-\varepsilon)\) \(0\) \(2\mu\delta\)

Thus \[ 2Ax+\lambda(c_0+\delta)=0,\qquad \lambda x+2\mu\delta=0. \]

13.2 Why the norm constraint is active

Complementary slackness says either \(\mu=0\) or \(\|\delta\|^2=\varepsilon\). If \(\mu=0\), the second stationarity equation gives \(\lambda x=0\). The case \(x=0\) violates the equality constraint. The case \(\lambda=0\) makes the first equation \(Ax=0\), again forcing \(x=0\). Both are impossible, so \(\mu>0\) and the perturbation budget is fully used.

This argument does not say that \(\mu=0\) implies \(\delta=0\). With \(\mu=0\), \(\delta\) simply disappears from the second stationarity equation; the contradiction uses feasibility and the first equation too.

13.3 The optimal perturbation is aligned with \(x\)

For \(\mu>0\), stationarity in \(\delta\) gives \[ \delta=-\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}x. \] Multiply the first stationarity equation by \(x^T\) and use \((c_0+\delta)^Tx=1\): \[ 2x^TAx+\lambda=0. \] Because \(A\succ0\) and \(x\ne0\), \(\lambda=-2x^TAx<0\). Hence \(-\lambda/(2\mu)>0\): \(\delta\) points in the same direction as \(x\). Since the constraint is active, \[ \delta^\star=\sqrt\varepsilon\,\frac{x^\star}{\|x^\star\|}. \]

The geometry agrees: for fixed \(\|x\|\) and \(\|\delta\|\), the largest \(\delta^Tx\) occurs when they point in the same direction. That helps satisfy the equality with a smaller \(x\), reducing the quadratic cost.

13.4 Closed forms and the dual function

Substituting \(\delta=-\lambda x/(2\mu)\) into the first equation gives \[ \left(2A-\frac{\lambda^2}{2\mu}I\right)x=-\lambda c_0. \] Define \(M=A-\lambda^2I/(4\mu)\). When invertible, \[ x(\lambda,\mu)=-\frac{\lambda}{2}M^{-1}c_0,\qquad \delta(\lambda,\mu)=-\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}x(\lambda,\mu). \]

At this stationary point, using the stationarity equation to simplify the Lagrangian yields \[ q(\lambda,\mu) =-\frac{\lambda^2}{4}c_0^T \left(A-\frac{\lambda^2}{4\mu}I\right)^{-1}c_0 -\lambda-\mu\varepsilon. \]

The joint Hessian of \(L\) in \((x,\delta)\) is \[ \begin{bmatrix}2A&\lambda I\\\lambda I&2\mu I\end{bmatrix}. \] A convenient region where it is positive definite is \(\mu>0\) and \(M\succ0\), equivalently \(\mu>\lambda^2/(4\lambda_{\min}(A))\). If it has a negative-curvature direction, the infimum is \(-\infty\). At a singular boundary, finiteness needs a separate check on the nullspace; numerical work can stay inside the positive-definite region.

Imposing the two primal constraints on the stationary formulas gives nonlinear equations for the two multipliers: \[ \left(\frac{\lambda^2}{4\mu}\right)^2c_0^TM^{-2}c_0=\varepsilon,\qquad c_0^TM^{-1}c_0=-\frac2\lambda-\frac{4\varepsilon\mu}{\lambda^2}. \] These can be solved numerically and the candidates checked for feasibility and positive curvature. With \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\), \(c_0=(1,1)^T\), and \(\varepsilon=0.04\), the minimum is approximately \(0.58797\) with \(\|\delta^\star\|=0.2\). Without uncertainty, it is \(0.8\). The equality is bilinear in \(x,\delta\), so the primal is nonconvex; zero duality gap is possible in this example but is not guaranteed generally.

This model is optimistic, not robust: it lets the optimizer choose the most helpful perturbation. Robust optimization instead protects against every allowed perturbation, typically using a min–max formulation.

14 Worked Problem: The Minimum-Variance Estimator and the Capon Spectrum

Consider observations \(X=s+N\), where \(s\) is a known signal and \(N\) is zero-mean noise. We want a linear filter \(h\) that preserves the signal with unit gain while minimizing output power:

\[ \min_h E[|h^HX|^2] \quad\text{subject to}\quad h^Hs=1. \]

The condition \(h^Hs=1\) means that the known signal passes through the filter unchanged. The assumptions are \(E[N]=0\) and the noise covariance \(K_N=E[NN^H]\) is Hermitian positive definite.

If the signal is complex, the objective must be the squared magnitude \(|h^HX|^2\). The square \((h^HX)^2\) could be complex and therefore would not be an ordered quantity that we can minimize.

14.1 Rewriting the objective; why the name fits

Use \(|u|^2=u^*u\) and \((h^HX)^*=X^Hh\):

\[ E[|h^HX|^2]=h^HE[XX^H]h. \]

Because \(X=s+N\) and the noise has zero mean, the cross terms vanish, giving

\[ R=E[XX^H]=ss^H+K_N. \]

Thus

\[ h^HRh=|h^Hs|^2+h^HK_Nh. \]

On the feasible set, \(h^Hs=1\), so the signal contribution is fixed at 1. Minimizing total output power is equivalent to minimizing output noise power. The output \(h^HX\) has mean 1 and variance \(h^HK_Nh\), which explains the name minimum variance.

14.2 (a) The optimal filter

Since \(K_N\succ0\), the noise-power objective is strictly convex. The linear constraint is affine, so there is one unique global minimizer.

Form a real-valued Lagrangian for the complex constraint:

\[ L=h^HK_Nh+\lambda(h^Hs-1)+\lambda^*(s^Hh-1). \]

The first constraint term depends on \(h^*\); the conjugate term depends on \(h\). Differentiating with respect to \(h^*\) gives

\[ \nabla_{h^*}L=K_Nh+\lambda s=0, \qquad h=-\lambda K_N^{-1}s. \]

Now impose \(h^Hs=1\). Since \(K_N^{-1}\) is Hermitian,

\[ -\lambda^*s^HK_N^{-1}s=1. \]

The scalar \(s^HK_N^{-1}s\) is positive real, so the resulting normalized filter is

\[ \boxed{h^\star=\frac{K_N^{-1}s}{s^HK_N^{-1}s}}. \]

There is also a short proof that this is globally optimal. For every feasible \(h\), Cauchy–Schwarz gives

\[ \begin{aligned} 1=|h^Hs|^2 &=\left|(K_N^{1/2}h)^H(K_N^{-1/2}s)\right|^2\\ &\le (h^HK_Nh)(s^HK_N^{-1}s). \end{aligned} \]

Therefore \(h^HK_Nh\ge1/(s^HK_N^{-1}s)\). Equality holds precisely when \(h\) is proportional to \(K_N^{-1}s\), and the constraint fixes the proportionality constant. One interpretation is that \(K_N^{-1/2}\) whitens the noise; after whitening, the matched filter is optimal.

Sometimes we know only the total covariance \(R\), not the separate noise covariance. We can still use it: the matrix inversion lemma gives

\[ R^{-1}s=(K_N+ss^H)^{-1}s =\frac{K_N^{-1}s}{1+s^HK_N^{-1}s}. \]

This is a scalar multiple of \(K_N^{-1}s\), so after normalization the same filter can be written as

\[ h^\star=\frac{R^{-1}s}{s^HR^{-1}s}. \]

14.3 (b) Variance and power

Let \(a=s^HK_N^{-1}s\). Substituting the optimal filter,

\[ \operatorname{Var}(h^{\star H}X) =h^{\star H}K_Nh^\star =\frac1a =\frac1{s^HK_N^{-1}s}. \]

The mean-square output power also includes the unit signal power:

\[ E[|h^{\star H}X|^2]=1+\frac1a =\frac1{s^HR^{-1}s}. \]

Mean-square power and variance differ because the output mean is 1. If instead \(X=As+N\) with unknown amplitude \(A\), then \(h^{\star H}X=A+h^{\star H}N\) is an unbiased estimate of \(A\) with variance \(1/a\). It is the best linear unbiased estimator, and under Gaussian noise it is the minimum-variance unbiased estimator.

For white noise, \(K_N=\sigma^2I\), and if every entry of \(s\) has magnitude 1, then \(a=N/\sigma^2\). The variance is \(\sigma^2/N\), the familiar reduction in noise from averaging \(N\) independent observations.

14.4 (c) The minimum-variance (Capon) spectrum

For spectral estimation, let the target signal be a complex sinusoid at frequency \(f\):

\[ e(f)= \begin{bmatrix} 1&e^{j2\pi f}&e^{j2\pi 2f}&\cdots&e^{j2\pi(N-1)f} \end{bmatrix}^T. \]

The constraint \(h^He(f)=1\) says that a sinusoid at frequency \(f\) passes with unit gain. The filter minimizes output power while preserving that sinusoid, suppressing as much as possible of other components and leakage. The resulting output power is the Capon or minimum-variance spectrum:

\[ h_f=\frac{K_X^{-1}e(f)}{e^H(f)K_X^{-1}e(f)}, \qquad \boxed{S_X^{MV}(f)=\frac{1}{e^H(f)K_X^{-1}e(f)}}. \]

In practice \(K_X\) is estimated from data snapshots, for example

\[ \widehat K_X=\frac1L\sum_{\ell=1}^Lx_\ell x_\ell^H. \]

The inverse is reliable only if there is enough data relative to the filter length; otherwise regularization or a shorter filter may be needed.

For one tone of power \(P\) in white noise, \(K_X=\sigma^2I+Pe_0e_0^H\). The matrix inversion lemma gives

\[ e^HK_X^{-1}e =\frac1{\sigma^2}\left[N-\frac{P|e^He_0|^2}{\sigma^2+PN}\right]. \]

At the tone frequency this yields \(S^{MV}(f_0)=P+\sigma^2/N\); away from the tone it is approximately \(\sigma^2/N\). The tone is captured while the noise contribution is averaged over an effective bandwidth of roughly \(1/N\). Some conventions multiply by \(N\) to report a spectral density.

The conventional Fourier-like choice \(h=e(f)/N\) does not adapt to the covariance. The Capon filter uses \(K_X^{-1}\) to suppress interference and can resolve nearby tones more sharply when the covariance estimate is accurate. For example, with two equal tones at 0.20 and 0.23 cycles/sample, \(N=16\), and 20 dB signal-to-noise ratio per tone, the spacing is smaller than \(1/N\):

Frequency examined Conventional filter Minimum-variance filter
At either tone about \(+1.6\) dB; the tones merge about \(0\) dB; separate peaks
Between tones, at 0.215 about \(+2.2\) dB; a single merged peak about \(-11.2\) dB; a dip between peaks
Away from tones leakage floor around \(-13\) to \(-21\) dB about \(-32\) dB, the white-noise floor \(\sigma^2/N\)

This comparison uses the true covariance, so it illustrates the method’s potential rather than the additional error from estimating covariance with finite data. The adaptive filter can place a null on the neighboring tone, while the conventional Fourier filter has fixed weights.

15 Practice Problems

Try each problem before opening its solution. The solutions show the main reasoning, not just the final number.

Problem 1 — Classify stationary points. Find and classify the stationary points of \(f(x)=x^4-2x^2\). Then use the higher-order test to classify \(x^3\) and \(x^6\) at zero.

TipSolution

Differentiate: \(f'=4x^3-4x=4x(x^2-1)\), so the stationary points are \(0\) and \(\pm1\). The second derivative is \(f''=12x^2-4\). At zero, \(f''=-4\), so zero is a local maximum with value 0. At \(\pm1\), \(f''=8\), so they are local minima; since \(f\to+\infty\) as \(|x|\to\infty\), they are global minima, with value \(-1\).

For \(x^3\), the first nonzero derivative at zero is third order, which is odd; the function changes sign across zero, giving a stationary inflection. For \(x^6\), the first nonzero derivative is sixth order and positive (\(f^{(6)}(0)=720\)), so zero is a strict minimum.

Problem 2 — Wirtinger derivatives. For real \(\beta\ne\pm1\), find stationary points and classify them for (a) \(|z-a|^2\), (b) \(|z|^2+\frac{\beta}{2}(z^2+z^{*2})\), and (c) \(z^HAz-2\operatorname{Re}\{b^Hz\}\) with Hermitian \(A\succ0\). Find the minimum value in (c).

TipSolution

  1. Write \(f=(z-a)(z^*-a^*)\). Then \(\partial f/\partial z^*=z-a\), so the stationary point is \(z=a\), where the squared distance is minimized.

  2. Differentiating with respect to \(z^*\) gives \(z+\beta z^*\). Since \(z=x+jy\), this equals \((1+\beta)x+j(1-\beta)y\). For \(\beta\ne\pm1\), both real coordinates must be zero, so the stationary point is \(z=0\). In real coordinates the objective is \((1+\beta)x^2+(1-\beta)y^2\): it is a strict minimum for \(|\beta|<1\) and a saddle for \(|\beta|>1\). Notice that the mixed derivative is 1 in every case; it alone does not detect the saddle.

  3. Expand the real term: \(-2\operatorname{Re}\{b^Hz\}=-(b^Hz+z^Hb)\). Then \(\nabla_{z^*}f=Az-b\). The stationary point is \(z^\star=A^{-1}b\). Substituting gives the minimum value \(-b^HA^{-1}b\).

Problem 3 — Gradient and level sets. For \(f=x_1^2+4x_2^2\), verify that the gradient at \((1,\frac12)\) is perpendicular to the level curve through that point.

TipSolution

The level value is \(1+4(1/4)=2\). Parametrize the ellipse by \(r(t)=(\sqrt2\cos t,\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\sin t)\). At \(t=45^\circ\), the point is \((1,\frac12)\) and the tangent is \(r'(t)=(-1,\frac12)\). The gradient is \(\nabla f=(2x_1,8x_2)=(2,4)\). Their dot product is \((2,4)\cdot(-1,\frac12)=-2+2=0\).

Problem 4 — Least squares through the origin. Fit \(y=ax\) to \((1,2),(2,3),(3,7)\).

TipSolution

Here \(\Phi=(1,2,3)^T\) and \(Y=(2,3,7)^T\). The normal equation is \((\Phi^T\Phi)a=\Phi^TY\), so \(14a=29\) and \(a=29/14\approx2.071\). The residuals are approximately \((-0.071,-1.143,0.786)\). Their inner product with \(\Phi\) is approximately \(-0.071-2.286+2.357=0\), as the orthogonality principle predicts.

Problem 5 — Equality constraint, sensitivity, and second order. Solve \(\min x_1^2+4x_2^2\) subject to \(x_1+x_2=1\). Verify \(dp^\star/d\varepsilon=-\lambda^\star\) when the right side becomes \(1+\varepsilon\), and check the second-order condition.

TipSolution

The Lagrangian is \(L=x_1^2+4x_2^2+\lambda(x_1+x_2-1)\). Stationarity gives \(2x_1+\lambda=0\) and \(8x_2+\lambda=0\), hence \(x_1=4x_2\). Combining with the constraint gives \(x^\star=(0.8,0.2)\) and \(\lambda^\star=-1.6\). The minimum value is \(0.8\).

If the right side is \(1+\varepsilon\), the solution scales to \((1+\varepsilon)(0.8,0.2)\), so \(p^\star(\varepsilon)=0.8(1+\varepsilon)^2\). Its derivative at zero is \(1.6=-\lambda^\star\).

The Hessian of the Lagrangian is \(\operatorname{diag}(2,8)\). Tangent directions have the form \((t,-t)\), and their curvature is \(2t^2+8t^2=10t^2>0\) for \(t\ne0\). The point is a strict constrained minimum.

Problem 6 — KKT by cases. Solve \(\min (x_1-2)^2+(x_2-2)^2\) subject to \(x_1+x_2\le2\). Check all four KKT conditions.

TipSolution

Write \(g=x_1+x_2-2\le0\). If the constraint were inactive, the unconstrained minimizer would be \((2,2)\), but there \(g=2>0\), so it is infeasible. Therefore the constraint must be active. Stationarity gives \(2(x_1-2)+\mu=0\) and \(2(x_2-2)+\mu=0\), so \(x_1=x_2\). Together with \(x_1+x_2=2\), this gives \(x^\star=(1,1)\) and \(\mu^\star=2\).

Stationarity holds by substitution; primal feasibility gives \(g=0\); dual feasibility gives \(\mu=2\ge0\); complementary slackness gives \(\mu g=0\). The minimum objective value is 2.

Problem 7 — Derive and solve a dual problem. Find the dual of \(\min x_1^2+x_2^2\) subject to \(x_1+x_2\ge2\).

TipSolution

Put the inequality in standard form: \(g=2-x_1-x_2\le0\). The Lagrangian is \(L=x_1^2+x_2^2+\mu(2-x_1-x_2)\), with \(\mu\ge0\). Minimizing over \(x\) gives \(x_i=\mu/2\). Substitution gives the dual function \(q(\mu)=2\mu-\mu^2/2\). It is maximized at \(\mu^\star=2\), with dual value 2.

The primal optimum is \(x^\star=(1,1)\) with value 2. The values agree, so there is no duality gap. The multiplier also satisfies stationarity: \(2x_i-\mu=0\).

Problem 8 — Complex least squares. Derive the normal equations for \(\min_z\|y-Hz\|^2\) using \(\nabla_{z^*}\). Show why differentiating with respect to \(z\) requires one more conjugation.

TipSolution

Expand: \[ J=y^Hy-y^HHz-z^HH^Hy+z^HH^HHz. \] Differentiating with respect to \(z^*\) gives \(\nabla_{z^*}J=-H^Hy+H^HHz\). Set it to zero to get \(H^HHz=H^Hy\).

Differentiating with respect to \(z\) instead produces the row expression \(-(y-Hz)^HH\). Taking its conjugate transpose yields the same column equation. The \(z^*\) derivative gives the natural column form directly.

Problem 9 — LCMV with \(A=I\). Find the minimum-norm vector \(z\) satisfying \(z_1+z_2+z_3=1\) and \(z_1-z_2=0\).

TipSolution

Use \(C=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\1&-1&0\end{bmatrix}\) and \(c=(1,0)^T\). Then \(CC^H=\operatorname{diag}(3,2)\), so \((CC^H)^{-1}c=(1/3,0)^T\). The minimum-norm solution is \[ z=C^H(CC^H)^{-1}c=\frac13(1,1,1)^T. \] It satisfies both constraints, and its squared norm is \(1/3\).

Problem 10 — Minimum variance in white noise. Let \(K_N=\sigma^2I\) and let the steering vector \(s=e(f)\). Find the optimal filter, its output variance, and the minimum-variance spectrum. Compare it with the conventional filter.

TipSolution

Since \(e^He=N\), the filter is \(h^\star=e/N\), the normalized matched filter. The output variance is \(\sigma^2/N\), and \[ S^{MV}(f)=\frac{1}{e^H(\sigma^{-2}I)e}=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}. \] In white noise the conventional and minimum-variance filters coincide. They differ when the covariance is not proportional to the identity, because then covariance weighting can suppress noise or interference more effectively.

16 Summary

16.1 Optimality conditions at a glance

Setting First-order condition Second-order sufficient condition
Unconstrained \(\nabla f=0\) \(\nabla^2f\succ0\)
Equality constraints \(\nabla f+\nabla g\,\lambda=0\), \(g=0\) (under LICQ) positive Lagrangian curvature in every nonzero tangent direction
Inequality constraints KKT: stationarity, feasibility, \(\mu\ge0\), complementary slackness positive Lagrangian curvature on nonzero critical directions, with suitable regularity
Convex problem KKT is necessary and sufficient under a suitable constraint qualification a separate local second-order test is not needed to establish global optimality

16.2 Rules used throughout

  1. Transpose reverses products: \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\). A scalar equals its transpose.
  2. \(\nabla(c^Tx)=c\) and \(\nabla(x^TAx)=(A+A^T)x\).
  3. The gradient is the coefficient vector of the linear change: \(f(x+h)=f(x)+g^Th+o(\|h\|)\).
  4. For real \(f(z,z^*)\), the useful complex descent direction is \(-\nabla_{z^*}f\).
  5. A complex equality contributes a real term such as \(2\operatorname{Re}\{\lambda^Hg\}\) to the Lagrangian.
  6. Use one multiplier per constraint. Equality multipliers are unrestricted; inequality multipliers satisfy \(\mu\ge0\) when minimizing with constraints \(g\le0\).
  7. Convert maximization to minimization before applying the standard KKT sign convention.
  8. In numerical work, solve linear systems; prefer QR or SVD when conditioning is a concern.

16.3 Pitfalls to remember

  • Strictly positive curvature is sufficient for a strict minimum; nonnegative curvature is only necessary and may be inconclusive.
  • Strict convexity gives at most one minimizer, but does not guarantee that a minimizer exists.
  • \(\partial f/\partial z\) can test stationarity, but the practical complex descent direction is \(-\partial f/\partial z^*\).
  • The matrix \(A^{-1}\) in \(A^{-1}c/(c^TA^{-1}c)\) cannot generally be canceled.
  • A nonlinear equality constraint can make the feasible set nonconvex, even when both objective and constraint function are strictly convex.
  • If LICQ fails, multipliers may not exist or may not be unique at an optimum.
  • The dual function uses a global minimization over the primal variable. A stationary point is enough only when it truly minimizes the Lagrangian.
  • Choosing an uncertainty \(\delta\) to help the objective is optimistic; robust optimization must protect against every allowed perturbation.
