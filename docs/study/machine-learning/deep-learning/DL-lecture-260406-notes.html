Challenges in Neural Network Optimization and First Derivative-Based Techniques (Momentum, Initialization)

Deep Learning Notes — Optimization II

Deep Learning
Optimization
Momentum
Initialization
Reasons why Newton’s method is not used in deep learning, the 8 challenges of neural network optimization (ill-conditioning, saddle point, cliff, surrogate loss, etc.), and a summary of Momentum/Nesterov Momentum and parameter initialization (Xavier init), the core ingredients of first-derivative-based optimization.
Published

April 6, 2026

ImportantOne-line summary of the key points of this note

The solution space of a neural network is ill-conditioned, full of saddle points and cliffs, and even the gradients we use are noisy. Therefore, we give up on finding the global optimum and instead employ practical devices such as momentum, good initialization, and learning rate scheduling to find a sufficiently good local minimum. This is the essence of first-derivative-based deep learning optimization.

1 Overall Structure Overview

This note consists of two parts.

  1. Why Neural Network Optimization Is Difficult — Limitations of Newton’s Method and 8 Challenges
  2. So What Do We Use — Materials for First-Derivative-Based Optimization: Momentum, Nesterov Momentum, Parameter Initialization

2 Starting Point Review: ERM and GD vs. SGD

The real goal is to minimize the risk for the data generation distribution \(p_{\text{data}}\).

\[ J^*(\theta) = \min_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\, L\big(f(x;\theta),\, y\big) \right] \]

However, since we cannot have data from the entire world, we instead minimize the empirical risk for the experience distribution \(\hat{p}_{\text{data}}\) of the training set (ERM, Empirical Risk Minimization).

\[ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim \hat{p}_{\text{data}}}\!\left[ L\big(f(x;\theta), y\big)\right] = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} L\big(f(x^{(i)};\theta),\, y^{(i)}\big) \]

Gradient Descent (GD) Stochastic GD (SGD)
gradient calculation unit total data \(m\) mini-batch \(k\)
gradient quality stable, low noise unstable, noisy
Important gradient Can cancel each other out Likely to be preserved
Computation/Memory Cost High (Requires Full Load) Low → Practical Standard
Theoretical properties Clear Less clear
local minimum escape confidently dive in sometimes escape thanks to randomness
NoteTwo additional arguments in favor of SGD
  1. The solution space contains many local minima. Even if the loss function is convex, the shape of the solution space is determined not by the loss but by the data distribution. Gradient Descent (GD) proceeds directly toward the local minimum with certainty, whereas the randomness of SGD provides an opportunity to escape, much like a random walk.
  2. Parallelization is easy. Multiple batches can be computed on multiple machines and aggregated.

3 Newton’s method: Theoretically optimal, discarded in practice

3.1 Idea: 2nd-order Taylor approximation

If we Taylor expand loss \(f(x)\) to the second order and discard the higher-order terms, the optimal step can be obtained in a closed form.

\[ f(x) \approx f(x_0) + (x - x_0)^\top g + \frac{1}{2}(x - x_0)^\top H (x - x_0) \]

  • \(g\): 1st derivative (gradient)
  • \(H\): 2nd derivative matrix (Hessian)

Differentiate this with respect to \(x\) and set it to 0 to get an update.

\[ x^{\ast} = x_0 - H^{-1} g \]

In other words, the curvature information indicates the optimal stride length without the need to tune the step size separately. For a convex objective, the global optimal point is reached with fewer iterations.

3.2 Why isn’t it used in deep learning?

WarningThree reasons why Newton’s method fails
  1. Computation Cost: If there are \(n\) parameters, the Hessian is \(n \times n\). For hundreds of millions to hundreds of billions of parameters, computation, storage, and inverse matrix operations are nearly impossible.
  2. The surface is not smooth: For very complex loss surfaces, the second-order approximation itself does not provide a meaningful step. Even if calculated expensively, it is not the optimal step.
  3. Dragged toward saddle points: Newton’s method points toward critical points where the gradient is zero. However, most critical points on high-dimensional surfaces are saddle points (see Section 4.3 below). If the signs of the eigenvalues of the Hessian are mixed, the extremum can be a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle point.

The classification of critical points is determined by the Hessian eigenvalue sign.

Hessian eigenvalue type of critical point
All positive (positive definite) local minimum
All negative (negative definite) local maximum
Mixed positive and negative numbers saddle point

4 8 Challenges of Neural Network Optimization

4.1 1. Ill-conditioning

Condition number is the ratio of the largest eigenvalue to the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix \(A\).

\[ \kappa(A) = \max_{i,j}\left|\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_j}\right| = \frac{|\lambda_{\max}|}{|\lambda_{\min}|} \]

Here, \(A\) is typically considered the covariance matrix of the feature space (or the Hessian of the loss surface). Eigenvalues indicate how spread the data is in the direction of each eigenvector.

  • \(\kappa \approx 1\): Data spreads isotropically → Similar step sizes work in either direction → Easy optimization
  • \(\kappa \gg 1\) (e.g., \(10^4\)): Wide distribution in some directions, dense distribution in others → step size becomes extremely sensitive

Mechanism of step size sensitivity:

  1. If the step is large relative to the dense (high curvature) direction → oscillates by skiping the minimum
  2. If the step is small relative to the spreading (small curvature) direction → stops before reaching the minimum

A situation where two directions cannot be satisfied simultaneously with a single learning rate is ill-conditioning.

ImportantThe higher the dimension, the default ill-conditioning

As dimensionality increases, the number of eigenvalue combinations increases, making it highly likely that the ratio \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) will grow. Since most real-world data is high-dimensional, it is safe to assume that the solution space in practice is almost always ill-conditioned.

4.2 2. Local minima — Difficult to determine convergence

Deep neural networks may not converge. The reasons:

  • There may be no local minimums at all. If there is a single global minimum and the rest is a series of plateaus, optimization wanders without a reference point to stop and eventually stops.
  • “stuck” state: A state where parameters continue to move even though the loss is not decreasing. Candidate causes are ① poor conditioning, ② excessive gradient noise (SGD), ③ overfitting (especially with small training data), and ④ other unknown causes.
  • It is common to stop before reaching a critical point. When using scheduling that gradually reduces the learning rate, if the learning rate becomes too small before entering a local minimum, training ends at an intermediate point. This happens very frequently in practice.
WarningYou cannot tell “whether it has converged well” based solely on the training loss curve

Even by examining the loss value and the optimization trajectory, it is impossible to determine whether the current stopping point is a good minimum. Whether the training was effective is ultimately revealed only when evaluated on test/validation data. It is difficult to know during training.

4.3 3. Saddle point, plateau — are they common? Unfortunately, yes.

Coin Toss Argument: Let us toss a coin for each eigenvalue of the Hessian.

  • Heads → Its eigenvalue is positive
  • Back side → Negative

For it to be a local minimum, all eigenvalues must be positive (all heads). As the dimension \(n\) increases, the probability of all heads becomes exponentially rare as \(\sim (1/2)^n\). Saddle points where one direction is a minimum and another is a maximum become the overwhelming majority of critical points.

Correction for Random Matrix Theory: In fact, this coin is not fair and is weighted. The lower the loss value \(J\), the higher the probability of getting heads (positive eigenvalues). Therefore:

  • Critical point with low \(J\) → Likely a local minimum
  • critical point with high \(J\) → mostly saddle point
TipPractical Implications

The worry about “getting stuck in a local minimum” is less fatal than expected. This is because reachable local minimums are clustered in areas with low losses anyway. The real problem is the saddle points scattered across high-loss regions, and Newtonian methods are dragged into these very things — this is another reason why first-order methods (+noise) are preferred.

Behavior near the saddle point: It appears to converge by showing a minimum in one direction, but the moment the gradient in the other direction increases, it rolls down into a different valley. At this point, one can observe the loss suddenly soaring.

4.4 4. Cliff and exploding gradient → gradient clipping

On the loss surface, there exists a cliff — a point where the loss drops or spikes. If the step size is sufficiently large, it jumps over the cliff and falls into an absurdly high loss region, causing the loss to spike significantly in just a single iteration, making it appear as though the training is broken.

  • Simple solution: Reduce the learning rate → However, overall training slows down. An unwanted trade-off.
  • Heuristic Solution: Gradient Clipping. Clips the gradient if its norm exceeds a threshold.

\[ g \leftarrow g \cdot \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\tau}{\lVert g \rVert}\right) \]

It interprets an abnormally large gradient as a signal that one is currently traversing highly irregular terrain, forcibly restricts stride length, and descends cautiously. Although it started as an absurdly simple heuristic, its excellent practical performance has made it a standard technique.

4.5 5. Long-term dependency (vanishing gradient)

As the number of hidden layers increases, the magnitude of the gradient backpropagated from the output to the input decreases exponentially (vanishing gradient). The learning signal generated in the output layer is diluted as it passes through multiple layers, so the parameters of the layer closest to the input are not effectively learned. Separate mechanisms (skip connection, normalization, etc.) are required to preserve information or reduce noise.

NoteSame name, different axis: long-term dependency of the time axis

What is being referred to here is the dependency (depth axis) between the input and output layers within a single MLP. In sequence data, a separate long-term dependency exists along the time axis — to understand a long paragraph, one must remember and connect the meanings of the preceding words and sentences.

Modern Transformers store this contextual information in the KV cache, but this consumes GPU memory explosively as the sequence becomes longer. While compression typically results in information loss, recent techniques have emerged that compress data by approximately six times without (or with minimal) loss, significantly improving cache efficiency. This topic will be covered again in the section on sequence modeling.

4.6 6. Inexact gradient → surrogate loss

The stochastic gradient used by SGD is not a true gradient. It is a noisy estimate that has only “tasted” a portion of the data called a mini-batch. This noise can lead optimization astray.

Solution: surrogate loss. Instead of the original complex loss surface, define a surrogate loss that is ① similar to it and ② much smoother and easier to optimize, and optimize on it.

  • The minimum of the surrogate is slightly different from the original minimum, but it stably reaches a point sufficiently close to the original solution with fewer iterations.
  • Since the surface itself is smooth, it eventually converges near the minimum even if the gradient is noisy.
ImportantOpen problem

The core question is “how to design a surrogate loss that is close to the original surface and whose minimum is close to the original solution.” The answer depends on the dataset and the objective function, and there is no general solution. If a good surrogate is found, optimization becomes dramatically easier, even when using noisy gradients. (Using cross-entropy instead of 0-1 loss in classification is a typical example of a surrogate loss.)

4.7 7. Inconsistency between local and global structures

1-Dimensional Thought Experiment: Let’s assume that the loss is increasing locally in a certain parameter range. From the perspective of looking only at the gradient, there are various possible interpretations.

  1. “The current valley is a good local minimum, and we must not go over the hill” → Stop here
  2. “Overfitting has started” → Stop training
  3. “There is a much deeper minimum beyond the hill” → A better year if you bravely cross over

The problem is that local observations alone cannot distinguish the three. Because local information provides poor correspondence regarding the global structure, the decision to stop or continue while monitoring the learning curve is inherently difficult.

4.8 8. Theoretical Limitations of Optimization

In neural network learning, we give up on finding the exact minimum of a function. This is because the surface is too complex to be virtually impossible. Instead, we change our goal:

Reduce the loss value sufficiently to obtain good generalization performance.

Theoretical analysis of “which optimization algorithms can achieve the objective (finding the global minimum)” is extremely difficult. Therefore, deriving a realistic performance bound—for example, a theoretical tool that guarantees the gap between the surrogate optimization result and the true optimal solution—remains an important goal of machine learning research. Creating such a guarantee would represent a meaningful advance in learning on complex surfaces.

5 Optimization Debugging: Path Visualization

One way to find out what SGD is actually doing — whether it is stuck in a local minimum, staying at a saddle point, wasting time circling around global obstacles, or navigating a bumpy canyon — is to visualize the optimal path.

Since direct visualization of high-dimensional space is impossible, low-dimensional projection is used.

Method Content Pros & Cons
2D subspace visualization Projecting lossy surfaces onto a 2D plane to observe them as contours/surfaces High information content / Less common
1D subspace visualization learning curve (loss vs. iteration), interpolation plot Very easy, standard practice / Potential for misjudgment (can mislead whether it is a good minimum or a valley to escape)

5.1 Lessons Learned from Visualization

  1. In most problems, there exists a linear subspace where the loss decreases monotonically. Even a seemingly insanely complex surface can have a simple and even linear underlying subspace.
  2. In some problems, there are obstacles between this subspace and the SGD path. Local ups and downs where the learning curve temporarily stagnates or spikes can often be ignored, and a decreasing trend can eventually be confirmed by observing for a sufficient period.
  3. Factored linear models capture many qualitative aspects of deep neural network learning. Factoring \(W\) of the linear model \(y = Wx + b\)Z into eigenvectors and eigenvalues reveals the linear subspace beneath the complex surface and serves as a tool for analyzing the optimization process.
TipThis section’s punchline

Even if the surface appears complex, the latent space can be simple. This is why learning is possible even though the surface becomes more complex as the model grows larger. Based on the same philosophy, finding a good surrogate loss—since it leverages the simple underlying structure—works very well in practice. (The ripples on the surface are merely caused by data noise and inclusivity, while the fundamental structure is often simple.)

6 Materials for First-Derivative-Based Optimization

Order of derivatives usable in gradient-based methods:

Order Name Remarks
1st gradient Standard
2nd order Hessian Sensitive to small changes in input; determines whether a pole is min or max
3rd order or higher higher-order gradient No practical examples

Second-order information tells us whether “this extremum is a minimum (it is okay to stop) or a maximum (need to go further),” but due to cost issues, first-order based optimization is the standard in deep learning. Three ingredients to stabilize first-order based optimization:

  1. Momentum — Adds inertia to noisy stochastic gradients
  2. Parameter Reset — Start near Good Valley
  3. Learning rate scheduling — What is called an “optimizer” in deep learning libraries (Next note)
NoteA trap named “Optimizer”

The optimizer modules of PyTorch/TensorFlow (SGD, AdaGrad, RMSProp, Adam, …) may look like different optimization algorithms, but they are all essentially stochastic gradient descent. The only difference is how the learning rate is scheduled. Depending on which scheduler is used on the same surface, it may converge quickly, slowly, or not converge at all — it is the factor that effectively determines learning efficiency, which is why it has been given the grandiose name of “optimizer.”

6.1 Momentum

Everyday definition: The force gained by motion (or a series of events) — the momentum in physics \(M = m \cdot v\), i.e., inertia.

Intuition in Machine Learning: At some point, due to bad mini-batch luck, the stochastic gradient may point in the wrong direction. However, it is rare for the downhill direction of a surface to change abruptly in a single step. In that case, instead of relying 100% on the current noisy gradient, let’s trust the direction (history) we have come down so far more — let’s give the gradient inertia.

Momentum algorithm [Polyak, 1964] accumulates the exponentially decaying moving average of past gradients and continues moving in that direction.

\[ \begin{aligned} v &\leftarrow \alpha\, v \;-\; \epsilon\, \nabla_\theta J(\theta) \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta + v \end{aligned} \]

  • \(v\) (velocity): The direction and speed of a parameter as it passes through the parameter space. It is defined as the exponentially damped mean of the negative gradient.
  • \(\alpha \in [0,1)\): hyperparameter. Determines how quickly the contribution of past gradients decays. For example, \(\alpha = 0.8\) implies that 80% of the weight is given to accumulated past velocity and the remainder to new gradients — trusting history more than the current “noisy” gradients.
  • Note: \(\alpha\) is another hyperparameter that must be specified when using momentum, and the results are quite sensitive to this value. It determines “how much inertia to use.”

6.2 Nesterov Momentum

One inefficiency of ordinary momentum: the gradient is calculated at a previous (old) parameter point. However, if it is certain that the vehicle will move to velocity anyway, that gradient is already outdated the moment it is calculated.

Nesterov momentum adds a correction factor — it calculates the gradient at the lookahead point where velocity is applied first.

\[ \begin{aligned} v &\leftarrow \alpha\, v \;-\; \epsilon\, \nabla_\theta J(\theta + \alpha v) \\ \theta &\leftarrow \theta + v \end{aligned} \]

In online training scenarios where parameters are updated live whenever a mini-batch arrives, the gradient is less worn out because it previews the gradient of the “space to be actually evaluated in the next iteration.” In practice, it usually performs better than standard momentum and is used as the default choice.

Ordinary Momentum Nesterov Momentum
gradient evaluation point current \(\theta\) lookahead \(\theta + \alpha v\)
Interpretation Past Average + Current Slope Past Average + Slope of Future Prediction Point
Real-world performance Standard Generally better

6.3 Parameter initialization strategy (excluding bias)

If you start in a good valley, you don’t have to worry about escaping it — initialization is one of the most important elements in machine learning optimization. However, there is no single silver bullet, and there is a collection of heuristics known to work well.

6.3.1 Default options and the “magnitude” problem

  • From which distribution is sampling? A Gaussian distribution or a Uniform distribution of a certain range is the standard.
  • Does magnitude matter? Yes. The magnitude of the initial values affects both ① the results of the optimization procedure and ② the network’s generalization ability. However, there is no single trend like “larger is always better” or “smaller is always better” — it depends on the surface (i.e., the data and the objective function).
  • Prescription for MLP: If there are \(m\) layers of inputs and \(n\) layers of outputs, the following uniform distribution is known to provide a good starting point.

\[ W_{ij} \sim U\!\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}},\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\right) \]

6.3.2 Xavier (Glorot) initialization [Glorot & Bengio, 2010]

Another prescription, normalized initialization (commonly known as Xavier init):

\[ W_{ij} \sim U\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{6}{m+n}},\; \sqrt{\frac{6}{m+n}}\right) \]

The core design philosophy is compromise between two goals.

  1. Initialize all layers to have the same activation variance (preserve forward signal)
  2. Initialize all layers to have the same gradient variance (backward signal preservation)
ImportantWhy Variance Equilibrium Saves Optimization — Causal Chain

If the layer-by-layer activation variance and gradient variance are maintained within a certain range → signals do not explode or vanish in any layer → local cliffs and explosions on the loss surface are suppressed → allowing for a stable descent that skips local bends, much like optimizing a smooth surrogate loss. Regularizing variance within a range effectively constrains the learning process.

Assumptions and Limitations: This derivation is based on the assumption that “the network consists only of a chain of non-linear matrix products.” Of course, real-world networks have numerous non-linear activations between inputs and outputs. It is used as a standard because it works quite well in practice despite the assumption being broken.

6.3.3 Gain factor — Correction for nonlinearity [Saxe et al., 2013]

Let’s assume we use ReLU. If about half of the activations are negative, that half will drop to zero, and the activation variance will drop by that proportion. The activation–gradient variance balance established by Xavier is broken.

Correction: Restores the magnitude lost through a gain factor. In the case of ReLU, since the variance is halved, a correction of \(\times 2\) (based on weighted standard deviation \(\times\sqrt{2}\)) is applied — generally, a scale adjusted to the rate at which negative activations occur is multiplied. By “simulating” nonlinearity in this way and restoring variance balance, a good local minimum is reached with fewer iterations. The importance of the gain factor was also confirmed in subsequent research [Sussillo, 2014]. (The He initialization family formalized the gain for ReLU.)

6.3.4 Sparse initialization [Martens, 2010]

Another approach proposed at the same time: the method of initializing a majority of parameters to 0 can also provide a good initial point and is widely used in various applications.

Initialization Distribution Design Principles
Naive uniform \(U(-1/\sqrt{m}, 1/\sqrt{m})\) Fan-in based scaling
Xavier (Glorot) \(U\!\big(-\sqrt{6/(m{+}n)}, \sqrt{6/(m{+}n)}\big)\) activation var ↔︎ gradient var equilibrium (linear assumption)
Xavier + gain Above \(\times\) gain Variance correction dying due to non-linearity (ReLU: gain \(=\sqrt{2}\))
Sparse init Set majority to zero Valid fan-in limit

8 Python Appendix: Reproducing the Core Phenomenon

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ---------- 1. Ill-conditioning: condition number와 GD ----------
# f(x) = 0.5 x^T A x,  A = diag(1, kappa)
def gd_path(kappa, lr, steps=60, momentum=0.0, nesterov=False):
    A = np.diag([1.0, kappa])
    x = np.array([8.0, 1.0]); v = np.zeros(2)
    path = [x.copy()]
    for _ in range(steps):
        g_point = x + momentum * v if nesterov else x
        g = A @ g_point
        v = momentum * v - lr * g
        x = x + v
        path.append(x.copy())
    return np.array(path)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 4))
for ax, kappa in zip(axes, [1, 10, 50]):
    A = np.diag([1.0, kappa])
    xs = np.linspace(-9, 9, 200); ys = np.linspace(-3, 3, 200)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(xs, ys)
    Z = 0.5 * (A[0, 0] * X**2 + A[1, 1] * Y**2)
    ax.contour(X, Y, Z, levels=20, alpha=0.4)
    p = gd_path(kappa, lr=1.8 / kappa)   # 큰 고유값 기준으로 안전한 lr
    ax.plot(p[:, 0], p[:, 1], "o-", ms=3)
    ax.set_title(f"condition number = {kappa}")
plt.suptitle("kappa가 클수록: 가파른 축은 진동, 완만한 축은 정체")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

# ---------- 2. Noisy gradient + Momentum = low-pass filter ----------
def sgd_1d(momentum=0.0, lr=0.05, noise=2.0, steps=300):
    x, v = 6.0, 0.0
    xs = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = 2 * x + noise * rng.standard_normal()   # f(x)=x^2 + gradient noise
        v = momentum * v - lr * g
        x = x + v
        xs.append(x)
    return np.array(xs)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.plot(sgd_1d(momentum=0.0), label="SGD (no momentum)", alpha=0.7)
plt.plot(sgd_1d(momentum=0.9), label=r"SGD + momentum ($\alpha=0.9$)", lw=2)
plt.axhline(0, color="k", ls="--", lw=0.8)
plt.legend(); plt.title("Momentum = gradient noise의 저역통과 필터링")
plt.xlabel("iteration"); plt.ylabel("x"); plt.show()

# ---------- 3. Xavier init: 층을 지나며 activation variance 보존 ----------
def forward_variance(init, depth=30, width=512, act=np.tanh):
    x = rng.standard_normal((1000, width))
    var_hist = []
    for _ in range(depth):
        if init == "naive":
            W = rng.normal(0, 0.5, (width, width))           # 너무 큰 분산
        elif init == "small":
            W = rng.normal(0, 0.01, (width, width))          # 너무 작은 분산
        elif init == "xavier":
            limit = np.sqrt(6 / (width + width))
            W = rng.uniform(-limit, limit, (width, width))
        x = act(x @ W)
        var_hist.append(x.var())
    return var_hist

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
for init in ["naive", "small", "xavier"]:
    plt.semilogy(forward_variance(init), label=init)
plt.legend(); plt.xlabel("layer depth"); plt.ylabel("activation variance (log)")
plt.title("Xavier init만 깊이에 걸쳐 variance를 보존 (폭발도 소멸도 없음)")
plt.show()

9 1-Minute Cheat Sheet

  • risk vs. empirical risk: The former is the \(p_{\text{data}}\) expected value (goal), the latter is the training set mean (ERM, the actual solution). The biggest difference from pure optimization is that the goal is a proxy.
  • Newton’s method: \(x^* = x_0 - H^{-1}g\) (2nd-order Taylor). Optimal step for convex surfaces. Discarded in deep learning due to ① Hessian cost, ② meaningless on complex surfaces, and ③ dragging at saddle points.
  • Condition number \(\kappa = |\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}|\). The larger the number, the more difficult the step size tuning becomes. Higher dimensions → almost always ill-conditioned.
  • Saddle point coin argument: For a minimum to exist, all eigenvalues must be positive → exponentially sparse in higher dimensions. Random matrix theory: The critical point of the lower \(J\) is the minimum, and the higher \(J\) is the saddle.
  • Cliff → gradient clipping (norm threshold clipping). Vanishing gradient ← depth axis long-term dependency.
  • Inexact gradient → A smooth surrogate loss design is the solution and an open problem.
  • Local↔︎global discrepancy: Even if the loss rises locally, there may be a better minimum beyond the hill — judgment cannot be made based on the loss curve alone; evaluation must be done via tests.
  • Theoretical Attitude: Abandon global minimum, goal is “sufficiently low loss → good generalization”.
  • Visualization Lesson: A monotonically decreasing linear subspace exists beneath the complex surface; the factored linear model captures the qualitative behavior.
  • Identity of Optimizer: All SGD. The only difference is learning rate scheduling.
  • Momentum: \(v \leftarrow \alpha v - \epsilon \nabla J\), \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + v\). EMA of past gradient = Inertia. \(\alpha\) is a sensitive hyperparameter.
  • Nesterov: Calculate gradient from lookahead point \(\theta + \alpha v\) — prevent old gradient, practical default.
  • Initialization: Size determines the result. MLP: \(U(-1/\sqrt{m}, 1/\sqrt{m})\). Xavier: \(U(\pm\sqrt{6/(m{+}n)})\) — activation var ↔︎ gradient var compromise, assumes linearity but is valid in practice. For non-linear types like ReLU, gain factor (ReLU: variance \(\times 2\)) correction is applied. Sparse initialization is also a type.
