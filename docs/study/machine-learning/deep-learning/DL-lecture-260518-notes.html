Few-shot / Zero-shot / Continual Learning

Published

May 18, 2026

1 View at a glance

covers methods for training models in situations where “data is scarce or data flows in over time.” It consists of two main axes.

  • Few-/Zero-Shot: When there is little or no label data for the class of interest.
  • Data flow (continual learning): When data/classes arrive sequentially over time.
NoteKey Message

In machine learning, there are two competing goals: fitting to the data and generalization. When the data and model are small, simple fitting hinders generalization, but when the data and model become sufficiently large, fitting itself leads to generalization (benign overfitting). Since this luxury cannot be enjoyed in areas where data is scarce, the shortfall must be compensated for by inductive bias, unlabeled data, or efficient use by humans.

2 Two Goals of Machine Learning and Semantic Mapping

2.1 Fit vs Generalization

The classic answer to the question “What is the goal of machine learning?” is generalization. However, in the era of large models, the answer becomes nuanced. As models improve and data grows, simply fitting the data better becomes one path to achieving generalization. In other words, while the two goals are inherently in competition, they may no longer conflict as the scale increases (overfitting is a problem on small scales, but on sufficiently large data, fitting is synonymous with generalization).

2.2 “What makes a chair a chair?”

The job of the model is to map the data \(x\) to the label space \(y\) (\(f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\)). Now, let’s consider the label “chair”. Chairs with backrests, stools without backrests, those with armrests, benches for three people—visually very diverse, but they share the same meaning.

ImportantWhy it is difficult: Because meaning is defined by affordance, not vision.

The concept of “chair” is defined not as a visual signal but as affordance and functionality (“something that can be sat on”) . Therefore, the problem of mapping an image to the meaning of a chair is an ill-defined problem (though not ill-posed in the mathematical sense). The input itself is not ready to be mapped to that meaning.

A classic example that pushed this insight to the extreme is Grabner et al., “What makes a chair a chair?” (CVPR 2011) . They placed a 3D avatar on an actual object, and if successful, determined it to be a “chair.” While this may seem somewhat reckless, it significantly improved accuracy by the standards of the time by directly verifying functionality instead of relying on visual signals. The lesson: If the space for expression is insufficient to contain meaning, the very definition of meaning must be rethought “outside the box.”

TipAnalogy from a wireless communication perspective

This is a sufficiency of representation (feature) space problem. When attempting to reconstruct an estimator using only the received signal \(y\) in a wireless network, if the observation is not identifiable, reconstruction is impossible no matter how good the estimator is. The failure of chair recognition is not a problem of the estimator (classifier), but rather is closer to a lack of identifiability of the observation model itself.

2.3 Remedies to fill the gap

Prescriptions for addressing data scarcity in the literature:

Prescription Cost Key Idea
Large-scale supervised Expensive, accurate Secure labels in bulk
Few-shot learning — Generalization to a few examples
Semi-supervised Cheap Labeled+Unlabeled Mix
Active learning Affordable Select and label “what to ask” to people
Self-supervised Inexpensive Generates supervised signals from the data itself
Unsupervised Cheapest Structure learning without labels

Looking at the axis, the top (supervised) is accurate but expensive, and the bottom (self-/unsupervised) is cheap but weak supervision.

3 Few-shot / N-shot / Zero-shot

3.1 Definition

  • Few-shot (\(N\)-shot) learning: Training with prime (\(N\)) examples for the class of interest.
  • Zero-shot learning: Training with 0 examples for the class of interest.

3.2 Most confusing point: zero-shot vs unsupervised

This is the part that students confuse the most. It becomes clear if you separate the two axes.

ImportantTwo independent axes
  • Axis 1 (Supervision Status): supervised ↔︎ unsupervised. Whether to label or not.
  • Axis 2 (Number of examples in class of interest): many-shot ↔︎ few-shot ↔︎ zero-shot.

These two are orthogonal. Zero-shot usually refers to the case where there are “zero examples of the class of interest” within the supervised paradigm , and unsupervised refers to “no labels at all”.

Class of Interest Example Label How It Works
Many-shot supervised Many Present Standard compliant
Few-shot Prime Present Prime examples + Strong prior knowledge
Zero-shot 0 items (intentionally excluded) (present in auxiliary information) inductive bias/extrapolation by auxiliary knowledge
Unsupervised (May contain) None No label at all

the model is “expected” to generalize to the semantics of interest without intentionally providing training examples . The “magic” that enables this generalization is inductive bias (or auxiliary semantic information, heritage). On the other hand, unsupervised simply lacks labels and does not control for the possibility that the concepts of interest may be mixed into the data.

TipAnalogy from a wireless communication perspective
  • Few-shot ≈ Channel estimation with a small number of pilots: Structural prior knowledge of the channel due to insufficient observations (sparseness, correlation structure) must be utilized. It is the same spirit as compressed sensing and MMSE estimation.
  • Zero-shot ≈ Blind Estimation: Estimating based solely on known structure/prior distribution without direct observation of the object of interest Estimated. It is essentially solved “without a pilot,” so prior knowledge is everything.
  • Unsupervised ≈ Clustering/Structure discovery of unlabeled signals.

3.3 Three branches binding prescriptions

There are ultimately three strategies for dealing with few-/zero-shot (broadly speaking, weakly-supervised).

  1. Use of inductive bias — architecture, hand-designed normalization, relationships (structure) between samples of data.
  2. Using unlabeled data — semi-/self-/unsupervised learning.
  3. Efficient use of humans — active learning (selecting samples for labeling), RLHF (feedback alignment).

Modern large-scale learning primarily pushes (2) to an unprecedented scale. Massive pre-training with unlabeled data yields unexpected performance improvements (emergent ability) in various downstream tasks.

NoteUnlabeled pre-training is a “stupid but expensive” universal solution

To borrow the instructor’s words, unlabeled pre-training is a simple (brute-force) but expensive alternative compared to other inductive bias and human-efficient techniques. Instead of using massive computations and data, it achieves universal performance and emergent capabilities. In resource-constrained situations (e.g., edge devices), resource-optimized solutions like inductive bias or active learning may actually be more suitable. One must determine “what is the best solution at this moment” on a task-by-task basis.

4 Revisiting Inductive Bias

4.1 Supervised learning = induction

Supervised learning is an induction that predicts the future (test samples) from the past (training samples). What constrains induction is inductive bias, and its implementation is regularization (regularizer).

4.2 Interpretation of Learning Objective Function and MAP

A typical learning objective function is

\[ \min_{\theta}\; \underbrace{L(\theta)}_{\text{손실(데이터 적합)}} \;+\; \lambda\,\underbrace{\Omega(\theta)}_{\text{정규화(귀납 편향)}}. \]

If data is scarce, \(L\) alone cannot be trusted, and abundant inductive bias \(\Omega\) fills the gaps.

ImportantNormalization = Negative logarithm prior distribution (MAP)

From a Bayesian perspective, this objective function is exactly the same as MAP estimation. \[ \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} =\arg\max_{\theta}\; \log p(\theta\mid \mathcal{D}) =\arg\max_{\theta}\;\Big[\underbrace{\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}_{=\,-L(\theta)}+\underbrace{\log p(\theta)}_{=\,-\lambda\Omega(\theta)}\Big]. \] , i.e., \(\Omega(\theta) = -\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\log p(\theta)\). Introducing inductive bias is equivalent to assigning the prior distribution \(p(\theta)\) to the parameters. (e.g., Gaussian prior distribution on \(L_2\) normalized \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(\theta\); Laplace prior distribution on \(L_1\) \(\Leftrightarrow\).)

MLE is a special case that “assumes” a uniform prior distribution, not that it “ignores” the prior distribution. Let’s remember the structure MAP = MLE + log-prior (=normalization).

4.3 Inductive biases of traditional ML

Inductive bias Model used Meaning
Maximum conditional independence Naïve Bayes Assumption of conditional independence between features
Minimum cross-validation error (Select Model) Select Minimum CV Error Hypothesis
Maximum margin SVM Decision boundary margin maximization
Minimum description length Decision tree Prefer short description (Occam’s Razor)
Minimum features (Select features) Remove useless features
Nearest neighbor k-NN Label by neighbor majority vote

4.4 Modern Inductive Bias

  • Architecture — CNN’s locality and translation equivariance, Transformer’s permutation structure, etc.
  • Hand-designed normalization — weight decay, dropout, loss of consistency, etc.
  • Data Structure (Relationships between samples) — Samples with weak relationships can be excluded from training to save computational resources.
Notefew-shot one-line summary

If there is no data → Use inductive bias or large-scale pre-training (efficiently utilizing humans).

5 Continual Learning: Classical Approach

5.1 Motivation: The unrealistic assumption that “all classes are known in advance”

Let’s assume we trained a classifier using 1,000 classes from ImageNet. Are 1,000 the only classes in the world? How do we add a new class?

Combine a new class into the dataset and retrain from scratch — although very inefficient, In fact, there is no better standard method than this.

Continuous learning aims to break the inefficiency of “starting over from scratch every time.” Furthermore, the semantic granularity of each class varies. “Dog” is broad, while “sedan SUV” is narrow. However, we treat them all as an equal single class . This is where the natural desire to gradually add new classes arises.

5.2 This is not a new problem

Continuous learning may seem new, but it is a reconstruction of long-studied adjacent fields.

Adjacent Fields Core Relationship with Continuous Learning
Transfer learning Transferring past knowledge to a new task Components of continuous learning
Domain adaptation Adapting to changes in the input domain \(\mathcal{X}\) Responding to changes in distribution
Multitask learning Simultaneous learning of multiple tasks Time-compressed continuous learning
Meta learning Learning how to learn Applying past learning to the present
Lifelong / never-ending Lifelong cumulative learning Another name for the same concept
TipDomain adaptation viewed through domain / range

In the function \(f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}\), \(\mathcal{X}\) is the domain, and \(\mathcal{Y}\) is the image, which is the range. Domain adaptation means leaving \(f\) as is, but expecting the function to work even if the domain \(\mathcal{X}\) changes. Example: A “camera” classifier was trained on an old DSLR, but to a five-year-old child these days, a camera is a “smartphone.” The domain has been extended from DSLR to phone, and we want it to map to the same label.

NoteMultitask = Time-compressed Continual Learning

Let’s assume that in multitask, each task is trained simultaneously using different data subsets. If we unfold this along the time axis (Task 1 → Task 2 → …), it is essentially continuous learning. Therefore, multitask is a time -compressed version of continuous learning, and continuous learning is the time-credit version of multitask.

5.3 ML 1.0 vs ML 2.0

ML 1.0 (Isolated Learning) ML 2.0 (Lifelong Learning)
Paradigm Train model once with dataset Continuous learning while preserving and utilizing past knowledge
Memory None (Non-cumulative knowledge) Accumulated
Data Requirement Need to secure a large dictionary Learn with a small number of examples (like a human)
Limitations Weaknesses of isolated learning Need for a systematic approach

Humans never learn in isolation. It is difficult to create intelligent agents using only ML 1.0 algorithms, so the motivation is that we must evolve to ML 2.0 (lifelong learning) that “learns like humans.”

5.4 Early research lineage

  • Thrun 1996 (lifelong learning): function sequence \(f_1,f_2,f_3,\dots\in F\) over a learner’s lifetime Learning. Each task \(f:\mathcal{I}\to\{0,1\}\) and \(f_{\text{dog}}(x)=1\) means “\(x\) is a dog”. Key Intuition: When learning \(f_{\text{dog}}\), reuse previously learned knowledge such as \(f_{\text{cat}},f_{\text{bird}}\) .
  • NELL (Never-Ending Language Learner, Mitchell et al.): From January 2010 to September 2018 Operates 24 hours a day. It extracts facts from the web to populate the knowledge base and improves it to “read better than yesterday.” As a result, it has accumulated approximately 120 million confidence-weighted beliefs and expanded its reading, reasoning, and learning capabilities, as well as its ontology. NELL is a typed, closed knowledge base ( a collection of tuples (subject, relation, object) .
  • NEIL (Never-Ending Image Learner): NELL’s image version. Run for 4 months on 200 cores, 2,500 units Discovered abnormal concepts (Object > 1,500, Scene > 1,034, Attribute > 87), analyzed over 5 million images, labeled 600,000 images, discovered 3,000 relationships. Learned Object–Object, Scene–Object, Object–Attribute, and Scene–Attribute relationships (e.g., discovered “Wheels are part of a car” as a bounding box inclusion relationship).
NoteFrom Knowledge Base to Neural Network

As the knowledge base grows larger (increasing nodes and edges), it reads better, reasons better, and learns better. This is because it knows “where to attach” new information to concepts it already knows. Today, instead of such explicit knowledge bases, we use a compressed knowledge base called a neural network, but the principle that accumulation and connections enhance capabilities remains the same.

6 Continuous Learning: Modern Setup

6.1 Classification Criteria: What Changes

Typical setup when \(P(Y)\) is fixed:

Setup Things that change over time Intuition
Class-incremental (CIL) Class set \(\mathcal{Y}\) is incrementally added \(t{=}1\): Book·Airplane, \(t{=}2\): Car·Dog …
Domain-incremental Input domain \(\mathcal{X}\) changes Samples of the same class but different domains at different time points

Continuous learning itself is not a problem limited to classification, but the most studied standard setup is CIL.

6.3 catastrophic forgetting

Because only a subset of the data is seen at each time step, performance on past tasks deteriorates rapidly when a new task is learned . Unlike standard learning (which uses all data in every epoch), forgetting is much more severe in continuous learning because past samples are seen only a limited number of times.

ImportantIn large models, there is less forgetting — instead, other problems arise.

In large models with a very large number of parameters, learned knowledge is stored scattered across a vast parameter space and is therefore not easily destroyed by small gradient updates. Consequently, catastrophic forgetting is not the core issue in large models. Instead, plasticity is the problem — large models find it difficult to absorb new facts quickly or completely. Even injecting a single small fact may not be reflected in all parameters, potentially leading to incorrect answers.

6.4 stability–plasticity dilemma

How can forgetting be completely prevented with 0 computational cost? — By learning nothing. No updates mean no forgetting. However, since the purpose of continuous learning is learning new things, this is meaningless.

\[ \underbrace{\text{Stability}}_{\text{과거 보존}} \;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\; \underbrace{\text{Plasticity}}_{\text{새것 흡수}} \]

The two are a trade-off. To learn something new, one risks sacrificing a part of the past. In cognitive science, the human brain faces the same dilemma.

TipAnalogy from a wireless communication perspective: Adaptive filter with a forgetting factor

This dilemma is essentially the same as the convergence vs. tracking trade-off of adaptive filters . Let us recall the forgetting factor \(\lambda\) of RLS or the step size \(\mu\) of LMS.

  • If \(\mu\) (or \(1-\lambda\)) is small: the steady-state error is small (=preserves the past well, stability), and However, tracking is slow when the channel changes (=slow absorption of new, low plasticity).
  • If \(\mu\) is large: Change tracking is fast (high plasticity), but fluctuates in the steady state and past statistics Forget(Low Stability).

Catastrophic forgetting corresponds to the phenomenon where an adaptive filter overwrites past statistics as it reconverges to new channel statistics. The “replay buffer” corresponds to storing past pilot/training samples.

6.5 Classification of CIL techniques (taxonomy)

Code 
flowchart TD
    T[CIL 방법론] --> R[Replay 기반]
    T --> G[정규화 기반]
    T --> P[파라미터 격리]
    R --> R1[과거 표본을 작은 버퍼에<br/>보관 후 새 과제와 함께 재생]
    G --> G1[Prior-focused<br/>이전 학습의 중요 파라미터를 고정<br/>예: EWC, Fisher 가중]
    G --> G2[Data-focused<br/>이전 모델의 출력을 정규화<br/>예: 지식 증류 LwF]
    P --> P1[새 과제는 새 파라미터/모델로<br/>나중에 병합 또는 라우팅]

  • Replay-based: Stores a portion of past samples in a small cache (replay buffer) and uses them together when training new tasks. Plays. The simplest and most powerful.
  • Normalization-based
    • Prior-focused: Penalizes parameters that were important in the past to move less. Typical examples include EWC(Elastic Weight Consolidation) 는 Fisher 정보 \(F_i\) 로 파라미터 중요도를 가중한다: \[ \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{new}}(\theta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_i\,(\theta_i-\theta^{*}_{\text{old},i})^2 . \] 여기서 \(F_i\) 는 과거 과제 손실의 곡률(2차 정보)을 근사하며, 로그-사전 \(\log p(\theta)\) 를 과거 사후분포로 둔 MAP(Section 4)의 한 형태로 볼 수 있다. 정보이론적으로 \(F\) 는 피셔 정보 행렬의 대각 근사다.
    • Data-focused: Knowledge distillation (e.g., LwF) that mimics the output (soft label) of the previous model.
  • Parameter Isolation: New data is trained with a new model/new parameters and merged later. Forgetting is almost non-existent. Although it does not exist, as the model continues to grow, storage costs become high, and a new problem arises regarding deciding which model to use during inference.
NoteExperimental Conclusion (Appendix Section 9 code)

In single-head setups with strong interference, simple replay tends to prevent forgetting much more robustly than regularization-based methods. In the appendix demo, naive sequential learning saw past task accuracy collapse to \(1.00\!\to\!0.00\), but recovered to \(0.97\) with only 20% replay.

6.6 More realistic setups (research trends)

Classical CIL assumes that it “looks only at a fixed class sample at each time point.” This is unrealistic. It extends to a more realistic setup.

Setup Problem Awareness Core Idea
Blurry / i-Blurry Do not view only one class at a time Blur task boundaries (other classes appear with low probability), new classes also appear gradually + anytime inference
Noisy labels Stream labels can be incorrect Learning on a tainted label stream
Boundary-free (Gaussian) stream Data distribution is a continuous function of time Boundary-free continuous distribution + Data prior scheduling
Hierarchical label expansion Differentiation from coarse classes to fine classes Learning new finer classes using hierarchy
Embodied agents Robots continuously learn new environments/behaviors Integrates new environments/behaviors while preserving past concepts
Name-only CL Human labelers are not always available Automated web data collection and cleaning using LLM/VLM as a labeler
Notei-Blurry’s Motivation (Probabilistic Intuition)

The “blurry” setup assumes that in \(t{=}1\), dogs and cats are seen mixed in with a high probability, while cars and boats are seen mixed in with a low probability, such as 1% . However, if the sample size is sufficiently large (e.g., more than 100 per class), even a class with a 0.1% probability will almost certainly appear at least once at every point in time. Then, effectively “all classes are always visible,” and it is no longer class-incremental. To fill this gap, the i-Blurry setup is designed so that some classes are added incrementally, thereby maintaining both realism and class-incremental properties.

6.7 Name-only / Human-free continuous learning

Human labelers or retraining data are not always available. In such cases, LLM and VLM are used as alternative labelers. If you simply provide the “names (semantics)” to the continuous learner, the rest (collecting new data from the web → automatic labeling → refining labels using LLM-image relationships → updating the model) is automated. Users do not need to collect datasets; they simply need to state the concept names.

7 Beyond CIL: Continuous Learning of Generative and Large Models

Until now, the focus has been on \(P(Y)\) (discrimination), but large generative models learn \(P(X)\) or \(P(X\mid Y)\). Modern large model training itself is, in fact, continuous learning.

NoteData pool + data fetcher

Classical learning involved iterating through a fixed dataset multiple times. There is no fixed dataset in training large models. Instead, there is a data pool (where growth, refinement, and new inputs occur continuously) and a model, with a data fetcher connecting them. The fetcher selects subsets useful for updating the model. The smarter the fetcher, the better the model obtained with less computation. The concepts of a fixed dataset and multiple epochs disappear, and some samples may appear multiple times, while others may not appear even once. In other words, it is the continuous learning paradigm itself.

LLM’s Continuous Learning Pipeline (Concept):

Abbreviation Step
DAP Domain-adaptive pre-training
SFT Supervised fine-tuning
CIT Continual instruction tuning
CMR Continual model refinement
CMA Continual model alignment
CMLLM Continual multimodal LLM

Here, the representative technique for efficiently utilizing humans is RLHF. It efficiently incorporates human preference feedback into model alignment (connected to the third strategy of few-/zero-shot, Section 3).

8 Summary

ImportantKey Summary
  1. few-shot (⊂ weak supervision) is a difficult generalization problem. In the absence of data, inductive bias, unlabeled data, Fills in as one of the efficient uses of people.
  2. zero-shot ≠ unsupervised. Zero-shot has 0 examples of the class of interest only (extrapolated with auxiliary knowledge), unsupervised has no label itself. They are mutually orthogonal axes.
  3. Normalization = negative log prior distribution. Including inductive bias is equivalent to a MAP that assigns \(p(\theta)\), and MLE is a special case of a uniform dictionary.
  4. Continuous learning is a data and computationally efficient learning scheme, which is particularly important for large models. However, we I’ve been in class-incremental for too long — it’s time to break free.
  5. The stability–plasticity dilemma (= convergence vs. tracking of adaptive filters) is the fundamental tension of continuous learning, and replay, normalization (EWC), and parameter isolation are the prescriptions.

9 Appendix: NumPy Demo of Catastrophic Oblivion and Replay

The code below reproduces the effects of catastrophic oblivion and replay using only NumPy, without external dependencies.

Setup. The input is 2D. The rule is domain-dependent (similar to XOR), so two tasks interfere with each other using the same parameters.

  • Assignment A: Samples only from the left area (\(x_1<0\)), Labels = (Left & Top) or (Right & Bottom)
  • Task B: Only samples from the right region (\(x_1>0\)), same rule

A single-head MLP can solve two tasks almost perfectly (upper bound) by looking at them jointly, but if trained sequentially (A→B), the decision boundary of A breaks down while only looking at B.

Code 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

# ---- 데이터: 영역 의존적 XOR 유사 규칙 ----
def lab(X):                       # +1 if (left & up) or (right & down)
    left, up = X[:, 0] < 0, X[:, 1] > 0
    return ((left & up) | ((~left) & (~up))).astype(float)

def region_A(n):                  # x<0 영역만 관측
    X = np.c_[rng.uniform(-2, 0, n), rng.uniform(-2, 2, n)]; return X, lab(X)
def region_B(n):                  # x>0 영역만 관측
    X = np.c_[rng.uniform(0, 2, n), rng.uniform(-2, 2, n)]; return X, lab(X)

XA, yA = region_A(2000); XB, yB = region_B(2000)
XAt, yAt = region_A(800); XBt, yBt = region_B(800)

# ---- 작은 2-layer MLP (tanh + sigmoid), 순수 NumPy ----
H = 64
def init():
    return [rng.normal(size=(2, H)) * 0.7, np.zeros(H),
            rng.normal(size=(H,)) * 0.7, np.zeros(1)]

def fwd(p, X):
    h = np.tanh(X @ p[0] + p[1]); z = h @ p[2] + p[3]
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(z, -30, 30))), h

def grads(p, X, y):
    pr, h = fwd(p, X); d = (pr - y) / len(y)
    dh = np.outer(d, p[2]) * (1 - h ** 2)
    return [X.T @ dh, dh.sum(0), h.T @ d, np.array([d.sum()])]

def acc(p, X, y):
    return ((fwd(p, X)[0] > 0.5).astype(float) == y).mean()

def train(p, X, y, lr=0.3, ep=2500, bs=256):
    p = [w.copy() for w in p]; n = len(X)
    for _ in range(ep):
        i = rng.choice(n, bs, replace=False)
        g = grads(p, X[i], y[i])
        for k in range(len(p)): p[k] -= lr * g[k]
    return p

# ---- 1) 과제 A 학습 ----
pA = train(init(), XA, yA)
print(f"after A      : accA={acc(pA, XAt, yAt):.2f}")

# ---- 2) Joint 학습 (상한, 두 과제를 함께) ----
pJ = train(init(), np.vstack([XA, XB]), np.concatenate([yA, yB]), ep=5000)
print(f"[Joint]       accA={acc(pJ, XAt, yAt):.2f}  accB={acc(pJ, XBt, yBt):.2f}  (upper bound)")

# ---- 3) Naive 순차 (A 학습 후 B만 학습) → 파국적 망각 ----
pN = train(pA, XB, yB)
print(f"[Naive]       accA={acc(pN, XAt, yAt):.2f}  accB={acc(pN, XBt, yBt):.2f}")

# ---- 4) Replay (B 학습 시 과거 A 표본 20% 혼합) → 회복 ----
idx = rng.choice(len(XA), 500, replace=False)
pR = train(pA, np.vstack([XB, XA[idx]]), np.concatenate([yB, yA[idx]]))
print(f"[Replay]      accA={acc(pR, XAt, yAt):.2f}  accB={acc(pR, XBt, yBt):.2f}")

Expected output (fixed random seed):

after A      : accA=1.00
[Joint]       accA=0.99  accB=1.00  (upper bound)
[Naive]       accA=0.00  accB=1.00
[Replay]      accA=0.97  accB=1.00

Interpretation. Joint(upper bound) solves both tasks almost perfectly. However, Naive sequential learning completely overwrites the decision boundary of A while learning B, collapsing to \(\text{accA}=0.00\) (catastrophic forgetting). Even replaying only 20% of the past samples nearly recovers the performance of A to \(0.97\) — demonstrating why Section 6’s replay-based technique is simple yet powerful.

NoteMemo on Normalization Base (EWC)

When applying EWC-style Fisher-weighted penalties to the same setup, in this strong interference, single head configuration, both tasks get stuck near \(\sim0.5\) and do not perform as well as replay. This is because being strongly fixed to the past posterior distribution results in a trade-off that satisfies neither task. This aligns with observations in the literature that “normalization-based approaches are not always the best” and that replay may be advantageous depending on the degree of interference and representation sharing between tasks.

## View at a glance {#sec-overview}

covers methods for training models in situations where "data is scarce or data flows in over time." It consists of two main axes.

- **Few-/Zero-Shot**: When there is little or no label data for the class of interest.
- **Data flow (continual learning)**: When data/classes arrive sequentially over time.

::: {.callout-note}
## Key Message
In machine learning, there are two competing goals: **fitting to the data** and 
**generalization**. When the data and model are small, simple fitting hinders generalization, but 
when the data and model become sufficiently large, fitting itself leads to generalization (benign overfitting). Since 
this luxury cannot be enjoyed in areas where data is scarce, the shortfall must be compensated for by **inductive bias**, 
**unlabeled data**, or **efficient use by humans**.
:::

## Two Goals of Machine Learning and Semantic Mapping {#sec-goals}

### Fit vs Generalization

The classic answer to the question "What is the goal of machine learning?" is **generalization**. However, in the era of large models, the answer becomes nuanced. 
As models improve and data grows, simply **fitting the data better** 
becomes one path to achieving generalization. In other words, while the two goals are inherently in competition, they may no longer conflict as the scale increases 
(overfitting is a problem on small scales, but on sufficiently large data, fitting is synonymous with generalization).

### "What makes a chair a chair?"

The job of the model is to map the data $x$ to the label space $y$ ($f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}$). 
Now, let's consider the label "chair". Chairs with backrests, stools without backrests, those with armrests, 
benches for three people—visually very diverse, but they share the same meaning.

::: {.callout-important}
## Why it is difficult: Because meaning is defined by affordance, not vision.
The concept of "chair" is defined not as a **visual signal** but as **affordance and functionality** ("something that can be sat on") 
. Therefore, the problem of mapping an image to the meaning of a chair 
is an **ill-defined problem** (though not ill-posed in the mathematical sense). The input itself is not ready to be mapped to that meaning.
:::

A classic example that pushed this insight to the extreme is Grabner et al., "What makes a chair a chair?" (CVPR 2011) 
. They **placed** a 3D avatar on an actual object, and if successful, determined it to be a "chair." While this may seem somewhat reckless, 
it significantly improved accuracy by the standards of the time by directly verifying functionality instead of relying on visual signals. The lesson: 
If the space for expression is insufficient to contain meaning, the very definition of meaning must be rethought "outside the box."

::: {.callout-tip}
## Analogy from a wireless communication perspective
This is a **sufficiency of representation (feature) space** problem. 
When attempting to reconstruct an estimator using only the received signal $y$ in a wireless network, if the observation is not **identifiable**, reconstruction is impossible no matter how good the estimator is. 
The failure of chair recognition is not a problem of the estimator (classifier), but rather is closer to a **lack of identifiability of the observation model itself**.
:::

### Remedies to fill the gap

Prescriptions for addressing data scarcity in the literature:

| Prescription | Cost | Key Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Large-scale supervised | Expensive, accurate | Secure labels in bulk |
| Few-shot learning | — | Generalization to a few examples |
| Semi-supervised | Cheap | Labeled+Unlabeled Mix |
| Active learning | Affordable | Select and label "what to ask" to people |
| Self-supervised | Inexpensive | Generates supervised signals from the data itself |
| Unsupervised | Cheapest | Structure learning without labels |

Looking at the axis, the top (supervised) is **accurate but expensive**, and the bottom (self-/unsupervised) is 
**cheap but weak supervision**.

## Few-shot / N-shot / Zero-shot {#sec-fewshot}

### Definition

- **Few-shot ($N$-shot) learning**: Training with **prime ($N$)** examples for the class of interest.
- **Zero-shot learning**: Training with **0 examples** for the class of interest.

### Most confusing point: zero-shot vs unsupervised

This is the part that students confuse the most. It becomes clear if you separate the two axes.

::: {.callout-important}
## Two independent axes
- **Axis 1 (Supervision Status)**: supervised ↔ unsupervised. Whether to label or not.
- **Axis 2 (Number of examples in class of interest)**: many-shot ↔ few-shot ↔ zero-shot.

These two are **orthogonal**. Zero-shot usually refers to the case where there are "zero examples of the class of interest" within the supervised paradigm 
, and unsupervised refers to "no labels at all".
:::

| Class of Interest Example | Label | How It Works |
|---|---|---|---|
| Many-shot supervised | Many | Present | Standard compliant |
| Few-shot | Prime | Present | Prime examples + Strong prior knowledge |
| **Zero-shot** | **0 items (intentionally excluded)** | (present in auxiliary information) | **inductive bias/extrapolation by auxiliary knowledge** |
| **Unsupervised** | (May contain) | **None** | No label at all |

the model is "expected" to generalize to the semantics 
of interest without **intentionally providing training examples** . The "magic" that enables this generalization 
is **inductive bias (or auxiliary semantic information, heritage)**. On the other hand, unsupervised simply lacks labels and 
does not control for the possibility that the concepts of interest may be mixed into the data.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Analogy from a wireless communication perspective
- **Few-shot ≈ Channel estimation with a small number of pilots**: Structural prior knowledge of the channel due to insufficient observations
(sparseness, correlation structure) must be utilized. It is the same spirit as compressed sensing and MMSE estimation.
- **Zero-shot ≈ Blind Estimation**: Estimating based solely on known structure/prior distribution without direct observation of the object of interest
Estimated. It is essentially solved "without a pilot," so prior knowledge is everything.
- **Unsupervised ≈ Clustering/Structure discovery of unlabeled signals**.
:::

### Three branches binding prescriptions

There are ultimately three strategies for dealing with few-/zero-shot (broadly speaking, weakly-supervised).

1. **Use of inductive bias** — architecture, hand-designed normalization, relationships (structure) between samples of data.
2. **Using unlabeled data** — semi-/self-/unsupervised learning.
3. **Efficient use of humans** — active learning (selecting samples for labeling), RLHF (feedback alignment).

Modern large-scale learning primarily pushes (2) to an unprecedented scale. Massive **pre-training** with unlabeled data 
yields unexpected performance improvements (emergent ability) in various downstream tasks.

::: {.callout-note}
## Unlabeled pre-training is a "stupid but expensive" universal solution
To borrow the instructor's words, unlabeled pre-training is 
a **simple (brute-force) but expensive** alternative compared to other inductive bias and human-efficient techniques. Instead of using massive computations and data, 
it achieves universal performance and emergent capabilities. In resource-constrained situations (e.g., edge devices), 
**resource-optimized** solutions like inductive bias or active learning may actually be more suitable. One 
must determine "what is the best solution at this moment" on a task-by-task basis.
:::

## Revisiting Inductive Bias {#sec-inductive}

### Supervised learning = induction

Supervised learning is an induction that **predicts the future (test samples) from the past (training samples).** What constrains induction is 
**inductive bias**, and its implementation is **regularization (regularizer)**.

### Interpretation of Learning Objective Function and MAP

A typical learning objective function is

$$
\min_{\theta}\; \underbrace{L(\theta)}_{\text{손실(데이터 적합)}} \;+\; \lambda\,\underbrace{\Omega(\theta)}_{\text{정규화(귀납 편향)}}.
$$

If data is scarce, $L$ alone cannot be trusted, and abundant inductive bias $\Omega$ fills the gaps.

::: {.callout-important}
## Normalization = Negative logarithm prior distribution (MAP)
From a Bayesian perspective, this objective function is exactly the same as **MAP estimation**.
$$
\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}}
=\arg\max_{\theta}\; \log p(\theta\mid \mathcal{D})
=\arg\max_{\theta}\;\Big[\underbrace{\log p(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}_{=\,-L(\theta)}+\underbrace{\log p(\theta)}_{=\,-\lambda\Omega(\theta)}\Big].
$$
, i.e., $\Omega(\theta) = -\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\log p(\theta)$. **Introducing inductive bias is 
equivalent to assigning the prior distribution $p(\theta)$ to the parameters**. (e.g., 
Gaussian prior distribution on $L_2$ normalized $\Leftrightarrow$ $\theta$; Laplace prior distribution on $L_1$ $\Leftrightarrow$.)

MLE is a special case that "assumes" a uniform prior distribution, not that it "ignores" the prior distribution. 
Let's remember the structure MAP = MLE + log-prior (=normalization).
:::

### Inductive biases of traditional ML

| Inductive bias | Model used | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum conditional independence | Naïve Bayes | Assumption of conditional independence between features |
| Minimum cross-validation error | (Select Model) | Select Minimum CV Error Hypothesis |
| Maximum margin | SVM | Decision boundary margin maximization |
| Minimum description length | Decision tree | Prefer short description (Occam's Razor) |
| Minimum features | (Select features) | Remove useless features |
| Nearest neighbor | k-NN | Label by neighbor majority vote |

### Modern Inductive Bias

- **Architecture** — CNN's locality and translation equivariance, Transformer's permutation structure, etc.
- **Hand-designed normalization** — weight decay, dropout, loss of consistency, etc.
- **Data Structure (Relationships between samples)** — Samples with weak relationships can be excluded from training to save computational resources.

::: {.callout-note}
## few-shot one-line summary
**If there is no data → Use inductive bias or large-scale pre-training (efficiently utilizing humans).**
:::

## Continual Learning: Classical Approach {#sec-cl-classic}

### Motivation: The unrealistic assumption that "all classes are known in advance"

Let's assume we trained a classifier using 1,000 classes from ImageNet. Are 1,000 the only classes in the world? 
How do we add a new class?

> Combine a new class into the dataset and **retrain from scratch** — although very inefficient,
> In fact, there is no better standard method than this.

Continuous learning aims to break the inefficiency of "starting over from scratch every time." Furthermore, the semantic granularity of each class 
varies. "Dog" is broad, while "sedan SUV" is narrow. However, we treat them all as an equal single class 
. This is where the natural desire to gradually add new classes arises.

### This is not a new problem

Continuous learning may seem new, but it is a reconstruction of long-studied adjacent fields.

| Adjacent Fields | Core | Relationship with Continuous Learning |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer learning | Transferring past knowledge to a new task | Components of continuous learning |
| Domain adaptation | Adapting to changes in the input domain $\mathcal{X}$ | Responding to changes in distribution |
| Multitask learning | Simultaneous learning of multiple tasks | **Time-compressed** continuous learning |
| Meta learning | Learning how to learn | Applying past learning to the present |
| Lifelong / never-ending | Lifelong cumulative learning | Another name for the same concept |

::: {.callout-tip}
## Domain adaptation viewed through domain / range
In the function $f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}$, $\mathcal{X}$ is the domain, and $\mathcal{Y}$ 
is the image, which is the range. **Domain adaptation** means leaving $f$ as is, but 
expecting the function to work even if the domain $\mathcal{X}$ changes. Example: A "camera" classifier was trained on an old DSLR, but 
to a five-year-old child these days, a camera is a "smartphone." The domain has been extended from DSLR to phone, and we 
want it to map to the same label.
:::

::: {.callout-note}
## Multitask = Time-compressed Continual Learning
Let's assume that in multitask, each task is trained simultaneously using different data subsets. 
If we unfold this along the time axis (Task 1 → Task 2 → ...), it is essentially continuous learning. Therefore, **multitask is a time 
-compressed version of continuous learning**, and **continuous learning is the time-credit version of multitask**.
:::

### ML 1.0 vs ML 2.0

| | ML 1.0 (Isolated Learning) | ML 2.0 (Lifelong Learning) |
|---|---|---|
| Paradigm | Train model once with dataset | Continuous learning while preserving and utilizing past knowledge |
| Memory | None (Non-cumulative knowledge) | Accumulated |
| Data Requirement | Need to secure a large dictionary | Learn with a small number of examples (like a human) |
| Limitations | Weaknesses of isolated learning | Need for a systematic approach |

Humans never learn in isolation. It is difficult to create intelligent agents using only ML 1.0 algorithms, 
so the motivation is that we must evolve to ML 2.0 (lifelong learning) that "learns like humans."

### Early research lineage

- **Thrun 1996 (lifelong learning)**: function sequence $f_1,f_2,f_3,\dots\in F$ over a learner's lifetime
Learning. Each task $f:\mathcal{I}\to\{0,1\}$ and $f_{\text{dog}}(x)=1$ means "$x$ is a dog". Key Intuition: When learning $f_{\text{dog}}$, 
reuse previously learned knowledge such as $f_{\text{cat}},f_{\text{bird}}$ .
- **NELL (Never-Ending Language Learner, Mitchell et al.)**: From January 2010 to September 2018
Operates 24 hours a day. It extracts facts from the web to populate the knowledge base and improves it to "read better than yesterday." 
As a result, it has accumulated approximately **120 million confidence-weighted beliefs** and expanded its reading, reasoning, and learning capabilities, as well as its ontology. NELL is a **typed, closed knowledge base** ( 
a collection of tuples (subject, relation, object) .
- **NEIL (Never-Ending Image Learner)**: NELL's image version. **Run for 4 months on 200 cores**, 2,500 units
Discovered abnormal concepts (Object > 1,500, Scene > 1,034, Attribute > 87), analyzed over 5 million images, labeled 600,000 images, 
discovered 3,000 relationships. Learned Object–Object, Scene–Object, Object–Attribute, and Scene–Attribute relationships (e.g., 
discovered "Wheels are part of a car" as a bounding box inclusion relationship).

::: {.callout-note}
## From Knowledge Base to Neural Network
As the knowledge base grows larger (increasing nodes and edges), it **reads better, reasons better, and learns better**. 
This is because it knows "where to attach" new information to concepts it already knows. Today, instead of such explicit knowledge bases, we 
use a **compressed knowledge base called a neural network**, but the principle that accumulation and connections enhance capabilities remains the same.
:::

## Continuous Learning: Modern Setup {#sec-cl-modern}

### Classification Criteria: What Changes

Typical setup when $P(Y)$ is fixed:

| Setup | Things that change over time | Intuition |
|---|---|---|
| **Class-incremental (CIL)** | Class set $\mathcal{Y}$ is incrementally added | $t{=}1$: Book·Airplane, $t{=}2$: Car·Dog … |
| **Domain-incremental** | Input domain $\mathcal{X}$ changes | Samples of the same class but different domains at different time points |

Continuous learning itself is not a problem limited to classification, but the most studied standard setup is **CIL**.

### Online vs Offline Continuous Learning

| | Offline CL | Online CL |
|---|---|---|
| Data access | Repeated access to the subset at that point in time | View a sample from the stream **once** and let it flow |
| Epoch | **Multiple Epochs** Allowed | Single Pass (1-epoch) |
| Difficulty | Relatively Easy | **More Difficult**(Must Converge Quickly) |

Since online cannot re-examine samples, it must converge quickly to a solution at a given point in time.

```{mermaid}
flowchart LR
    subgraph Offline
      D1[Data-1<br/>다중 에폭] --> D2[Data-2<br/>다중 에폭] --> D3[Data-3<br/>다중 에폭]
    end
    subgraph Online
      S[스트림: x1 x2 x3 ... 각 1회] --> M[모델 즉시 업데이트]
    end
```

### catastrophic forgetting

Because only a **subset** of the data is seen at each time step, performance on past tasks deteriorates rapidly when a new task is learned . Unlike standard learning (which uses all data in every epoch), 
forgetting is much more severe in continuous learning because past samples are seen only a limited number of times.

::: {.callout-important}
## In large models, there is less forgetting — instead, other problems arise.
In large models with a very large number of parameters, learned knowledge is stored scattered across a vast parameter space and 
is therefore not easily destroyed by small gradient updates. Consequently, **catastrophic forgetting 
is not the core issue in large models.** Instead, **plasticity** is the problem — large models 
find it difficult to absorb new facts quickly or completely. Even injecting a single small fact may not be reflected in all parameters, potentially leading to incorrect answers.
:::

### stability–plasticity dilemma

How can forgetting be completely prevented with **0 computational cost**? — **By learning nothing.** No updates mean 
no forgetting. However, since the purpose of continuous learning is **learning new things**, this is meaningless.

$$
\underbrace{\text{Stability}}_{\text{과거 보존}} \;\;\longleftrightarrow\;\; \underbrace{\text{Plasticity}}_{\text{새것 흡수}}
$$

The two are a trade-off. To learn something new, one risks sacrificing a part of the past. In cognitive science, the human brain 
faces the same dilemma.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Analogy from a wireless communication perspective: Adaptive filter with a forgetting factor
This dilemma is essentially the same as the **convergence vs. tracking trade-off of adaptive filters** 
. Let us recall the forgetting factor $\lambda$ of RLS or the step size $\mu$ of LMS.

- If $\mu$ (or $1-\lambda$) is **small**: the steady-state error is small (=preserves the past well, **stability**), and
However, tracking is slow when the channel changes (=slow absorption of new, **low plasticity**).
- If $\mu$ is **large**: Change tracking is fast (**high plasticity**), but fluctuates in the steady state and past statistics
Forget(**Low Stability**).

Catastrophic forgetting corresponds to the phenomenon where an adaptive filter overwrites past statistics as it reconverges to new channel statistics. 
The "replay buffer" corresponds to storing past pilot/training samples.
:::

### Classification of CIL techniques (taxonomy)

```{mermaid}
flowchart TD
    T[CIL 방법론] --> R[Replay 기반]
    T --> G[정규화 기반]
    T --> P[파라미터 격리]
    R --> R1[과거 표본을 작은 버퍼에<br/>보관 후 새 과제와 함께 재생]
    G --> G1[Prior-focused<br/>이전 학습의 중요 파라미터를 고정<br/>예: EWC, Fisher 가중]
    G --> G2[Data-focused<br/>이전 모델의 출력을 정규화<br/>예: 지식 증류 LwF]
    P --> P1[새 과제는 새 파라미터/모델로<br/>나중에 병합 또는 라우팅]
```

- **Replay-based**: Stores a portion of past samples in a small cache (replay buffer) and uses them together when training new tasks.
Plays. The simplest and most powerful.
- **Normalization-based**
  - *Prior-focused*: Penalizes parameters that were important in the past to move less. Typical examples include
    **EWC(Elastic Weight Consolidation)** 는 Fisher 정보 $F_i$ 로 파라미터 중요도를 가중한다:
    $$
    \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\text{new}}(\theta)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_i\,(\theta_i-\theta^{*}_{\text{old},i})^2 .
    $$
    여기서 $F_i$ 는 과거 과제 손실의 곡률(2차 정보)을 근사하며, **로그-사전 $\log p(\theta)$ 를
    과거 사후분포로 둔 MAP**(@sec-inductive)의 한 형태로 볼 수 있다. 정보이론적으로 $F$ 는
    피셔 정보 행렬의 대각 근사다.
  - *Data-focused*: Knowledge distillation (e.g., LwF) that mimics the output (soft label) of the previous model.
- **Parameter Isolation**: New data is trained with a **new model/new parameters** and merged later. Forgetting is almost non-existent.
Although it does not exist, as the model continues to grow, storage costs become high, and a new problem arises regarding **deciding which model to use during inference**.

::: {.callout-note}
## Experimental Conclusion (Appendix @sec-appendix code)
In single-head setups with strong interference, simple **replay** 
tends to prevent forgetting much more robustly than regularization-based methods. In the appendix demo, naive sequential learning 
saw past task accuracy collapse to $1.00\!\to\!0.00$, but recovered to $0.97$ with only 20% replay.
:::

### More realistic setups (research trends)

Classical CIL assumes that it "looks only at a fixed class sample at each time point." This is unrealistic. 
It extends to a more realistic setup.

| Setup | Problem Awareness | Core Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Blurry / i-Blurry** | Do not view only one class at a time | Blur task boundaries (other classes appear with low probability), new classes also appear gradually + anytime inference |
| **Noisy labels** | Stream labels can be incorrect | Learning on a tainted label stream |
| **Boundary-free (Gaussian) stream** | Data distribution is a continuous function of time | Boundary-free continuous distribution + Data prior scheduling |
| **Hierarchical label expansion** | Differentiation from coarse classes to fine classes | Learning new finer classes using hierarchy |
| **Embodied agents** | Robots continuously learn new environments/behaviors | Integrates new environments/behaviors while preserving past concepts |
| **Name-only CL** | Human labelers are not always available | Automated web data collection and cleaning using LLM/VLM as a labeler |

::: {.callout-note}
## i-Blurry's Motivation (Probabilistic Intuition)
The "blurry" setup assumes that in $t{=}1$, dogs and cats are seen mixed in with a high probability, while cars and boats are seen mixed in with a low probability, such as 1% 
. However, if the sample size is sufficiently large (e.g., more than 100 per class), even a class with a 0.1% probability 
will **almost certainly appear at least once** at every point in time. Then, effectively "all classes are always visible," and it is no longer 
class-incremental. To fill this gap, the **i-Blurry** setup is designed so that some classes 
are added incrementally, thereby maintaining both realism and class-incremental properties.
:::

### Name-only / Human-free continuous learning

Human labelers or retraining data are not always available. In such cases, LLM and VLM are used as **alternative labelers**. 
If you simply provide the "names (semantics)" to the continuous learner, the rest (collecting new data from the web → automatic labeling → 
refining labels using LLM-image relationships → updating the model) is automated. Users do not need to collect datasets; 
they simply need to **state the concept names**.

## Beyond CIL: Continuous Learning of Generative and Large Models {#sec-cl-beyond}

Until now, the focus has been on $P(Y)$ (discrimination), but large generative models learn $P(X)$ or $P(X\mid Y)$. 
Modern large model training itself is, in fact, continuous learning.

::: {.callout-note}
## Data pool + data fetcher
Classical learning involved iterating through a fixed dataset multiple times. There is no fixed dataset in training large models. 
Instead, there is a **data pool** (where growth, refinement, and new inputs occur continuously) and a **model**, with a 
**data fetcher** connecting them. The fetcher selects subsets useful for updating the model. The smarter the fetcher, 
the better the model obtained with less computation. The concepts of a fixed dataset and multiple epochs disappear, and some samples 
may appear multiple times, while others may not appear even once. In other words, it is the **continuous learning paradigm itself**.
:::

LLM's Continuous Learning Pipeline (Concept):

| Abbreviation | Step |
|---|---|
| DAP | Domain-adaptive pre-training |
| SFT | Supervised fine-tuning |
| CIT | Continual instruction tuning |
| CMR | Continual model refinement |
| CMA | Continual model alignment |
| CMLLM | Continual multimodal LLM |

Here, the representative technique for **efficiently utilizing humans** is **RLHF**. 
It efficiently incorporates human preference feedback into model alignment (connected to the third strategy of few-/zero-shot, @sec-fewshot).

## Summary {#sec-summary}

::: {.callout-important}
## Key Summary
1. **few-shot (⊂ weak supervision)** is a difficult generalization problem. In the absence of data, inductive bias, unlabeled data,
Fills in as one of the efficient uses of people.
2. **zero-shot ≠ unsupervised.** Zero-shot has 0 examples of the class of interest only (extrapolated with auxiliary knowledge),
unsupervised has no label itself. They are mutually orthogonal axes.
3. **Normalization = negative log prior distribution.** Including inductive bias is equivalent to a MAP that assigns $p(\theta)$, and
MLE is a special case of a uniform dictionary.
4. **Continuous learning is a data and computationally efficient learning scheme**, which is particularly important for large models. However, we
I’ve been in **class-incremental** for too long — it’s time to break free.
5. The **stability–plasticity dilemma** (= convergence vs. tracking of adaptive filters) is the fundamental tension of continuous learning, and
replay, normalization (EWC), and parameter isolation are the prescriptions.
:::

## Appendix: NumPy Demo of Catastrophic Oblivion and Replay {#sec-appendix}

The code below reproduces the effects of **catastrophic oblivion** and **replay** using only NumPy, without external dependencies.

**Setup.** The input is 2D. The rule is domain-dependent (similar to XOR), so two tasks interfere with each other using the same parameters.

- Assignment A: Samples only from the left area ($x_1<0$), Labels = (Left & Top) or (Right & Bottom)
- Task B: Only samples from the right region ($x_1>0$), same rule

A single-head MLP can solve two tasks almost perfectly (upper bound) by looking at them **jointly**, but 
if trained sequentially (A→B), the decision boundary of A breaks down while only looking at B.

```{python}
#| label: cl-demo
#| fig-cap: "Naive 순차 학습은 과제 A를 파국적으로 잊지만, replay는 회복시킨다."
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)

# ---- 데이터: 영역 의존적 XOR 유사 규칙 ----
def lab(X):                       # +1 if (left & up) or (right & down)
    left, up = X[:, 0] < 0, X[:, 1] > 0
    return ((left & up) | ((~left) & (~up))).astype(float)

def region_A(n):                  # x<0 영역만 관측
    X = np.c_[rng.uniform(-2, 0, n), rng.uniform(-2, 2, n)]; return X, lab(X)
def region_B(n):                  # x>0 영역만 관측
    X = np.c_[rng.uniform(0, 2, n), rng.uniform(-2, 2, n)]; return X, lab(X)

XA, yA = region_A(2000); XB, yB = region_B(2000)
XAt, yAt = region_A(800); XBt, yBt = region_B(800)

# ---- 작은 2-layer MLP (tanh + sigmoid), 순수 NumPy ----
H = 64
def init():
    return [rng.normal(size=(2, H)) * 0.7, np.zeros(H),
            rng.normal(size=(H,)) * 0.7, np.zeros(1)]

def fwd(p, X):
    h = np.tanh(X @ p[0] + p[1]); z = h @ p[2] + p[3]
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-np.clip(z, -30, 30))), h

def grads(p, X, y):
    pr, h = fwd(p, X); d = (pr - y) / len(y)
    dh = np.outer(d, p[2]) * (1 - h ** 2)
    return [X.T @ dh, dh.sum(0), h.T @ d, np.array([d.sum()])]

def acc(p, X, y):
    return ((fwd(p, X)[0] > 0.5).astype(float) == y).mean()

def train(p, X, y, lr=0.3, ep=2500, bs=256):
    p = [w.copy() for w in p]; n = len(X)
    for _ in range(ep):
        i = rng.choice(n, bs, replace=False)
        g = grads(p, X[i], y[i])
        for k in range(len(p)): p[k] -= lr * g[k]
    return p

# ---- 1) 과제 A 학습 ----
pA = train(init(), XA, yA)
print(f"after A      : accA={acc(pA, XAt, yAt):.2f}")

# ---- 2) Joint 학습 (상한, 두 과제를 함께) ----
pJ = train(init(), np.vstack([XA, XB]), np.concatenate([yA, yB]), ep=5000)
print(f"[Joint]       accA={acc(pJ, XAt, yAt):.2f}  accB={acc(pJ, XBt, yBt):.2f}  (upper bound)")

# ---- 3) Naive 순차 (A 학습 후 B만 학습) → 파국적 망각 ----
pN = train(pA, XB, yB)
print(f"[Naive]       accA={acc(pN, XAt, yAt):.2f}  accB={acc(pN, XBt, yBt):.2f}")

# ---- 4) Replay (B 학습 시 과거 A 표본 20% 혼합) → 회복 ----
idx = rng.choice(len(XA), 500, replace=False)
pR = train(pA, np.vstack([XB, XA[idx]]), np.concatenate([yB, yA[idx]]))
print(f"[Replay]      accA={acc(pR, XAt, yAt):.2f}  accB={acc(pR, XBt, yBt):.2f}")
```

Expected output (fixed random seed):

```
after A      : accA=1.00
[Joint]       accA=0.99  accB=1.00  (upper bound)
[Naive]       accA=0.00  accB=1.00
[Replay]      accA=0.97  accB=1.00
```

**Interpretation.** Joint(upper bound) solves both tasks almost perfectly. However, Naive sequential learning 
completely overwrites the decision boundary of A while learning B, collapsing to $\text{accA}=0.00$ (catastrophic forgetting). 
Even replaying only 20% of the past samples nearly recovers the performance of A to $0.97$ — 
demonstrating why @sec-cl-modern's replay-based technique is simple yet powerful.

::: {.callout-note}
## Memo on Normalization Base (EWC)
When applying EWC-style Fisher-weighted penalties to the same setup, in this **strong interference, single head** configuration, both tasks 
get stuck near $\sim0.5$ and do not perform as well as replay. 
This is because being strongly fixed to the past posterior distribution results in a trade-off that satisfies neither task. This 
aligns with observations in the literature that "normalization-based approaches are not always the best" and that replay may be advantageous depending on the degree of interference and representation sharing between tasks.
:::
