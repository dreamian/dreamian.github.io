The central question of this topic is one: What must be learned to solve the optimality equation without a model, for each transition, and to which fixed-point equation is that learning applied the Robbins–Monroe algorithm? The algorithm is as simple as TD(0). It assigns weights \(1-\alpha\) to the old estimate and \(\alpha\) to the single-sample estimate on the right-hand side of the Bellman equation. The weight of understanding is placed in three places: (i) why \(Q^*\) rather than \(v^*\), (ii) why the action can be chosen with any policy (off-policy), and (iii) why search is still a problem. The convergence theorem is treated at the level of results and proof ideas.

Common notation:

  • discounted MDP \(\langle S,A,p,r,\gamma\rangle\), \(S\) and \(A\) are finite, \(\gamma\in(0,1)\).
  • \(Q^*\): Optimal Q-function. \(Q\): Estimator being trained (state-action lookup table).
  • Transition data \((s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1})\), \(r_t=r(s_t,a_t)\), \(s_{t+1}\sim p(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t)\).
  • \(T\): Bellman operator for Q-function. \(\mathcal F_t\): history up to time \(t\) (including action \(a_t\)).
  • \(\alpha_t(s,a)\): Step size of cell \((s,a)\). Cells not updated at time \(t\) are \(0\).
  • \(\|Q\|_\infty:=\max_{s,a}|Q(s,a)|\). The reward is the spirit world, \(|r|\le R_{\max}\).
  • \(\varepsilon\): ε-greedy search probability. \(\tau>0\): Boltzmann search temperature (written as \(\tau\) to distinguish it from the operator \(T\)).

3 0. Starting point

3.1 Can the optimality equation be solved without a model?

First question to consider. TD(0) was obtained by replacing the expected value of the policy evaluation equation \(v=T^\pi v\) with a single sample. What happens if we apply the same operation to the optimality equation \(v=Tv\)? And even if we obtain \(v^*\), what is required again when selecting a policy?

Answer. The problem to be solved

\[ \max_\pi\ \mathbb{E}^\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tr(s_t,a_t)\Big] \]

and the optimization target is policy. What is desired is a model-free variant of VI·PI. \(v=Tv\) has two stumbling blocks.

  1. max is outside the expected value. \(v(s)=\max_a\big[r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}[v(s')\mid s,a]\big]\). With a single sample, it is impossible to create a bias-free “max value by comparing the expected values for each action” → Section 4.2.2
  2. The model is needed again for policy extraction. \(\pi^*(s)\in\arg\max_a[r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v^*(s')]\) → Section 5.3.1

Solving the two problems at once is learning the state-action value function \(Q^*\). In the Bellman equation of \(Q^*\), the max falls inside the expectation value, and policy extraction is performed without a model as \(\arg\max_aQ^*(s,a)\).

TipSignificance

Q-learning is the first and most representative of the RL algorithms we will see, and it is the foundation of many algorithms such as DQN. However, there are almost no new ideas. It is simply the application of the TD operation of “writing the Bellman equation in the form of expectations and converting expectations into samples” to the equation \(Q^*\). What is new is the choice of what to apply it to.

4 I. Q-function and its Bellman equation

4.1 What is \(Q^*\)

First question to consider. \(v^*(s)\) is the “expected cumulative reward when doing one’s best, starting from state \(s\)”. If we group the first action into \(a\), what is the quantity? What if we maximize that with respect to \(a\)?

Answer. The optimal Q-function is the maximum expected cumulative reward that can be obtained starting from state \(s\) and fixing the first action to \(a\).

\[ Q^*(s,a):=\max_\pi Q^\pi(s,a)=\max_\pi\ \mathbb{E}^\pi\Big[\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tr(s_t,a_t)\,\Big|\,s_0=s,\ a_0=a\Big]. \]

The argument is not a single state, but a (state, action) pair. Policy \(\pi\) determines only the action of \(t\ge1\), and \(a_0=a\) is enforced. Directly from the definition

\[ \boxed{\,v^*(s)=\max_{a\in A}Q^*(s,a).\,} \]

Maximizing the optimal value with respect to the first action again results in the optimal value free up to the first action → Section 4.2.1

4.2 Bellman equation of \(Q^*\)

First question to consider. If value decomposition is applied to \(Q^*(s,a)\), what fixes the immediate reward part and what becomes the remainder? Can that remainder be rewritten as \(Q^*\)?

Answer. Since action \(a\) is fixed, the immediate reward \(r(s,a)\) is fixed. The remainder is the expected value of the optimal value from the next state, and since \(v^*(s')=\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\),

\[ Q^*(s,a)=\underbrace{r(s,a)}_{\text{immediate reward}}+\gamma\sum_{s'\in S}p(s'\mid s,a)\underbrace{v^*(s')}_{\text{optimal value of next state}} =r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'\in S}p(s'\mid s,a)\max_{a'\in A}Q^*(s',a'). \]

Define the entire right-hand side as an operator.

\[ (TQ)(s,a):=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'\in S}p(s'\mid s,a)\max_{a'\in A}Q(s',a'). \]

Then the Bellman equation is a fixed-point problem.

\[ \boxed{\,Q=TQ.\,} \]

4.2.1 ✍ Derivation: \(Q^*=r+\gamma Pv^*\) and \(v^*=\max Q^*\)

Guide Question:

  1. Take \(\max_\pi\) from the decomposition of Section 4.1.1. Why is it \(\le r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v^*(s')\)?
  2. Why does the equality hold? What result of MDP theory is required?
  3. Show that maximizing the result of 1–2 for \(a\) yields \(v^*=\max_aQ^*\). Write the optimality equation \(v^*=Tv^*\).
  4. Combine the two results to get \(Q^*=TQ^*\).
NoteSolution

1. For all \(\pi\) and \(s'\), since \(v^\pi(s')\le v^*(s')\) \[ Q^\pi(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v^\pi(s')\le r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v^*(s'). \] The inequality holds even when taking \(\max_\pi\).

2. In the discounted MDP, there exists a stationary deterministic optimal policy \(\pi^*\) that is \(v^{\pi^*}=v^*\) simultaneously in all states. If we fix only the first move to \(a\) and follow \(s_1\) through \(\pi^*\), we reach the upper bound exactly. Therefore \[ Q^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v^*(s'). \] “Simultaneously” is the key. Regardless of the next state, the optimal value of that state must be obtainable with a single, consistent policy in order to raise each term within the expected value to \(v^*(s')\) simultaneously.

3. Maximizing the result of 2 for \(a\) \[ \max_aQ^*(s,a)=\max_a\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v^*(s')\Big]=(Tv^*)(s)=v^*(s). \] The last equality is the optimality equation.

4. If we write 3 to \(s'\), we get \(v^*(s')=\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\). If we substitute this into 2, we get \(Q^*=TQ^*\).

4.2.2 What is different from the equation \(v\): the position of the max

First question to consider. Where is \(\max\) located in each of the two equations? Why is that location critical for sample-based learning?

Answer.

\[ \begin{aligned} v^*(s)&=\max_a\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}\big[v^*(s')\mid s,a\big]\Big]&&\text{max가 기댓값 \textbf{밖}},\\ Q^*(s,a)&=r(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\,\Big|\,s,a\Big]&&\text{max가 기댓값 \textbf{안}}. \end{aligned} \]

The equation \(v\) covers the maximization of choosing an action from the current state. In the equation \(Q\), since the current action is already fixed as \(a\), the remaining maximization is how to optimally choose the next action from the next state. Since that choice is made after seeing the arrived \(s'\), \(\max\) falls within the expectation for \(s'\).

NoteRationale: What can be made without bias using a single sample?

Let’s assume one observation \((s,a,r,s')\) is given.

  • \(Q\) Page: Since \(r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\) is a function of \(s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)\), its conditional expectation is exactly \((TQ)(s,a)\). It is an unbiased single-sample estimate.
  • Page \(v\): To estimate \(\max_a\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\mid s,a]\), an expected value is needed for each action. However, only the result of a single action is observed at a time. If we take a sample for each action and substitute it with \(\max_a(\text{샘플}_a)\), then by Jensen \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\max_a X_a\big]\ \ge\ \max_a\mathbb{E}[X_a] \] is biased upward. The order of the expected value and the max cannot be changed.
TipSignificance

Since max is within the expectation, the equation for \(Q^*\) becomes “Expectation = Sample Mean”, and Robbins–Monro can be applied directly → Section 5.1. This is the first reason for training \(Q^*\) instead of \(v^*\). The second reason is policy extraction → Section 5.3

4.3 The monotoniness and contraction of \(T\)

First question to consider. What was the basis for \(T\) being monotonic and \(\gamma\) being contracted with respect to \(v\)? Does the same basis apply to \(T\) with respect to \(Q\)? What new inequality is needed?

Answer. The Bellman operator for the Q-function is also monotonic and is \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\)-contraction from \(\gamma\).

\[ Q\le Q'\ \Rightarrow\ TQ\le TQ',\qquad \|TQ-TQ'\|_\infty\le\gamma\|Q-Q'\|_\infty. \]

By the Banach fixed point theorem, the fixed point of \(T\) is unique, and it is \(Q^*\). Only one new inequality is required.

\[ \Big|\max_af(a)-\max_ag(a)\Big|\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|. \]

Guide Question:

  1. Prove the max inequality above . Find the maximum point \(a_1\) of \(f\).
  2. Show monotony.
  3. Show the abbreviation . Where does \(r(s,a)\) go? Where is \(\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)=1\) used?
NoteSolution

If you set it as 1. \(a_1\in\arg\max_af(a)\) \[ \max_af-\max_ag\le f(a_1)-g(a_1)\le\max_a|f(a)-g(a)|. \] If you swap the roles of \(f\) and \(g\), the opposite direction is the same.

2. If \(Q\le Q'\), then at each \(s'\)Z, it is \(\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\le\max_{a'}Q'(s',a')\). Since it is \(p\ge0\), the weighted sum also preserves order. Therefore, \(TQ\le TQ'\).

3. The compensation term is canceled out of the difference. \[ \big|(TQ)(s,a)-(TQ')(s,a)\big| =\gamma\Big|\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\Big(\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-\max_{a'}Q'(s',a')\Big)\Big| \le\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\,\|Q-Q'\|_\infty=\gamma\|Q-Q'\|_\infty. \] Write the triangle inequality and number 1 in the inequality sign, and finally write \(\sum_{s'}p=1\). Take the maximum for \((s,a)\) on the left side, and you are done.

4.4 When the model is known: Value iteration using Q-function

First question to consider. If \(Q=TQ\) is a contracted fixed-point problem, what is the simplest solution? Which part of the iteration uses transition probabilities?

Answer. Starting from an arbitrary \(Q_0\), repeatedly apply \(T\).

  1. Initializes \(Q_0\).
  2. For all \((s,a)\) \(Q_{k+1}(s,a)\leftarrow r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\max_{a'}Q_k(s',a')\).
  3. \(k\leftarrow k+1\). Repeat until convergence.

Due to the conciseness of \(T\), it is \(\|Q_k-Q^*\|_\infty\le\gamma^k\|Q_0-Q^*\|_\infty\), and the converged Q-function is optimal. In every iteration, all \((s,a)\) are updated (synchronous).

The problem is that the transition probability is required for the calculation of the expected value in step 2, \(\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)(\cdot)\).

\[ \textbf{Q: How can we perform VI using samples?} \]

NoteBasis: It is a repetition like the state value VI.

If you set it to \(v_k:=\max_aQ_k(\cdot,a)\) \[ v_{k+1}(s)=\max_a\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)v_k(s')\Big]=(Tv_k)(s). \] Q-VI is simply storing the “median value by action” in the state value VI.

5 II. Q-learning

5.1 Sample VI with Stochastic Approximation

First question to consider. In Step 2 of the Q-VI, to replace the expected value \(\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\) with a single sample, which of the transition data \((s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1})\) should be inserted? Which cell can be modified with that sample?

Answer. Input the observed \(s_{t+1}\) as is. Weight the old estimate with \(1-\alpha\), and the new estimate (a single-sample estimate of the compensation signal \(r_t\) and the entire expectation) with \(\alpha\).

\[ Q(s_t,a_t):=(1-\alpha)\,Q(s_t,a_t)+\alpha\Big[r_t+\gamma\max_{a'\in A}Q(s_{t+1},a')\Big]. \]

If you group it as \(\alpha\)

\[ \boxed{\,Q(s_t,a_t):=Q(s_t,a_t)+\alpha\Big[\underbrace{r_t+\gamma\max_{a'\in A}Q(s_{t+1},a')}_{\text{new estimate}}-\underbrace{Q(s_t,a_t)}_{\text{old estimate}}\Big].\,} \]

The part inside the parentheses is the learning direction, which is the same shape as the temporal difference of TD(0). Since only one transfer samples are required, it is completely online. The only cell to edit is \((s_t,a_t)\), where the sample appeared.

ImportantAll updates

Weights \(1-\alpha\) to the old Q estimate and \(\alpha\) to the single-sample estimate on the right-hand side of the Q-function Bellman equation.

Even if an action is selected via a random policy, the condition is applied using that value once it has been selected.

5.2 Algorithms and Personality

Answer.

Q-learning algorithm

  1. Initialize to \(Q\equiv0\) and \(t\leftarrow0\). (Since it is a finite state/behavior, it can be initialized with an arbitrary table, but it is usually set to 0.)
  2. From state \(t\) at time \(s_t\), select a random action \(a_t\) and proceed the system to \(t+1\).
  3. Update cell \((s_t,a_t)\) with sample \((s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1})\). \[ Q(s_t,a_t)\leftarrow Q(s_t,a_t)+\alpha_t\Big[r_t+\gamma\max_{a'\in A}Q(s_{t+1},a')-Q(s_t,a_t)\Big]. \]
  4. Set to \(t\leftarrow t+1\) and go to step 2.

After convergence, the policy is made into \(\pi^*(s)\in\arg\max_{a\in A}Q(s,a)\) → Section 5.3

Personality:

  • Model-free, fully online. Updates once for each transition.
  • It is completely incremental. \((s_t,a_t)\) only one cell changes at a time. As the state-action space grows, it takes a very long time to sufficiently visit all cells. In large problems, this naive tabular version cannot be used, and a functional approximation is required.
  • There are degrees of freedom in action selection. The “random action” in step 2 is an off-policy property → Section 5.4
TipSignificance

A model-free, fully online algorithm is derived from just two steps: “Apply DP to the Q-function to obtain the Bellman equation → Apply SA to it.” The rest of the discussion (policy extraction, off-policy, search, convergence) is entirely about why and when this simple update formula works.

5.3 Policy Extraction: Why \(Q\) instead of \(v\)

First question to consider. We obtained a sufficiently good \(Q\). Is a transition probability necessary when creating a policy? What if we trained \(v^*\) in the same way?

Answer. From the current state, choose the action that maximizes \(Q\).

\[ \pi^*(s)\in\arg\max_{a\in A}Q(s,a). \]

At this stage, transition probabilities are not used. It is completely model-free up to policy extraction. This is the decisive reason for training \(Q\).

If \(v^*\) were trained, the model would need to be retrained for policy extraction → Section 5.3.1. There is a trade-off. Since \(Q^*\) contains more information with \(|S||A|\) digits than \(v^*\), training is more difficult and requires more samples.

5.3.1 If you only trained ✍ \(v^*\)

Guide Question:

  1. Write the formula to select the optimal policy from \(v^*\). Where does the model go?
  2. What is inside the square brackets of that expression?
  3. Show that \(\arg\max_aQ^*(s,a)\) is actually the optimal policy.
NoteSolution

1. \[ \pi^*(s)\in\arg\max_a\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\,v^*(s')\Big]. \] Both \(r(s,a)\) and \(p(s'\mid s,a)\) are required. Even if you go to the trouble of training \(v^*\) as a model-free model, the model is needed again at the end.

2. According to result 2 of Section 4.2.1, the inside of the brackets is exactly \(Q^*(s,a)\). \(Q^*\) is a “calculation looking one step ahead with the model” that has been pre-calculated and stored.

3. Regarding the deterministic policy \(\pi(s)\in\arg\max_aQ^*(s,a)\) \[ (T^\pi v^*)(s)=r(s,\pi(s))+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,\pi(s))v^*(s')=Q^*(s,\pi(s))=\max_aQ^*(s,a)=v^*(s). \] , that is, \(v^*=T^\pi v^*\). \(T^\pi\) is a contraction, and since the fixed point is uniquely \(v^\pi\), it is \(v^\pi=v^*\).

TipSignificance

There are two reasons for training \(Q^*\) when combined with Section 4.2.2: (i) The max is within the expected value, so the single-sample estimate is unbiased; and (ii) A model is not required for policy extraction. This is because both have absorbed the “model looking one step ahead” into \(Q\).

5.4 Off-policy

First question to consider. Must the policy for selecting action \(a_t\) in Step 2 be the same as the policy targeted by learning (greedy for \(Q^*\))?

Answer. It does not have to be the same. Q-learning learns properly regardless of the policy used to generate behavior. This is called an off-policy algorithm.

\[ Q(s_t,a_t)\leftarrow Q(s_t,a_t)+\alpha_t\Big[r_t+\gamma\max_{a'\in A}Q(s_{t+1},a')-Q(s_t,a_t)\Big]. \]

What goes into the target value is not the actual next action to be taken, but \(\max_{a'}\), that is, the selection of the optimal policy, which is the learning goal. Therefore, the policy that generates the action does not appear in the target value.

Result:

  • It has very high flexibility. You can choose any behavior, such as greedy, ε-greedy, or Boltzmann.
  • Already collected samples can be reused to update. Experience replay utilizes this property when scaling to larger problems (DQN), and it improves sample efficiency.

↓ Down: Section 5.4.1 ↑ Up: Section 3.1

5.4.1 ✍ Why is it acceptable to choose actions arbitrarily?

Guide Question:

  1. Where does the policy that selects \(a_t\) appear under the noise condition \(\mathbb{E}[w_t\mid\mathcal F_t]=0\) of Section 5.1.1?
  2. Then, do behavior policies have no effect on learning? What do they affect?
  3. If the \(\max_{a'}Q(s_{t+1},a')\) of the target value is replaced with the \(Q(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})\) of the \(a_{t+1}\) of the next action actually taken, what is the SA for which fixed-point equation?
  4. Why is it okay to reuse \((s,a,r,s')\) collected in the past?
NoteSolution

1. It does not appear. The conditional expectation depends only on what value \(a_t\) is, and not on how that value was chosen. The distribution of \(s_{t+1}\) is only \(p(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t)\), and there is no action policy in the definition of \(T\). Therefore, regardless of which action policy is used, the fixed point that SA points to is the same \(Q^*\).

2. There is an influence. The action policy determines how often a cell is updated. It does not change fixed points, but determines (i) whether the sum of the step sizes of each cell \(\sum_t\alpha_t(s,a)\) diverges (infinite visit condition) → Section 7.1.1, and (ii) the convergence speed. If the action policy never selects a cell, that cell remains at its initial value. This is a search problem → Section 6.1

If you choose 3. \(a_{t+1}\sim\mu(\cdot\mid s_{t+1})\) \[ \mathbb{E}\big[Q(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})\mid\mathcal F_t\big]=\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s_t,a_t)\sum_{a'}\mu(a'\mid s')Q(s',a'). \] This is the Bellman operator \(T^\mu\) for the action policy \(\mu\), and the fixed point is \(Q^\mu\). In this case, the learning target becomes the value of the policy itself that generated the data (on-policy). The reason Q-learning is off-policy is precisely \(\max\) of the target value.

4. In the stored \((s,a,r,s')\), \(s'\) is still a sample of \(p(\cdot\mid s,a)\). The target value \(r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\) can be recalculated using the current \(Q\), and its conditional expectation is \((TQ)(s,a)\) for the current \(Q\). It does not matter when or under what policy the data was generated.

TipSignificance

Off-policy separates “collecting data” from “what to learn.” Thanks to this, the data collection policy can be designed to be dedicated to exploration. However, separation does not mean that exploration is resolved. Even if the learning goal is independent of the action policy, learning actually occurs only in the cells visited by the action policy.

6 III. Search

6.1 Problems with Greedy policies

First question to consider. If we only use greedy actions on the current \(Q\) during training, which cells remain unupdated? If there are unupdated cells, which assumption of the Convergence Theorem is violated?

Answer. The Greedy policy chooses the action that appears most beneficial at the current \(Q\) (exploitation).

\[ \pi(s)\in\arg\max_{a\in A}Q(s,a),\qquad a_t=\pi(s_t). \]

If \(Q\) is optimal, then the greedy policy is optimal. However, the probability that \(Q\) is near the optimal value early in training is very low.

The problem is search. If only a greedy policy is used, some state-action pairs may never be visited. In that case, the information for those cells can never be obtained, and convergence to the optimal Q-factor is not guaranteed. Even if all states are visited, certain beneficial actions may never be attempted.

↓ Down: Section 6.1.1 · Section 6.1.2 ↑ Up: Section 3.1

6.1.1 ✍ Two action examples

There is one state (\(s\)) and two actions. Regardless of the action taken, it returns to \(s\). The rewards are deterministically \(r(s,a_1)=1\) and \(r(s,a_2)=2\). A greedy policy is used to start at \(Q\equiv0\) and choose \(a_1\) in case of a tie. \(\alpha_t\in(0,1]\).

Guide Question:

  1. \(Q^*(s,a_1)\), \(Q^*(s,a_2)\) and what is the optimal action?
  2. What is the sign of \(Q(s,a_1)\) after the first update? What does the greedy policy choose from then on?
  3. What happens to \(Q(s,a_2)\)? Where does \(Q(s,a_1)\) converge?
  4. Is the greedy policy optimal after training?
NoteSolution

1. \(v^*=\max_aQ^*(s,a)\) and \(Q^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma v^*\). If you always choose \(a_2\), then \(v^*=2/(1-\gamma)\). \[ Q^*(s,a_2)=\frac{2}{1-\gamma},\qquad Q^*(s,a_1)=1+\frac{2\gamma}{1-\gamma}. \] Since \(Q^*(s,a_2)-Q^*(s,a_1)=1>0\), the optimal action is \(a_2\).

2. Since it is a tie, \(a_1\) is selected, followed by \(Q(s,a_1)\leftarrow0+\alpha_0(1+\gamma\cdot0-0)=\alpha_0>0\). Now that it is \(Q(s,a_1)>0=Q(s,a_2)\), the greedy continues to select \(a_1\). Afterwards, \(Q(s,a_1)\) is not decreased (the 3 updates below increase it while it is \(Q<1/(1-\gamma)\)).

3. \(a_2\) is never selected, so it remains in \(Q(s,a_2)=0\) forever. The update of \(Q(s,a_1)\) is \[ Q(s,a_1)\leftarrow Q(s,a_1)+\alpha_t\big[1+\gamma\max\{Q(s,a_1),0\}-Q(s,a_1)\big]=Q(s,a_1)+\alpha_t\big[1+(\gamma-1)Q(s,a_1)\big] \] , and the fixed point is \(Q=1+\gamma Q\), i.e., \(1/(1-\gamma)\). At an appropriate step size, \(Q(s,a_1)\to1/(1-\gamma)\).

4. In the learned \(Q\), the greedy value is \(a_1\). It is not optimal. Furthermore, the values of already visited cells are also incorrect. It is \(1/(1-\gamma)<Q^*(s,a_1)\). This is because incorrect values from unvisited cells (\(Q(s,a_2)=0\)) are mixed within the target value \(\max_{a'}Q(s,a')\). The damage from the search failure does not stop at unvisited cells but spreads through bootstrapping.

TipSignificance

It is important to note that the off-policy property, where the policy selecting the action does not appear in the target value, is not broken in this example. The fixed point of Q-learning is still \(Q^*\). What is wrong is not the fixed point, but the assumption of the convergence theorem is broken because it is \(\sum_t\alpha_t(s,a_2)=0\) in the \((s,a_2)\) cell → Section 7.1.1

6.1.2 Exploitation–exploration trade-off

Answer.

  • To obtain a near-optimal policy with Q-learning, we must explore all potentially beneficial state-action pairs to gather information.
  • If a greedy policy is used for every update, certain beneficial actions may never be attempted even if all states are explored.
  • To overcome this, you must sacrifice some of the immediate gains you can obtain now through greedy choices and explore thoroughly.
  • This trade-off arises from a lack of knowledge regarding system dynamics and compensation structures. To acquire that knowledge, one must search instead of focusing on current gains.
TipSignificance

If the model is known, no search is needed. The VI calculates every \((s,a)\) every time. The search problem is the price paid for being model-free, and it arises in all sequential decision problems where the model is unknown, not limited to RL. This problem will be addressed again later using methods such as entropy normalization.

↑ Go Up: Section 6.1

6.2 ε-greedy and other methods

First question to think of. How can you make all actions continuously attempted by slightly modifying a greedy policy?

Answer.

ε-greedy. Choose a greedy action with probability \(1-\varepsilon\) and a random action with probability \(\varepsilon\). In other words, search for a probability of \(\varepsilon\).

\[ \Pr(a_t=a\mid s_t=s)= \begin{cases} 1-\varepsilon+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{|A|}, & a=\arg\max_{a'}Q(s,a')\ (\text{유일하다면}),\\[6pt] \dfrac{\varepsilon}{|A|}, & \text{그 밖}. \end{cases} \]

Another method:

  • Segment Switching. (i) Alternate between search segments and (ii) greedy segments. Use random actions in search segments and greedy actions in greedy segments.
  • Boltzmann Search. Probability of action \(a\) from state \(s\) \[ \frac{\exp\big(Q(s,a)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{a'\in A}\exp\big(Q(s,a')/\tau\big)} \] Select . \(\tau>0\) is the temperature parameter → Section 6.2.2

ε-greedy is the most widely used in Q-learning. This is not because it is efficient, but because it is simple and allows scheduling of ε.

NoteRationale: ε-greedy attempts all actions infinitely in a visited state.

Let us assume that a state \(s\) is visited infinitely often. At each visit, the conditional probability that each action is selected, regardless of the past, is at least \(\varepsilon/|A|>0\). Since the sum of conditional probabilities diverges (a conditional version of the Borel–Cantelli lemma), each action is selected infinitely with a probability of 1.

However, this is the case if states are visited infinitely. Whether all states are visited infinitely depends on the connection structure of the MDP.

↓ Down: Section 6.2.1 · Section 6.2.2 ↑ Up: Section 3.1

6.2.1 ε scheduling

First question to consider. How does the quality of \(Q\) differ between the early and late stages of training? Should one start with a large ε or a small ε?

Answer. Start with a large ε and gradually decrease.

  • Initial: Since there is almost no known information, focus on searching to improve the quality of \(Q\).
  • Review: If \(Q\) becomes reliable, reduce ε and use greedy behavior more.

ε can also be fixed from beginning to end.

NoteReason: If you reduce too quickly

There is a tension in the rate at which ε is reduced, similar to the SA step size, which dictates that it “should be reduced, but not too quickly.” If the search probability at the \(k\)-th visit of a certain state is \(\varepsilon_k\), then \(\sum_k\varepsilon_k=\infty\) is required for an infinite search. For example, \(\varepsilon_k=1/k\) is sufficient, but \(\varepsilon_k=1/k^2\) allows for only a finite number of searches with a probability of 1 (Borel–Cantelli). The goal of schedule design is to ensure that the search continues infinitely while eventually approaching greediness.

TipSignificance

It is the principle that “the more information is accumulated, the more one trusts their own estimate.” It is a design in the same direction as starting with a large λ in TD(λ) and reducing it.

6.2.2 ✍ Two limits of Boltzmann search

Guide Question:

  1. If \(\tau\to0^+\), what is the probability? Assume that \(\arg\max\) is unique.
  2. \(\tau\to\infty\) If?
  3. Does the probability change when the same constant \(c\) is added to all \(Q(s,\cdot)\)? How is it different compared to ε-greedy?
NoteSolution

If we set 1. \(a^*:=\arg\max_aQ(s,a)\) and \(\Delta_a:=Q(s,a^*)-Q(s,a)\) and divide the numerator and denominator by \(\exp(Q(s,a^*)/\tau)\) \[ \Pr(a)=\frac{e^{-\Delta_a/\tau}}{\sum_{a'}e^{-\Delta_{a'}/\tau}}. \] If \(\tau\to0^+\), then \(a\ne a^*\), since \(e^{-\Delta_a/\tau}\to0\), \(\Pr(a^*)\to1\). It is a greedy policy.

2. Since all exponents go to 0, \(\Pr(a)\to1/|A|\). is uniformly random.

3. Since \(e^{c/\tau}\) is canceled out in the numerator and denominator, it does not change. The probability depends only on the difference \(\Delta_a\) of the \(Q\) values. ε-greedy selects all non-greedy actions with equal probability \(\varepsilon/|A|\), whereas Boltzmann selects actions with small differences (almost good) more often and actions with large differences less often. Since the probability of every action is always positive, the search continues.

TipSignificance

\(\tau\) is a handle that acts like ε. Starting with the large \(\tau\) and reducing it, it transitions from search to utilization.

7 IV. Convergence

7.1 Convergence Theorem

Answer.

ImportantTheorem (Q-learning convergence)

Each state-action pair is visited infinitely many times and \[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty\alpha_t(s,a)=+\infty,\qquad\sum_{t=0}^\infty\alpha_t^2(s,a)<+\infty \] If , then for each \((s,a)\), \(Q(s,a)\) converges to \(Q^*(s,a)\) with probability 1.

The proof idea is the two axes of the SA convergence theorem.

  • \(T\) is a contraction map → Section 4.3.1
  • The noise term satisfies the conditional mean and conditional variance conditions.

7.1.2 What organization means in reality

Answer. It is sufficient to know the fact that there is a theoretical guarantee and its structure (reduction + noise conditions). Even when running tabular Q-learning on simple problems, it is very difficult to perfectly learn \(Q^*\).

  • The core assumption of “visiting all state-action pairs infinitely” cannot be satisfied in reality.
  • Even with ε-greedy and ε-scheduling, the search problem cannot be perfectly solved. ε-greedy is a simple but not efficient search method, and it can be theoretically shown to be inefficient.
  • Since updates occur one cell at a time, even a slight increase in the state-action space makes the time required to sufficiently visit all cells unmanageable.
TipSignificance

The two items following are the two limitations of Q-learning: search and scalability. The scalability problem leads to approximate Q-learning, which approximates the Q-function with a parameterized structure (weight vector) and learns those weights.
