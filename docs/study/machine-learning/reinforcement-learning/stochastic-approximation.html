3 0. Starting point

3.1 Can the fixed-point problem be solved model-free?

First question to consider. Where exactly was the transition probability \(p\) used at each step of VI and PI? If you do not know \(p\)Z and can only obtain the transition sample \((s,a,r,s')\), what can be used to replace it?

Answer. VI and PI are both methods for solving the fixed-point problem. And the model is placed in the expected value position, \(\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)(\cdot)\), inside the Bellman operator. The expected value can be approximated by samples. However, a single sample can differ significantly from the expected value. Therefore, stochastic approximation (SA) is designed so that this error (noise) is averaged out and disappears through iteration.

\[ \textbf{Key question: } \text{How can we solve fixed point problems in a model-free manner?} \]

TipSignificance

SA is the theoretical framework for value-based RL algorithms (TD, Q-learning). Subsequently, Q-learning is derived as “applying Robbins–Monroe SA to the Bellman equation for \(Q^*\)” → Section 5.1.2

↓ Scroll Down: Section 3.1.1

3.1.1 What kind of fixed-point problem was VI·PI?

Answer.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{정책 평가:}\quad & v=T^\pi v &&\Longleftrightarrow\quad v(s)=r(s,\pi(s))+\gamma\textstyle\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,\pi(s))\,v(s'),\\ \text{최적성 방정식:}\quad & v=Tv &&\Longleftrightarrow\quad v(s)=\max_a\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\textstyle\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\,v(s')\Big]. \end{aligned} \]

\(T^\pi\) is an affine operator, and \(T\) is a non-linear operator because of \(\max\). Both are contractions of \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) to \(\gamma\). Therefore, by the Banach fixed point theorem, \(v_{k+1}=Tv_k\) (or \(T^\pi v_k\)) converges to the unique fixed point. However, \(p\) is required for each application of the operator.

TipSignificance

If PI’s policy improvement phase uses \(Q^\pi\), it becomes \(\pi'(s)\in\arg\max_aQ^\pi(s,a)\), causing the model to disappear. The remaining problem is the evaluation phase and VI fixed point calculation. SA targets precisely this area.

4 I. Model of Stochastic Approximation

4.1 Deterministic repetitions and relaxation repetitions

Questions to consider first. Do \(x_{k+1}=Hx_k\) and \(x_{k+1}=(1-\alpha_k)x_k+\alpha_kHx_k\) have the same fixed point? If \(H\) is a contraction of \(\beta\), is the mapping of the second iteration also a contraction?

Answer. The two deterministic iterations for the general fixed-point problem \(Hx=x\) are as follows.

\[ x_{k+1}=Hx_k,\qquad x_{k+1}=(1-\alpha_k)x_k+\alpha_kHx_k,\quad \alpha_k\in(0,1]. \]

The second equation is a weighted average with weights of \(1-\alpha_k\) for the previous estimate and \(Hx_k\) for the new information \(\alpha_k\). The fixed points of the two iterations are the same, and if \(H\) is a reduction, both converge to \(x^*\).

NoteBasis

Let’s set the constant \(\alpha\) to \(H_\alpha:=(1-\alpha)I+\alpha H\).

  • The fixed point is the same: \(H_\alpha x=x \iff \alpha(Hx-x)=0 \iff Hx=x\).
  • Reduction Factor: If \(H\) is \(\|\cdot\|\)-reduction for \(\beta\) \[ \|H_\alpha x-H_\alpha y\|\le(1-\alpha)\|x-y\|+\alpha\beta\|x-y\|=\big(1-\alpha(1-\beta)\big)\|x-y\|. \] Since \(1-\alpha(1-\beta)<1\), \(H_\alpha\) is also a reduction. As the coefficients approach 1 more than \(\beta\), convergence slows down in deterministic cases.
TipSignificance

In critical cases, there seems to be no reason to use relaxation iterations. However, the situation changes if you use a noisy value instead of \(Hx_k\). In that case, \(\alpha_k\) becomes the handle that determines how much to trust the noise. This is the core of SA → Section 4.2

↑ Go Up: Section 3.1

4.2 When \(H\) is unknown: Repeat with noisy observations

First question to consider. When \(Hx_k\) cannot be calculated exactly, what must be assumed to be accessible in order to continue the iteration?

Answer. Consider the case where the structure of \(H\) is completely unknown or it is difficult to calculate \(Hx_k\) accurately. Instead, assume that access to the following random variables is available.

\[ s_k=Hx_k+w_k,\qquad w_k:\text{ random noise}. \]

\(Hx_k\) and \(w_k\) are not observed individually, and only their sum \(s_k\) is observed. If \(s_k\) is substituted for \(Hx_k\) in the relaxation iteration, it becomes a stochastic approximation algorithm.

\[ x_{k+1}:=(1-\alpha_k)x_k+\alpha_k\underbrace{(Hx_k+w_k)}_{s_k}. \]

  • \(s_k\) can often be obtained from sample data (simulations or experiments).
  • \(s_k\) may be very different from \(Hx_k\).
  • However, if appropriate conditions are applied to the noise \(w_k\), the difference does not significantly affect the calculation of \(x^*\) in the long run.

4.2.1 Reasons why noise can disappear over time

Answer. If the update equation is written by separating it into the movement direction and noise, it is as follows.

\[ x_{k+1}=x_k+\alpha_k\underbrace{(Hx_k-x_k)}_{\text{평균적 방향}}+\alpha_k\underbrace{w_k}_{\text{잡음}}. \]

When accumulated up to the step \(k\), the contribution of noise is in the form \(\sum_{j<k}\alpha_jw_j\). If the conditional mean of the noise is 0, this sum is a martingale. Therefore, they cancel each other out, and if the step size decreases sufficiently quickly, the tail goes to 0. On the other hand, the contribution in the average direction \(Hx_k-x_k\) continues to accumulate in proportion to \(\sum\alpha_k\). Eventually, the noise is averaged out and disappears, and the trajectory follows a deterministic iteration.

TipSignificance
  • This intuition directly translates into two types of assumptions. The noise side is a conditional mean of 0 and bounded variance → Section 7.1. The step size side is that the average direction accumulates endlessly (\(\sum\alpha=\infty\)), and the noise is tamed (\(\sum\alpha^2<\infty\)) → Section 6.2
  • When viewed as a small \(\alpha_k\), the locus follows the ODE \(\dot x=Hx-x\) (ODE method). If \(H\) is a contraction, the equilibrium point \(x^*\) of this ODE is globally asymptotically stable.

5 II. Robbins–Monro

5.1 Viewing the Bellman equation as \(x=\mathbb{E}[f(x,\xi)]\)

First question to consider. When the expected value is unknown, what is the simplest way to approximate it using \(N\) samples? What is the problem with using that method in every iteration?

Answer. View the Bellman equation in the following abstract form.

\[ x=\mathbb{E}[f(x,\xi)],\qquad \xi:\text{ 확률 변수}. \]

Given sample \(\{\hat\xi_1,\dots,\hat\xi_N\}\), the process can be repeated by replacing the expected value with the sample mean.

\[ x_{k+1}:=(1-\alpha_k)x_k+\alpha_k\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Nf(x_k,\hat\xi_i). \]

However, since \(N\) must be evaluated in every iteration, computational efficiency is poor if \(N\) is large. Therefore, only one sample is used per iteration.

\[ \boxed{\,x_{k+1}:=(1-\alpha_k)x_k+\alpha_kf(x_k,\hat\xi_k)\,}\qquad\text{(Robbins–Monro 알고리즘)} \]

TipSignificance: Relationship with SGD

Robbins–Monro is originally an algorithm \(x_{k+1}=x_k-\alpha_kG(x_k,\hat\xi_k)\) that finds the roots of \(g(x):=\mathbb{E}[G(x,\xi)]=0\). To transform this into a fixed-point form, one can set it to \(Hx:=x-g(x)\). In particular, if it is \(G=\nabla_xF\), it becomes SGD that minimizes \(\mathbb{E}[F(x,\xi)]\). Therefore, the step size condition of SGD and the step size condition of SA share the same root.

5.1.1 ✍ Why is single-sample update SA, and why is the noise mean 0?

Guide Question:

  1. To fit the Robbins–Monro expression into the form \(x_{k+1}=(1-\alpha_k)x_k+\alpha_k(Hx_k+w_k)\), what should \(Hx_k\) and \(w_k\) be defined as, respectively?
  2. If \(\hat\xi_k\) is drawn from the same distribution as \(\xi\) independently of the past, what is the conditional mean of \(w_k\) when \(x_k\) is given?
NoteSolution

1. \[ Hx_k:=\mathbb{E}[f(x_k,\xi)],\qquad w_k:=f(x_k,\hat\xi_k)-\mathbb{E}[f(x_k,\xi)]. \] Then \(Hx_k+w_k=f(x_k,\hat\xi_k)\), so Robbins–Monro is a special case of SA. Here, the expected value is taken only for \(\xi\), while fixing \(x_k\).

2. \(x_k\) is measurable with respect to \(\mathcal F_k\) and since \(\hat\xi_k\perp\mathcal F_k\) \[ \mathbb{E}[w_k\mid\mathcal F_k]=\mathbb{E}\big[f(x_k,\hat\xi_k)\mid\mathcal F_k\big]-\mathbb{E}[f(x_k,\xi)]=\mathbb{E}[f(x_k,\xi)]-\mathbb{E}[f(x_k,\xi)]=0. \]

TipSignificance

Among the noise conditions of SA, “conditional mean 0(Section 7.1)” is not an artificial assumption. It holds automatically if the expected value is substituted with a sample. It is sufficient that the sample is drawn without bias.

5.1.2 ✍ Which Bellman equation fits this form

Guide Question:

  1. In Policy Evaluation \(v=T^\pi v\), if the coordinates corresponding to state \(s\)Z are written in the form \(\mathbb{E}[f(v,\xi)]\), what are \(\xi\) and \(f\)?
  2. What is the order of \(\max\) and the expected value in the optimality equation \(v=Tv\)? Can an unbiased estimate of \((Tv)(s)\) be made with a single sample?
  3. What is the order in the Bellman equation for \(Q^*\)?
NoteSolution

If you set it to 1. \(\xi=s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,\pi(s))\), \(f(v,s')=r(s,\pi(s))+\gamma v(s')\) \[ v(s)=\mathbb{E}_{s'}\big[r(s,\pi(s))+\gamma v(s')\big]. \] Applying Robbins–Monro results in \(v_{k+1}(s)=(1-\alpha_k)v_k(s)+\alpha_k\big(r+\gamma v_k(s')\big)\). This is TD(0).

2. \((Tv)(s)=\max_a\mathbb{E}_{s'}[\cdots]\), i.e., \(\max\), is outside the expected value. A single sample gives only \(s'\) for a single action. If we take \(\max\) for the single-sample estimate of each action, it becomes \(\mathbb{E}[\max_a\hat q_a]\ge\max_a\mathbb{E}[\hat q_a]\) due to the Jensen inequality, which is biased upward. Therefore, it does not fit this form.

3. \[ Q^*(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim p(\cdot\mid s,a)}\Big[r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\Big]. \] This time, \(\max\) is inside the expected value. Therefore, if we set \(x=Q\), \(\xi=s'\), and \(f(Q,s')=r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\), it is exactly in the form \(x=\mathbb{E}[f(x,\xi)]\). If we apply Robbins–Monro, \[ Q_{k+1}(s,a)=(1-\alpha_k)Q_k(s,a)+\alpha_k\Big(r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_k(s',a')\Big). \] This is Q-learning.

TipSignificance

There are two reasons why the value-based method learns \(Q\) instead of \(v\): (i) The policy can be extracted without a model. (ii) The optimality equation takes the form where \(\max\) is contained within the expectation, allowing Robbins–Monro to be applied directly.

6 III. Step size

6.1 Algorithms and step directions by coordinate

Answer. If different step sizes are allowed for each coordinate, a typical SA is as follows.

\[ x_{k+1}(i)=(1-\alpha_k(i))\,x_k(i)+\alpha_k(i)\big[(Hx_k)(i)+w_k(i)\big]. \]

Assumption: For each \(i\), there are infinitely many \(\alpha_k(i)>0\)Z and \(k\). In other words, all coordinates are updated infinitely often.

Rearranging gives \(x_{k+1}(i)=x_k(i)+\alpha_k(i)\big[(Hx_k)(i)-x_k(i)+w_k(i)\big]\). Therefore, the step direction is \((Hx_k)(i)-x_k(i)+w_k(i)\), and the remaining problem is how to choose \(\alpha_k(i)\).

TipSignificance: Asynchronous update

In Q-learning, the coordinates \(i\) represent the pair \((s,a)\). In a single transition, only the visited coordinate \((s_k,a_k)\) is updated, while the remaining coordinate is \(\alpha_k(i)=0\). Therefore, the assumption that “it is updated infinitely often” becomes the search condition that all \((s,a)\) are visited infinitely often. Since whether a coordinate is visited depends on the trajectory, the step size is also a random variable. This is the reason why “with a probability of 1” is attached to the subsequent condition.

6.1.1 ✍ Constant step size: Variance remains

Let the coordinates \(i\) be fixed, and let the variance of \(w_k(i)\)Z be \(\sigma^2\), and let \(w_k\) be independent of \(x_k\).

Guide Question:

  1. When \(\alpha_k(i)\equiv\alpha\), what is the lower bound of \(\operatorname{Var}(x_{k+1}(i))\)?
  2. In the simplest case, \(Hx\equiv c\) (constant, reduction factor 0), where do the mean and variance of the error \(e_k:=x_k-c\) go?
NoteSolution

1. \(x_{k+1}(i)=\underbrace{(1-\alpha)x_k(i)+\alpha(Hx_k)(i)}_{A_k}+\alpha w_k(i)\), and since \(A_k\) is a function of \(x_k\), it is independent of \(w_k\). \[ \operatorname{Var}(x_{k+1}(i))=\operatorname{Var}(A_k)+\alpha^2\sigma^2\ \ge\ \alpha^2\sigma^2. \] At every step, new noise adds variance by \(\alpha^2\sigma^2\), so the variance cannot go to 0.

2. \(e_{k+1}=(1-\alpha)e_k+\alpha w_k\) is.

  • Average: \(\mathbb{E}e_k=(1-\alpha)^ke_0\to0\).
  • Variance: \(V_{k+1}=(1-\alpha)^2V_k+\alpha^2\sigma^2\), so the normal value is as follows. \[ V_\infty=\frac{\alpha^2\sigma^2}{1-(1-\alpha)^2}=\frac{\alpha\,\sigma^2}{2-\alpha}>0. \]

The average goes to \(x^*\), but \(x_k\) continues to move randomly within a radius \(O(\sqrt\alpha\,\sigma)\)Z around \(x^*\).

TipSignificance

With a constant step size, it reaches the vicinity of \(x^*\) at best and does not converge. For convergence to occur, it must be \(\alpha_k\to0\)** so that the influence of noise eventually diminishes. Conversely, as \(\alpha\) becomes smaller, the size of the vicinity \(\sqrt{\alpha/(2-\alpha)}\,\sigma\) decreases, but the average error decreases slowly to \((1-\alpha)^k\). This is the bias-variance trade-off of a constant step size. In non-stationary environments, a constant step size may actually be advantageous.

6.1.2 If you shrink too fast: cannot escape

First question to consider. If \(\sum_k\alpha_k(i)=A<\infty\) and the step direction is bounded at \(B\), how far can \(x_k(i)\) go from \(x_0(i)\)?

Answer. If you accumulate the update formula

\[ |x_k(i)-x_0(i)|\le\sum_{\tau=0}^{k-1}\alpha_\tau(i)\,\big|(Hx_\tau)(i)-x_\tau(i)+w_\tau(i)\big|\le A\,B. \]

That is, \(x_k\) cannot go beyond the fixed vicinity of radius \(AB\) centered on \(x_0\). If \(x^*\) is outside of it, it cannot be reached.

TipSignificance

Reduce the step size, but the cumulative movable amount \(\sum\alpha_k\) must be infinite. This ensures that no matter how far the initial point is, it can sufficiently traverse the domain of \(H\) and reach \(x^*\). Combined with Section 6.1.1, this becomes “Do not make it too big, nor too small too quickly.”

↑ Go Up: Section 6.1

6.2 Step size condition

Answer. Assume that the following holds with a probability of 1.

\[ \text{(S1)}\ \ \sum_{k=0}^\infty\alpha_k(i)=+\infty\quad\forall i, \qquad \text{(S2)}\ \ \sum_{k=0}^\infty\alpha_k(i)^2<+\infty\quad\forall i. \]

  • (S1) prevents it from shrinking too quickly → prevents the inescapable problem (Section 6.1.2).
  • (S2) eventually causes it to decrease sufficiently quickly → prevents the residual variance problem (Section 6.1.1).

A representative example is \(\alpha_k=1/(k+1)\).

6.2.1 The sum of ✍ \(1/(k+1)\) diverges, and the sum of squares converges.

If you set it to \(n=k+1\), it is the same as looking at \(\sum_{n\ge1}1/n\) and \(\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2\).

Guide Question:

  1. If the terms of \(\sum 1/n\) are grouped into intervals \((2^{m-1},2^m]\), what is the sum of each group greater than or equal to?
  2. Can \(1/n^2\) be pressed from above as a telescopic series term in \(n\ge2\)?
  3. When \(\alpha_k=1/(k+1)^p\), what is the range of \(p\) that simultaneously satisfies (S1) and (S2)?
NoteSolution

1. The interval \((2^{m-1},2^m]\) contains \(2^{m-1}\) terms, and each term is \(\ge1/2^m\). Therefore, the sum of the group is \(\ge1/2\). \[ \sum_{n=1}^{2^M}\frac1n\ \ge\ 1+\frac M2\ \xrightarrow{M\to\infty}\ \infty. \] When compared using integrals, it is \(\sum_{n=1}^N1/n\ge\int_1^{N+1}dx/x=\ln(N+1)\). It diverges, but at a rate of \(\ln N\), it diverges very slowly.

2. Since \(n^2>n(n-1)\), it is \(\dfrac1{n^2}<\dfrac1{n-1}-\dfrac1n\). When combined, the middle terms cancel out. \[ \sum_{n=1}^N\frac1{n^2}<1+\Big(1-\frac1N\Big)<2. \] The partial sum of the positive term series converges because it is monotonically increasing and bounded. The limit value \(\pi^2/6\) (Basel problem) is not required for the SA condition, and only the fact that it is finite is required.

3. By integral comparison, it is \(\sum n^{-p}<\infty\iff p>1\).

  • To satisfy (S1), \(p\le1\).
  • To satisfy (S2), \(2p>1\).

Therefore, it is \(p\in(1/2,\,1]\). \(1/(k+1)\) is the right end of this interval, that is, the fastest decreasing case among the allowed ones.

TipSignificance

The key point of the grouping argument in problem 1 is that when the size of the term decreases by a factor of \(1/2\), the number of terms increases by exactly double. Therefore, each group contributes a fixed amount. When squared, the size of the term decreases by a factor of \(1/4\) and the number doubles, so the sum of the groups decreases in a geometric sequence. This is why \(p=1\) is the boundary.

↑ Go Up: Section 6.2

6.2.2 If ✍ \(\alpha_k=1/(k+1)\), then SA is the sample mean.

Let’s consider the simplest case, \(f(x,\xi)=\xi\), that is, \(x=\mathbb{E}[\xi]\).

Guide Question:

  1. If you solve \(x_{k+1}=\frac{k}{k+1}x_k+\frac1{k+1}\hat\xi_k\), what is \(x_k\)? Where does \(x_0\) go?
  2. If \(\hat\xi_j\) is iid and the variance is \(\sigma^2\), what is \(\operatorname{Var}(x_k)\)? If we write this as \(\alpha\)?
NoteSolution

1. Multiplying both sides by \(k+1\) gives \((k+1)x_{k+1}=kx_k+\hat\xi_k\). Therefore, \(kx_k=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\hat\xi_j\), and (in \(k=0\), the coefficient of \(x_0\) becomes 0) \[ x_k=\frac1k\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\hat\xi_j\qquad(k\ge1). \] Since \(\alpha_0=1\) is in the first update, the initial value is immediately erased. \(x_k\) is the sample mean.

2. \(\operatorname{Var}(x_k)=\sigma^2/k=\sigma^2\alpha_{k-1}\to0\). By the law of strong large numbers \(x_k\to\mathbb{E}\xi\) (probability 1).

TipSignificance

SA is a generalization of the method for iteratively (online) calculating the sample mean. \(1/(k+1)\) is the choice that gives equal weight to all samples. Decreasing more slowly (\(p<1\)) gives greater weight to recent samples. This results in slightly larger variance in exchange for quickly forgetting the initial values and initial estimation errors. In the general case where \(H\) depends on \(x\), \(Hx_k\) itself is inaccurate in the initial iterations, so \(p<1\) is often actually better.

↑ Go Up: Section 6.2

6.2.3 If you write the roles of the two conditions accurately

First question to consider. In the case of the scalar \(Hx\equiv c\), how is the error \(e_k=x_k-c\) divided into the initial value term and the noise term?

Answer. If you solve \(e_{k+1}=(1-\alpha_k)e_k+\alpha_kw_k\)

\[ e_k=\underbrace{e_0\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-\alpha_j)}_{\text{초기값의 흔적}} +\underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\alpha_jw_j\prod_{l=j+1}^{k-1}(1-\alpha_l)}_{\text{누적 잡음}}. \]

  • (S1) forgets initial value: \(\prod(1-\alpha_j)\to0\iff\sum\alpha_j=\infty\).
  • (S2) tames the cumulative noise to probability 1: the cumulative sum of the noise \(M_k=\sum_{j<k}\alpha_jw_j\) converges to probability 1.
NoteBasis

Let’s call it (S1) \(\alpha_j\in[0,1)\).

  • Since \(1-\alpha\le e^{-\alpha}\), it is \(\prod_{j<k}(1-\alpha_j)\le\exp\big(-\sum_{j<k}\alpha_j\big)\). Therefore, if it is \(\sum\alpha=\infty\), the product goes to 0.
  • Conversely, from \(\alpha\le1/2\), it becomes \(\ln(1-\alpha)\ge-\alpha-\alpha^2\). Therefore, if it is \(\sum\alpha<\infty\), the product has a positive lower bound.

If (S2) \(\mathbb{E}[w_j\mid\mathcal F_j]=0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[w_j^2\mid\mathcal F_j]\le\sigma^2\), then \(M_k\) is a martingale. Since the increments at different times are orthogonal, \[ \mathbb{E}[M_k^2]=\sum_{j<k}\alpha_j^2\,\mathbb{E}[w_j^2]\le\sigma^2\sum_j\alpha_j^2<\infty. \] , that is, \(M_k\) is a \(L^2\)-bounded martingale, and by the martingale convergence theorem, it converges to a probability of 1. Therefore, it is tail \(\sum_{j\ge J}\alpha_jw_j\to0\). The amount of noise at late time points shaking the trajectory eventually becomes zero.

Mean squared error also becomes 0 through the same decomposition. The second moment of the noise term is \(\sigma^2\sum_j\alpha_j^2\prod_{l>j}(1-\alpha_l)^2\). Let’s split this sum in two at time point \(J\). The part after \(J\) can be reduced to \(\sigma^2\sum_{j\ge J}\alpha_j^2\) (S2). The part before \(J\) is multiplied by \(\prod_{l\ge J}(1-\alpha_l)^2\to0\) and cancels out (S1).

TipSignificance

Strictly speaking, if we only want mean square convergence, \(\alpha_k\to0\) and (S1) are sufficient. Where (S2) is essentially used is probability 1 convergence. The accumulated noise must converge to a single martingale for each locus to eventually follow a deterministic iteration. Therefore, the convergence theorem is described as “with probability 1” → Section 8.1

7 IV. Noise Conditions

7.1 What conditions are required for noise?

Answer. For each \(i\) and \(k\), assume the following.

\[ \text{(N1)}\ \ \mathbb{E}[w_k(i)\mid\mathcal F_k]=0, \qquad \text{(N2)}\ \ \mathbb{E}[w_k(i)^2\mid\mathcal F_k]\le C_0+C_1\|x_k\|^2. \]

  • (N1) is the condition that the conditional mean is 0. Noise does not continuously push in a specific direction.
  • (N2) is the condition that the conditional variance is bounded. However, it is not a constant, but is allowed to increase proportionally to \(\|x_k\|^2\).
TipSignificance
  • The reason for using conditional instead of unconditional \(\mathbb{E}[w_k]=0\) is that the RL data is not iid. Samples on the trajectory depend on the past. However, it is sufficient if the new sample is unbiased given the past.
  • The reason (N2) allows \(\|x_k\|^2\) is that, as seen in Section 7.1.2, the sample noise of the Bellman operator is proportional to the magnitude of the current estimate.

↓ Down: Section 7.1.1 · Section 7.1.2 ↑ Up: Section 3.1

7.1.1 What goes into history \(\mathcal F_k\)

Answer.

\[ \mathcal F_k:=\{x_0,\dots,x_k,\ w_0,\dots,w_{k-1},\ \alpha_0,\dots,\alpha_k\}. \]

  • \(x_k\) enters: The current estimate is already known.
  • Only up to \(w_{k-1}\): This noise \(w_k\) is unknown yet.
  • step size goes up to \(\alpha_k\): step size can be a random variable that depends on the trajectory, such as the number of visits, but is determined before seeing \(w_k\).
NoteNotation caution

Some literature uses \(\gamma_k\) for the step size, so \(\mathcal F_k\)Z and \(\gamma_0,\dots,\gamma_k\) appear. This is the step size, not the discount rate.

TipSignificance

Since \(\alpha_k\) enters \(\mathcal F_k\), it becomes \(\mathbb{E}[\alpha_kw_k\mid\mathcal F_k]=\alpha_k\mathbb{E}[w_k\mid\mathcal F_k]=0\). Thanks to this property, the accumulated noise \(\sum\alpha_jw_j\) of Section 6.2.3 becomes a martingale. If the step size is determined based on this noise, this property is broken.

7.1.2 ✍ Do the noises in Robbins–Monroe and Q-learning satisfy the conditions?

Guide Question:

  1. In Robbins–Monro, if \(|f(x,\xi)|\le a+b\|x\|\) (\(a,b\) constant), can \(C_0,C_1\) of (N2) be captured?
  2. Check (N1) and (N2) for Q-learning \(f(Q,s')=r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\). Let the reward be \(|r|\le R_{\max}\) and be bounded.
NoteSolution

1. (N1) was shown in Section 5.1.1. Since variance can be compressed by the second moment, \[ \mathbb{E}[w_k^2\mid\mathcal F_k]=\operatorname{Var}\big(f(x_k,\xi)\big)\le\mathbb{E}\big[f(x_k,\xi)^2\big]\le(a+b\|x_k\|)^2\le2a^2+2b^2\|x_k\|^2. \] Therefore, you can set it to \(C_0=2a^2\) and \(C_1=2b^2\).

2.

  • (N1): \(s'\) originates from \(p(\cdot\mid s_k,a_k)\) when the entire past is given (Markov property). Therefore, even if the trajectory is not iid, it is \(\mathbb{E}[w_k\mid\mathcal F_k]=0\).
  • (N2): \(|f(Q,s')|\le R_{\max}+\gamma\|Q\|_\infty\le R_{\max}+\gamma\|Q\|_2\). If you input \(a=R_{\max}\) and \(b=\gamma\) into the result of 1, it is \(C_0=2R_{\max}^2\) and \(C_1=2\gamma^2\).
TipSignificance

The noise in Q-learning comes from ZZZPH0015186ZZZ \(\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\), and its magnitude is proportional to the magnitude of the current \(Q\). This case cannot be handled with a constant variance bound. Therefore, (N2) is of the form \(C_0+C_1\|x_k\|^2\).

8 V. Convergence Theorem

8.1 Convergence under contraction

Answer.

ImportantSummary 1 (Abbreviation)

If the step size conditions (S1)–(S2) and noise conditions (N1)–(N2) hold, and \(H\) is a reduction mapping for the (weighted) maximum norm, then \(x_k\) converges to the fixed point \(x^*\) of \(H\) with probability 1.

The proof is in Bertsekas & Tsitsiklis, Neuro-Dynamic Programming (1996), Section 4.3.6. Only the result is written here.

TipSignificance
  • Bellman Operator. The Bellman operators for \(T\), \(T^\pi\), and \(Q\) are all \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) to \(\gamma\)-reductions. Therefore, the convergence of tabular TD(0) and Q-learning is derived directly from this theorem. → Section 8.2.2
  • The assumptions are a direct transfer of the intuition of Section 6.1.1–Section 6.2.3. The noise must have a conditional mean of 0 and a variance that is appropriately bounded, and the step size must tame the noise by endlessly stacking the average direction.
  • However, in actual applications, especially when using functional approximations, this assumption often does not hold. It is sufficient to understand it as a criterion for distinguishing between regions with and without theoretical guarantees.

↑ Go Up: Section 3.1

8.2 Convergence under monotony

Answer. Even if \(H\) is not a contraction, convergence can be shown under the following conditions.

ImportantTheorem 2 (Monotonicity)

Let’s assume that \(H\) satisfies the following.

  1. Monotonic: \(x\le x'\Rightarrow Hx\le Hx'\) (Element-wise inequality).
  2. Unique Fixed Point: \(Hx^*=x^*\) and \(x^*\) exist uniquely.
  3. Inequality for constant shift: For any \(\eta>0\) \[ Hx-\eta\mathbf 1\ \le\ H(x-\eta\mathbf 1)\ \le\ H(x+\eta\mathbf 1)\ \le\ Hx+\eta\mathbf 1. \]

If (S1)–(S2), (N1)–(N2) holds and the sequence \(x_k\) generated by SA is bounded with probability 1, then \(x_k\) converges to \(x^*\) with probability 1.

TipSignificance

requires monotonality and boundedness instead of abbreviation. The cost is that the boundedness of \(x_k\) must be demonstrated separately. While boundedness follows with abbreviation, it is assumed here.

↓ Down: Section 8.2.1 · Section 8.2.2 ↑ Up: Section 3.1

8.2.1 ✍ What does assumption (3) mean?

Guide Question:

  1. Where does the middle inequality \(H(x-\eta\mathbf 1)\le H(x+\eta\mathbf 1)\) of assumption (3) come from? Actually, the new content is the two outer inequalities, but how would you put it?
  2. Show that combining assumptions (1) and (3) gives \(\|Hx-Hy\|_\infty\le\|x-y\|_\infty\)
NoteSolution

1. The middle inequality is derived directly from monotonicity (1). The two outer inequalities are as follows. \[ H(x+\eta\mathbf 1)\le Hx+\eta\mathbf 1,\qquad H(x-\eta\mathbf 1)\ge Hx-\eta\mathbf 1. \] If all components of the input are raised (or lowered) by a constant \(\eta\), the output increases (or decreases) by at most \(\eta\) for each component. In other words, there is no amplification in the \(\mathbf 1\) direction.

2. If set to \(\eta:=\|x-y\|_\infty\), it becomes \(y-\eta\mathbf 1\le x\le y+\eta\mathbf 1\). By monotonicity and (3) \[ Hy-\eta\mathbf 1\le H(y-\eta\mathbf 1)\le Hx\le H(y+\eta\mathbf 1)\le Hy+\eta\mathbf 1. \] Therefore, it is \(\|Hx-Hy\|_\infty\le\eta=\|x-y\|_\infty\).

TipSignificance

When combining monotonality and (3), \(H\) is a non-expansive mapping from \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\). It is non-expansive but may not be contracted. This implies that the contraction coefficient is allowed up to 1. In this case, the uniqueness of the fixed point (Assumption 2) and the boundedness of the trajectory do not arise automatically, so they are assumed separately.

8.2.2 ✍ Does the Bellman operator satisfy the assumptions of the two theorems?

Guide Question:

  1. Is \(T\) a forma? What if you write \(T(v+\eta\mathbf 1)\) as \(Tv\)?
  2. Which theorem can be applied when \(\gamma<1\) and when \(\gamma=1\), respectively?
NoteSolution

1.

  • Modification: If \(v\le v'\), then for each \(a\)Z, it is \(r+\gamma\sum_{s'}p\,v\le r+\gamma\sum_{s'}p\,v'\). Since \(\max\) is forged, it is \(Tv\le Tv'\).
  • Constant Shift: Since \(\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)=1\), it is \(T(v+\eta\mathbf 1)=Tv+\gamma\eta\mathbf 1\). Therefore \[ Tv-\eta\mathbf 1\le Tv-\gamma\eta\mathbf 1=T(v-\eta\mathbf 1)\le T(v+\eta\mathbf 1)=Tv+\gamma\eta\mathbf 1\le Tv+\eta\mathbf 1. \] If \(\gamma\le1\), then assumption (3) holds. The Bellman operator for \(T^\pi\) and \(Q\) is verified in the same way.

2.

  • If \(\gamma<1\), then \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) is a contraction of \(\gamma\), so write Theorem 1.
  • If \(\gamma=1\) (e.g., a stochastic shortest path problem with a termination state), \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\)-reduction is not guaranteed. In this case, monotonicity and (3) still hold. Therefore, if we separately show that the fixed point is unique and the iteration is bounded, we can write Theorem 2.
TipSignificance

The two theorems rely on different properties of the Bellman operator. Theorem 1 relies on discounting (contraction), and Theorem 2 relies on preservation of the order of expectation and \(\max\) (monotonicity). Theorem 2 is used to extend the convergence of Q-learning to problems without discounting.
