Summary of Core Machine Learning Concepts

Machine Learning Basics · Generalization · Learning Algorithms · Class Imbalance

Published

July 24, 2026

1 Components of Machine Learning

Machine learning can be summarized as the process of finding a function \(f: X \to y\) from data. This process is broken down into four elements: a model that defines a set of candidate functions, a loss that measures the quality of the candidates, training that finds candidates that minimize the loss, and evaluation and validation that check whether the found function works well on new data.

1.1 Model: Parametric vs. Non-parametric

A parametric model has a fixed number of parameters regardless of dataset size. Linear regression, \(y = X\beta + \epsilon\), and logistic regression are typical examples. Its strong assumptions allow stable estimates even with little data; however, incorrect assumptions lead to persistent bias, regardless of how much data is available.

Non-parametric models have an increasing number of valid parameters depending on the data. KNN, decision trees, and random forests belong to this category. While they are flexible and capable of capturing complex relationships, they require a large amount of data and carry a high risk of overfitting.

1.2 Loss

The loss function serves to formulate the learning objective. In regression, it is the MSE based on the sum of squared residuals.

\[ L(\beta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left(y_i - f(x_i;\beta)\right)^2 \]

, in classification cross-entropy (negative log-likelihood)

\[ L(\beta) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{k} \mathbb{1}[y_i = k]\, \log p_k(x_i;\beta) \]

is primarily used. From a statistical perspective, minimizing MSE is equivalent to Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) under the Gaussian error assumption, and minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to MLE for categorical distributions. The roles are distinguished in that loss is the subject of learning (optimization), while the metrics below are tools for humans to interpret performance.

1.3 Learning: Supervised Learning and Unsupervised Learning

Supervised learning uses input-label pairs \((x, y)\). When \(y\) is categorical, the task is classification; when it is continuous, the task is regression. Unsupervised learning finds structure in unlabeled data, such as clusters, density, and low-dimensional representations.

1.4 Metrics

1.4.1 Classification Metrics

In binary classification, the following is defined from the four cells (TP, FP, FN, TN) of the confusion matrix.

\[ \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP}, \qquad \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}, \qquad F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}} \]

Precision is the “proportion of actual positives among predicted positives,” and recall is the “proportion of actual positives caught.” The two have a trade-off relationship with a threshold between them, and since \(F_1\) is the harmonic mean of the two values, the performance drops significantly if either one collapses. In multi-class systems, a distinction is made between the macro mean (treating all classes equally), which simply averages class-specific metrics, and the weighted/micro mean, which is weighted by the sample size.

1.4.2 Regression Metrics

MSE is sensitive to outliers by squared penalty for large errors, while MAE is robust as it responds linearly to the magnitude of the error. \(R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}\) is interpreted as “how much variance is explained compared to a model that predicts only by the mean” and is scale-independent.

1.4.3 Clustering Metrics

Since there is no correct answer, silhouette coefficients that reflect both within-cluster cohesion and between-cluster separation are used.

1.5 Evaluation and Validation

The core principle is that test data is used only once to verify generalization performance after all selections are complete. All selections, including models, hyperparameters, variables, and thresholds, are performed using validation performance generated within the training data. Repeated use of test data in the selection process can lead to overfitting, causing performance estimates to become optimistically biased.

The method of splitting data must reflect the structure of the data. Stratified ensures that class proportions are maintained after splitting, grouped prevents information leakage caused by samples from the same individual, patient, or device entering training and validation simultaneously, and time series always learns from the past and validates against the future to prevent inversions where the past is predicted by future data.

Regarding validation methods, holdout is fast as it divides only once, but the estimation is unstable depending on the luck of the split, while k-fold cross-validation (k-fold CV) utilizes all samples for validation by dividing the data into k equal parts and using each piece for validation once, thereby estimating the mean and variance of the validation performance together. The computational cost becomes k times higher.

Model selection proceeds in the order of comparing candidate models on the same validation framework, retraining the selected model on the entire training data, and finally reporting generalization performance on the evaluation data.

2 Generalization

2.1 Bias-Variance Decomposition

The expected prediction error for the new input \(x_0\) is decomposed as follows.

\[ \mathbb{E}\left[(y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\right)^2}_{\text{편향}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat f(x_0) - \mathbb{E}[\hat f(x_0)])^2\right]}_{\text{분산}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{줄일 수 없는 오차}} \]

Bias is a systematic error that occurs because the model is too simple to represent the true function, and variance is the degree to which the estimated model fluctuates whenever the training data changes. As model complexity increases, bias decreases and variance increases; therefore, the complexity that minimizes the total error lies at the midpoint where the sum of the two is minimized. A typical example is that in KNN, reducing the neighbor count \(k\) increases complexity (low bias, high variance), while increasing it decreases it (high bias, low variance).

2.2 Diagnosis of Overfitting and Underfitting

The diagnostic tool is the comparison of training error and validation error. If the training error is very small while the validation error is large, it indicates overfitting (variance problem); if the two errors are similar but both are large, it indicates underfitting (bias problem). For overfitting, complexity reduction, regularization, data expansion, and early stopping are prescribed, while for underfitting, complexity expansion and feature addition are prescribed. If the training/validation curve is plotted with complexity as the axis, the validation error forms a U-shape, and the minimum point is the optimal complexity.

2.3 Hyperparameter Optimization

Hyperparameters are values (tree depth, regularization strength, number of neighbors, etc.) that are not determined by learning but are specified externally by humans. Grid Search creates a grid of candidate values, evaluates all combinations through cross-validation, and selects the best one. Although the implementation is simple and reproducible, the number of combinations increases exponentially with the number of parameters; therefore, when there are many candidates, random search or Bayesian optimization become alternatives.

2.4 Feature Subset Selection

The purpose of reducing variables is to suppress overfitting (reduce variance), improve interpretability, and reduce computational costs. The characteristics of the three approaches are distinctly different.

The filter method filters variables by ranking them based on the statistical association (correlation coefficient, F-statistic, etc.) between each variable and the target variable, regardless of the model. While it is very fast and independent of the model, it misses interaction or combination effects between variables because it examines them one by one.

The wrapper method explores subsets based on the validation performance of the actual model. Since examining all subsets (\(2^p\)) is nearly impossible, it employs greedy search methods such as forward selection, which adds variables with the greatest performance improvement one by one from empty sets, and backward elimination, which removes variables with the smallest contribution one by one from the whole. Although it reflects combination effects, it is costly because the model must be trained iteratively, and it does not guarantee global optimality due to being a greedy search.

Embedded methods are cases where variable selection is embedded in the training process itself. Lasso adds an L1 penalty to the loss

\[ \min_\beta \; \frac{1}{n}\lVert y - X\beta \rVert^2 + \lambda \sum_j |\beta_j| \]

Reduces some coefficients to exactly zero. This stems from the geometric property where the solution lies on the axis (meaning the coefficients become zero) because the vertices of the L1 constraint region lie on the axis. Although it is efficient as selection and estimation are performed simultaneously in a single learning step, the selection result is dependent on the corresponding model.

3 Learning algorithm

3.1 Gradient Descent

Since the gradient of loss \(L(\beta)\) is the direction in which the loss increases most rapidly locally, the parameter is shifted in the opposite direction.

\[ \beta^{(t+1)} = \beta^{(t)} - \eta \, \nabla_\beta L(\beta^{(t)}) \]

The MSE loss in linear regression is \(\nabla_\beta L = -\frac{2}{n} X^\top (y - X\beta)\).

The learning rate \(\eta\) is the most important setting that determines convergence. If it is too small, convergence is slow, and if it is too large, it skips minimum points and oscillates or diverges. From the perspective of second-order approximation, the stability limit of the learning rate is inversely proportional to the curvature of the loss surface (the maximum eigenvalue of the Hessian).

The reason why variable scaling uniformity is important can be understood from the same perspective. If the scale difference between variables is large, the contours of the loss surface become elongated, distorted ellipses (due to the large condition number), and convergence in the direction of small curvature becomes extremely slow with a small learning rate tailored to the direction of large curvature. Standardization makes the surface nearly isotropic, allowing all directions to converge at a similar speed with a single learning rate.

Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) approximates gradients using random mini-batches instead of the entire dataset. Since it is an unbiased estimator of batch gradients, it moves in the same direction on average, but significantly reduces the computational load per iteration and helps to move away from local minima or saddle points where the gradient noise is shallow.

Early stopping observes the validation loss, which is set aside from the training data, during each iteration; if there is no improvement for a certain number of iterations (patience), it stops and adopts the parameters at the point where the validation loss was minimum. This can be interpreted as implicit regularization that views the number of iterations as the axis of complexity.

3.2 EM Algorithm (Expectation-Maximization)

If there are unobserved latent variables \(z\) (e.g., which component of the mixture distribution each sample came from), the sum falls into the log-likelihood \(\log p(x;\theta) = \log \sum_z p(x, z;\theta)\), making direct maximization difficult. EM bypasses this through two iterations.

In the E-step, the posterior probability (responsibility value) of the latent variable is calculated using the current parameters. In a Gaussian mixture, the responsibility value for sample \(x_j\) belonging to component \(i\) is

\[ T_{ji} = \frac{\tau_i \,\mathcal{N}(x_j; \mu_i, \sigma_i^2)}{\sum_k \tau_k \,\mathcal{N}(x_j; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)} \]

In the M-step, the responsibility value is used as a weight, like the “partial ownership” of membership, to update the parameters.

\[ \tau_i = \frac{\sum_j T_{ji}}{n}, \qquad \mu_i = \frac{\sum_j T_{ji} x_j}{\sum_j T_{ji}}, \qquad \sigma_i^2 = \frac{\sum_j T_{ji} (x_j - \mu_i)^2}{\sum_j T_{ji}} \]

Each iteration is equivalent to maximizing the lower bound of the log-likelihood (obtained via Jensen’s inequality), which causes the likelihood to increase monotonically and guarantees convergence. However, since the endpoint may be a local optimal, changing the initial values and trying multiple times is a practical approach.

3.3 MCMC(Markov Chain Monte Carlo)

Monte Carlo Estimation approximates the expected value \(\mathbb{E}_p[h(X)]\) by the sample mean \(\frac{1}{N}\sum h(x_i)\) drawn from \(p\). The problem arises when it is difficult to draw a direct sample from \(p\)—particularly when the normalization constant is unknown, such as in the posterior distribution of Bayesian inference.

A Markov chain is a stochastic process in which the next state depends only on the current state, and if conditions are met, it converges to a unique stationary distribution regardless of the initial state. The idea of MCMC is to “design the transition rules of the chain such that the stationary distribution becomes the target distribution \(p\)”.

The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm implements this as follows: From the current state \(x\), candidates are selected from the proposal distribution \(q(x' \mid x)\), and the adoption probability

\[ \alpha = \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{p(x')\, q(x \mid x')}{p(x)\, q(x' \mid x)}\right) \]

We either adopt or remain in the current state. The key point is that the normalization constant is canceled out because \(\alpha\) uses only the proportion of \(p\). In other words, we only need to be able to compute \(\tilde p\) of \(p(x) \propto \tilde p(x)\). This rule is designed to satisfy the detailed balance \(p(x)T(x\to x') = p(x')T(x'\to x)\), which guarantees that \(p\) is a normal distribution.

In practice, the initial sample (burn-in) before the chain reaches a normal distribution is discarded, the probability of acceptance is calculated on a logarithmic scale for numerical stability, and the mixing of the chain is verified using a trace plot. In Bayesian inference, setting it to \(\tilde p(\theta) = p(\text{data}\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\) allows obtaining a sample of the posterior distribution to calculate the posterior mean, credit interval, etc.

4 Resolve class imbalance

4.1 Why is it a problem?

If “all negative” is predicted in data where positives make up 0.2% of the total, the accuracy is 99.8%, but the model has done nothing. In imbalanced situations, accuracy distorts performance, so metrics from the minority class perspective (precision, recall, \(F_1\), PR curve) must be used, and the problem of loss minimization being dominated by the majority class is corrected using the methods below. One important principle: Resampling should be applied only to training data, and validation and evaluation data must maintain their original distribution to estimate performance in actual operating environments.

4.2 Over sampling

Increase the sample size of the minority class to balance the ratio.

Random Over Sampling randomly duplicates the minority class. It is simple and does not lose information, but because the same samples are repeated, it is prone to overfitting to the minority class region.

SMOTE synthesizes instead of duplicating. Linearly interpolates between the prime class sample \(x\) and its k-nearest prime class neighbor \(x_{nn}\)

\[ x_{new} = x + u \cdot (x_{nn} - x), \qquad u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1) \]

Creates a new sample. This creates diversity rather than replication, but noisy samples may be generated when the distance between neighbors is actually in the majority class region.

ADASYN extends SMOTE by assigning more synthetic samples to minority samples that are surrounded by majority classes (making learning difficult). While this effectively reinforces learning intensively near the decision boundary, it is consequently more sensitive to noise.

Oversampling has the advantage of not losing information, but the disadvantage is that as the data grows, the training cost increases and there is a risk of overfitting and noise amplification.

4.3 Under sampling

Reduce the majority class sample to balance the ratio.

Random Under Sampling randomly discards the majority of classes. It is fast and reduces training costs, but it loses potentially useful information.

Tomek’s Link finds pairs of neighbors that belong to different classes. Since these pairs are ambiguous (or noisy) samples spanning class boundaries, removing the majority class side from the pair clarifies the decision boundary. Because it cannot significantly alter ratios, it is often used in combination with other methods.

Undersampling has the advantage of faster learning and clearer boundaries, but the disadvantage that information loss is inevitable. It is practical when the data is sufficiently large.

4.4 Adjust classification threshold

Without modifying the data, the threshold of the positive diagnosis criterion \(P(y=1\mid x) \ge 0.5\) is adjusted. Since lowering the threshold increases recall but decreases precision, the threshold at which \(F_1\) (or the cost function) is optimal in the validation data is searched for and used. Models with well-corrected probability estimations are more effective, and the cost structure can be directly reflected in problems where misclassification costs are asymmetric (fraud detection, disease diagnosis).

4.5 Differential data weighting

In loss calculation, greater weight is given to misclassification of minority class samples.

\[ L = \sum_i w_{y_i} \, \ell(y_i, \hat y_i), \qquad w_k \propto \frac{n}{K \cdot n_k} \]

class_weight='balanced' uses this method to assign weights proportional to the inverse of the class frequency. Since it only changes the loss landscape without modifying the data, it avoids the side effects of resampling (replication overfitting, information loss) and is simple to implement. Conceptually, it is equivalent to oversampling in terms of expected loss, but it is efficient because it does not actually increase the sample size.

4.6 Perspective on Method Selection

No single method is always superior. Candidates are generated by considering data size (oversampling tends to be advantageous for small data, while undersampling is more favorable for large data), the quality of the model’s probability correction (threshold adjustment is simpler if it is good), and implementation and computational costs (weighting is the cheapest), and the final selection is always based on the indicators of the validation data that maintain the original distribution.
