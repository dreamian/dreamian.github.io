The Geometry of Linear Equations

Row Picture, Column Picture, and the Singular Case

Published

October 26, 2026

1 Overview

A linear system \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) can be read in two complementary ways.

  • Row picture: each equation is a line (in 2D), a plane (in 3D), or a hyperplane (in \(n\)D). The solution is the point where all of them intersect.
  • Column picture: the whole system is a single vector equation. The solution is the set of weights that combine the columns of \(A\) into \(\mathbf{b}\).

Both pictures describe the same solution. They also fail at the same time: when the system is singular, the row picture loses its unique intersection point and the column picture loses the ability to reach every \(\mathbf{b}\). Understanding exactly how they fail is the best way to understand why the good case works.

2 Two Equations, Two Unknowns

Consider

\[ \begin{aligned} 2x - y &= 1 \\ x + y &= 5. \end{aligned} \]

2.1 Row Picture

Each equation is a straight line in the \(xy\)-plane. The line \(2x - y = 1\) passes through \((\tfrac12, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\); the line \(x + y = 5\) has slope \(-1\). A point satisfies both equations if and only if it lies on both lines, so the solution is their intersection, \((x, y) = (2, 3)\).

2.2 Column Picture

The same two equations are the two components of one vector equation:

\[ x \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Now the unknowns are weights: how much of column 1 and how much of column 2 are needed to produce \(\mathbf{b}\). With \(x = 2\) and \(y = 3\),

\[ 2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}. \;✓ \]

Geometrically, \(2(\text{column } 1)\) and \(3(\text{column } 2)\) are two sides of a parallelogram, and \(\mathbf{b}\) is its diagonal. This is the parallelogram law of vector addition, the same rule used for centuries to combine forces in mechanics.

3 Three Equations, Three Unknowns

\[ \begin{aligned} 2u + v + w &= 5 \\ 4u - 6v \phantom{{}+w} &= -2 \\ -2u + 7v + 2w &= 9 \end{aligned} \tag{1} \]

3.1 Row Picture: Intersecting Planes

Each equation describes a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Plane 1. \(2u + v + w = 5\) meets the axes at \((\tfrac52, 0, 0)\), \((0, 5, 0)\) and \((0, 0, 5)\). A plane is fixed by any three of its points that are not collinear.

NoteThe right-hand side only shifts the plane

The coefficients \((2, 1, 1)\) form the normal vector and fix the orientation of the plane. Changing the right side moves the plane parallel to itself: \(2u + v + w = 10\) is the parallel plane twice as far from the origin, and \(2u + v + w = 0\) passes through the origin.

Plane 2. \(4u - 6v = -2\) has no \(w\) term. That does not make it a line: \(w\) is simply free, so the plane is vertical (parallel to the \(w\)-axis). Even \(u = 0\) is a plane in 3D.

Intersection. Planes 1 and 2 meet in a line. Plane 3 cuts that line at a single point, the solution

\[ (u, v, w) = (1, 1, 2). \]

Check: \(2 + 1 + 2 = 5\), \(\;4 - 6 = -2\), \(\;-2 + 7 + 4 = 9\). ✓

3.2 Column Picture: A Linear Combination

The same system as one vector equation:

\[ u \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + v \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} + w \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -2 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{b}. \tag{2} \]

A vector in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) can be viewed as a column of three numbers, as a point with those coordinates, or as an arrow from the origin to that point. Whichever view is used, only two operations matter:

  • Addition, component by component: \((5,0,0) + (0,-2,0) + (0,0,9) = (5,-2,9)\).
  • Scalar multiplication: \(2\,(1,0,2) = (2,0,4)\), and \(-2\,(1,0,2)\) points the opposite way.

Combining both operations gives a linear combination, the central idea of the subject. The weights found in the row picture produce \(\mathbf{b}\):

\[ 1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -2 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The numbers \((1, 1, 2)\) are simultaneously the intersection point in the row picture and the weights in the column picture.

4 Beyond Three Dimensions

With \(n\) equations in \(n\) unknowns:

Row picture Column picture
Objects \(n\) hyperplanes in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) \(n\) column vectors and \(\mathbf{b}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)
Question Where do all hyperplanes meet? Which weights combine the columns into \(\mathbf{b}\)?

A single equation \(a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n = b\) (with not all \(a_i = 0\)) defines a hyperplane, a flat set of dimension \(n - 1\). In the good case, each additional equation cuts the dimension of the intersection by exactly one:

\[ n - 1 \;\to\; n - 2 \;\to\; \cdots \;\to\; 0. \]

After all \(n\) equations the intersection has dimension \(0\): a single point, the solution.

TipPrecise version

“Each equation lowers the dimension by one” holds exactly when the new equation’s coefficient row is not a combination of the previous rows. In general, if the system is consistent, the solution set has dimension \(n - r\), where \(r\) is the number of independent rows (the rank). The unique-solution case is \(r = n\).

5 The Singular Case

5.1 Row Picture: Four Ways to Fail

In 2D, the only way for two lines to fail is to be parallel. In 3D there are more possibilities, and planes can fail without any two of them being parallel.

(a) Two parallel planes. \(2u + v + w = 5\) and \(4u + 2v + 2w = 11\) have proportional normals \((2,1,1)\) and \((4,2,2)\), but dividing the second by 2 gives \(2u + v + w = 5.5\). Two distinct parallel planes: no solution.

(b) Three planes forming a “triangle.”

\[ \begin{aligned} u + v + w &= 2 \\ 2u \phantom{{}+v} + 3w &= 5 \\ 3u + v + 4w &= 6 \end{aligned} \tag{3} \]

No two planes are parallel, so every pair meets in a line. But the left side of equation 3 is the sum of the first two left sides, while the right sides give \(2 + 5 = 7 \neq 6\). Thus

\[ (\text{eq. } 1) + (\text{eq. } 2) - (\text{eq. } 3): \quad 0 = 1, \]

a contradiction. Seen end-on, the three lines of intersection are parallel and the planes form a triangular prism: no common point.

(c) A whole line of solutions. Change the last right side from \(6\) to \(7\). The same combination now gives \(0 = 0\), so equation 3 adds no new information. The third plane moves parallel to itself until it contains the line where planes 1 and 2 meet. The system is still singular, but now it has too many solutions rather than too few.

(d) Three parallel planes. If all three normals are proportional, e.g.

\[ u + v + w = b_1, \qquad 2u + 2v + 2w = b_2, \qquad 3u + 3v + 3w = b_3, \]

then for most \(\mathbf{b}\) the three planes are distinct and parallel: no solution. For special right sides with \(b_1 = b_2/2 = b_3/3\) they coincide, and the whole plane is the solution set. The right side \(\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}\) is always special: every equation is satisfied by \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\), so every plane passes through the origin, and parallel planes through a common point must be the same plane.

NoteSummary of the row picture

A singular system has either no solution (most right sides) or infinitely many (special right sides). It never has exactly one.

5.2 Column Picture: Columns Trapped in a Plane

Write system (3) in column form:

\[ u \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + v \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + w \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{b}. \tag{4} \]

Call the columns \(\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \mathbf{a}_3\). They lie in a common plane through the origin, so every linear combination of them also lies in that plane.

  • If \(\mathbf{b}\) is outside the plane, no combination reaches it: no solution. This is the typical situation.
  • If \(\mathbf{b}\) is inside the plane, it can be reached in infinitely many ways.

Why are the columns coplanar? Find a nontrivial combination that gives zero:

\[ 3\,\mathbf{a}_1 - \mathbf{a}_2 - 2\,\mathbf{a}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0}. \]

So \(3\mathbf{a}_1 = \mathbf{a}_2 + 2\mathbf{a}_3\): column 1 lies in the plane spanned by columns 2 and 3, and only two of the three columns are independent.

Which \(\mathbf{b}\) lie in the plane? A normal vector to the plane is

\[ \mathbf{a}_2 \times \mathbf{a}_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -3 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \;\propto\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \]

and indeed \(\mathbf{a}_1 \cdot (1,1,-1) = 1 + 2 - 3 = 0\). Hence

\[ \mathbf{b} \text{ is reachable} \iff b_1 + b_2 - b_3 = 0. \]

For \(\mathbf{b} = (2,5,7)\): \(2 + 5 - 7 = 0\), solvable. For \(\mathbf{b} = (2,5,6)\): \(2 + 5 - 6 = 1 \neq 0\), not solvable.

ImportantOne vector, two roles

The vector \((1, 1, -1)\) is exactly the combination of equations used in the row picture: \((\text{eq. } 1) + (\text{eq. } 2) - (\text{eq. } 3)\). The same vector that combines the rows into \(\mathbf{0}\) is perpendicular to every column. The solvability condition \(b_1 + b_2 - b_3 = 0\) is the right-hand side of that combination, which must equal \(0\) rather than \(1\).

5.3 The Complete Solution When \(\mathbf{b}\) Is in the Plane

For \(\mathbf{b} = (2,5,7)\), note that \(\mathbf{a}_1 + \mathbf{a}_3 = (2,5,7)\), so \((u,v,w) = (1, 0, 1)\) is one solution. Adding any multiple of \((3, -1, -2)\), which produces \(\mathbf{0}\), does not change the right side:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ w \end{bmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{particular solution}} + \; t \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}}_{A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R}. \]

This is a line of solutions, matching configuration (c) in the row picture. The structure “one particular solution plus every solution of \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\)” holds for every consistent linear system.

6 Rows Fail Exactly When Columns Fail

The column dependence above was not a coincidence. The rows were dependent (row 1 + row 2 = row 3), and for a square matrix this forces the columns to be dependent as well.

ImportantFact

For an \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\), the following are equivalent:

  1. The \(n\) hyperplanes do not meet in exactly one point (row picture breaks down).
  2. The rows of \(A\) are linearly dependent.
  3. The columns of \(A\) are linearly dependent, i.e. they lie in a common subspace of dimension less than \(n\) (column picture breaks down).

Sketch of why rows dependent \(\Rightarrow\) columns dependent.

  1. If some row is a combination of the others, elimination subtracts exactly that combination and produces a zero row.
  2. A zero row means fewer than \(n\) pivots, so at least one column has no pivot. Its unknown is a free variable.
  3. Setting that free variable to \(1\) and solving by back-substitution gives \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}\) with \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\), which is precisely a nontrivial combination of the columns equal to \(\mathbf{0}\).

The converse follows by applying the same argument to \(A^{\mathsf T}\). This is a structural fact rather than a numerical accident, and it holds in every dimension.

7 Summary

Nonsingular Singular
Row picture \(n\) hyperplanes meet in one point No common point, or a line/plane/… of them
Column picture Columns span all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\); every \(\mathbf{b}\) is reachable in one way Columns lie in a lower-dimensional subspace; \(\mathbf{b}\) reachable only if it lies there, then in infinitely many ways
Rows / columns Independent Dependent (both, always together)
Number of solutions Exactly one Zero or infinitely many

The nonsingular case, where there is exactly one solution to be computed, is the problem that elimination solves efficiently. The singular case, where one must describe all solutions or decide that none exist, leads to the ideas of column space, null space, and rank.
