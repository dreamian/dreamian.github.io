Matrix Notation and Matrix Multiplication

Column rule, elementary matrices, associativity, and why order matters

Published

October 26, 2026

1 Matrix Notation

Writing out every equation and every elimination step works for a \(3\times 3\) system but becomes hopeless for large systems. Matrix notation records the system concisely, and matrix multiplication records the operations that simplify it.

1.1 A Running Example

Consider

\[ \begin{aligned} 2u + v + w &= 5 \\ 4u - 6v \phantom{{}+ w} &= -2 \\ -2u + 7v + 2w &= 9 . \end{aligned} \]

Three kinds of quantities appear: nine coefficients, three unknowns, and three right-hand sides. They are collected as

\[ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & -6 & 0 \\ -2 & 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad x = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ w \end{bmatrix}, \qquad b = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -2 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}, \]

and the system becomes \(Ax = b\), with solution \(x = (1, 1, 2)\). Here \(x = (1,1,2)\) written on a line is still a column vector, just temporarily lying down.

In general, \(m\) equations in \(n\) unknowns give an \(m \times n\) coefficient matrix,

\[ Ax = b, \qquad A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\; x \in \mathbb{R}^n,\; b \in \mathbb{R}^m , \]

where \(a_{ij}\) denotes the entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) of \(A\). \(A\) is square when \(m = n\) and rectangular otherwise. Matrices of the same shape are added, and matrices are multiplied by scalars, one entry at a time, exactly as vectors are.

1.2 Two Views of \(Ax\)

The product \(Ax\) is defined so that \(Ax = b\) reproduces the original system. Its first component is row 1 of \(A\) “times” the column \(x\):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ w \end{bmatrix} = 2u + v + w . \]

Row times column is the inner product: a \(1\times n\) matrix times an \(n \times 1\) matrix gives a \(1 \times 1\) matrix. At the solution, \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}(1,1,2)^\top = 2 + 1 + 2 = 5\). For the product to make sense, the length of each row of \(A\) (the number of columns) must equal the length of \(x\): an \(m \times n\) matrix maps \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to \(\mathbb{R}^m\).

The product \(Ax\) is a single double-indexed sum, but it can be grouped in two different ways.

Row view (dot products). The \(i\)-th component is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with \(x\):

\[ (Ax)_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} x_j = a_{i1}x_1 + a_{i2}x_2 + \cdots + a_{in}x_n . \]

The sum runs along row \(i\) of \(A\). (In Einstein’s tensor notation the repeated index implies summation, and one writes simply \(a_{ij}x_j\).)

Column view (linear combination). Write \(A = [\,a_1\ a_2\ \cdots\ a_n\,]\) with \(a_j\) the \(j\)-th column. Then

\[ Ax = x_1 a_1 + x_2 a_2 + \cdots + x_n a_n . \]

Derivation. Compare the \(i\)-th components:

\[ (Ax)_i = \sum_j a_{ij} x_j = \sum_j x_j\, (a_j)_i = \Big( \sum_j x_j a_j \Big)_i . \]

Both views are the same sum; the row view fixes \(i\) and sums over \(j\), while the column view adds whole column vectors at once. For the running example,

\[ A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -6 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -2 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}. \]

1.3 Why the Column Rule Matters

The row view is convenient for computation; the column view exposes the structure of \(Ax = b\).

  1. Existence. \(Ax = b\) asks whether \(b\) is a linear combination of the columns of \(A\). A solution exists iff \(b \in C(A)\), the column space.
  2. Uniqueness. \(Ax = 0\) has a nonzero solution iff the columns are linearly dependent. This leads to the nullspace, independence, and rank.
  3. Extension to products. If \(B = [\,b_1 \cdots b_p\,]\), then \[ AB = [\,Ab_1\ \cdots\ Ab_p\,]. \] Every column of \(AB\) is a combination of the columns of \(A\), so \(C(AB) \subseteq C(A)\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(AB) \le \operatorname{rank}(A)\).
  4. The row rule. By the same argument, \(y^\top A\) is a combination of the rows of \(A\). This is why multiplying by a matrix on the left performs row operations.
NoteWireless intuition

In a MIMO model \(y = Hx + n\), the column rule reads \(Hx = \sum_j x_j h_j\): the symbol from transmit antenna \(j\) rides along its channel vector \(h_j\). The received signal space is \(C(H)\), and the number of spatially multiplexable streams is \(\operatorname{rank}(H)\).

2 Homogeneous and Inhomogeneous Systems

The right-hand side \(b\) is the vector of inhomogeneous terms: the terms that do not multiply any unknown.

Type Form Meaning
Homogeneous \(Ax = 0\) right-hand side is zero
Inhomogeneous \(Ax = b,\ b \neq 0\) right-hand side is nonzero

Homogeneous systems always have the trivial solution \(x = 0\), and their solution set is a subspace:

\[ Ax_1 = 0,\ Ax_2 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; A(c_1x_1 + c_2x_2) = 0 . \]

This subspace is the nullspace \(N(A)\).

Inhomogeneous systems may have no solution (exactly when \(b \notin C(A)\)), and their solution set is not a subspace: \(0\) is not a solution, and \(A(x_1 + x_2) = 2b \neq b\).

Structure of the solution set. If \(x_p\) is any particular solution of \(Ax = b\), then

\[ \{\, x : Ax = b \,\} = x_p + N(A). \]

Proof. If \(Ax = b\), then \(A(x - x_p) = b - b = 0\), so \(x - x_p \in N(A)\). Conversely, if \(x_n \in N(A)\), then \(A(x_p + x_n) = b\). \(\blacksquare\)

Geometrically, the solution set is the nullspace translated by \(x_p\), an affine set that does not pass through the origin. The same decomposition, “general solution = particular + homogeneous”, appears for linear differential equations, because it follows from linearity alone.

3 Matrix Multiplication

3.1 Two Requirements That Force the Rule

How should two matrices be multiplied? Two natural requirements determine the answer.

  1. Consistency with elimination. Applying an elimination matrix \(E\) to both sides of \(Ax = b\) gives \(E(Ax) = Eb\). We want this to equal \((EA)x = Eb\), where \(EA\) is the coefficient matrix after the step. So \(EA\) must be built so that \((EA)x = E(Ax)\) for every \(x\), and then we simply write \(EAx\).
  2. Consistency with matrix–vector products. If \(B\) has a single column \(x\), then \(AB\) should equal \(Ax\). If \(B = [\,b_1\ b_2\ b_3\,]\) has several columns, then \[ AB = A\,[\,b_1\ b_2\ b_3\,] = [\,Ab_1\ Ab_2\ Ab_3\,]. \]

There is only one rule satisfying these, and it makes everything work.

3.2 The Rule

If \(A\) is \(m\times n\) and \(B\) is \(n \times p\), then \(AB\) is \(m \times p\). The number of columns of \(A\) must equal the number of rows of \(B\), so that \(A\) can multiply each column of \(B\). (Square matrices must have the same size.) The \((i,j)\) entry is the inner product of row \(i\) of \(A\) and column \(j\) of \(B\):

\[ (AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}. \]

For instance, if \(A\) is \(3 \times 4\) and \(B\) is \(4 \times 2\), then \(AB\) is \(3\times 2\) and

\[ (AB)_{32} = a_{31}b_{12} + a_{32}b_{22} + a_{33}b_{32} + a_{34}b_{42}. \]

The rule is the same whether the product is written \(AB\), \(EA\), \(PA\), \(LU\), or \(LDU\).

3.3 A Worked Example

\[ AB = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 5 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 17 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 8 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

  • Entry view. \(17 = (2)(1) + (3)(5)\) uses row 1 of \(A\) and column 1 of \(B\); \(8 = (4)(2) + (0)(-1)\) uses row 2 and column 2.
  • Column view. \(A\) multiplies each column of \(B\) separately. The third column of \(B\) is zero, so the third column of \(AB\) is zero. Every column of \(AB\) is a combination of the columns of \(A\).
  • Row view. Row 1 of \(AB\) uses the numbers \(2, 3\) from row 1 of \(A\): \[ 2\,[\,1\ \ 2\ \ 0\,] + 3\,[\,5\ \ -1\ \ 0\,] = [\,17\ \ 1\ \ 0\,]. \] Every row of \(AB\) is a combination of the rows of \(B\), exactly as in elimination.

Two more small examples:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{row exchange}} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 7 & 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 8 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad IA = A, \quad BI = B . \]

3.4 Four Ways to Read \(AB\)

The same product has four equivalent readings:

View Statement
Entry \((AB)_{ij} = (\text{row } i \text{ of } A)\cdot(\text{column } j \text{ of } B)\)
Column column \(j\) of \(AB\) \(= A\,(\text{column } j \text{ of } B)\)
Row row \(i\) of \(AB\) \(= (\text{row } i \text{ of } A)\,B\)
Outer product \(AB = \sum_{k} (\text{column } k \text{ of } A)(\text{row } k \text{ of } B)\)

Each column-times-row term in the last view is a rank-one matrix, so \(AB\) is a sum of \(n\) simple matrices. For example,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 & 0 \\ 6 & 6 & 0 \\ 6 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 8 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 & 0 \\ 10 & 14 & 4 \\ 7 & 8 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

3.5 Block Multiplication

All four readings are special cases of block multiplication: if \(A\) and \(B\) are cut into blocks whose shapes are compatible, the blocks multiply like scalar entries (keeping their order). For example,

\[ \begin{bmatrix} A & B \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = AC + BD . \]

Cutting \(B\) into columns gives the column view, cutting \(A\) into rows gives the row view, rows of \(A\) against columns of \(B\) gives the entry view, and columns of \(A\) against rows of \(B\) gives the outer-product view.

Block elimination on a \(2\times 2\) block matrix with invertible \(A\) subtracts \(CA^{-1}\) times the first block row from the second:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ -CA^{-1} & I \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & D - CA^{-1}B \end{bmatrix}, \]

and \(S = D - CA^{-1}B\) is the Schur complement. With \(1 \times 1\) leading block \(a\), this is exactly elimination on column 1: the block below the pivot becomes zero.

4 Elementary Matrices

An elementary matrix is obtained by applying a single elementary row operation to the identity \(I\). Multiplying by it on the left performs the same operation on any matrix.

4.1 Elimination Matrix \(E_{ij}\)

The elimination step “subtract \(\ell\) times row \(j\) from row \(i\)” is represented by \(I\) with its \((i,j)\) entry replaced by \(-\ell\):

\[ E_{ij} = I - \ell\, e_i e_j^\top . \]

For example, in the \(3\times 3\) case, “row 2 \(\leftarrow\) row 2 \(- \ell\cdot\) row 1” is

\[ E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -\ell & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E_{21} \begin{bmatrix} r_1 \\ r_2 \\ r_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} r_1 \\ r_2 - \ell r_1 \\ r_3 \end{bmatrix}. \]

4.2 One Elimination Step on the Running Example

The first step subtracts \(2\times\) equation 1 from equation 2. On the right-hand side,

\[ Eb = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -2 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -12 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Components 1 and 3 are unchanged because rows 1 and 3 of \(E\) are rows of \(I\). To keep the equation valid, the same operation must be applied to \(Ax\), giving \(E(Ax) = Eb\), and on the coefficient matrix

\[ EA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & -6 & 0 \\ -2 & 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -8 & -2 \\ -2 & 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The new system is simpler (a zero now sits below the first pivot) and equivalent (adding \(2\times\) equation 1 back to equation 2 recovers the original), so both systems have exactly the same solution \(x\).

4.3 Why Left Multiplication Is a Row Operation

By the row rule,

\[ (EA)_{i,:} = \sum_k e_{ik}\, A_{k,:}, \]

so row \(i\) of \(EA\) is a combination of the rows of \(A\) with coefficients from row \(i\) of \(E\). Row 2 of \(E_{21}\) is \((-\ell, 1, 0)\), giving \(-\ell r_1 + r_2\); all other rows of \(E_{21}\) are rows of \(I\), so those rows are unchanged. By the column rule, multiplication on the right performs column operations.

4.4 Three Types and Their Inverses

Type Operation Matrix Inverse
Row addition \(r_i \leftarrow r_i - \ell r_j\) \(I - \ell e_i e_j^\top\) \(I + \ell e_i e_j^\top\)
Row exchange \(r_i \leftrightarrow r_j\) \(P_{ij}\) (\(I\) with rows \(i,j\) swapped) \(P_{ij}\) itself
Scaling \(r_i \leftarrow c\, r_i,\ c\neq 0\) \(I\) with \((i,i)\) entry \(c\) \((i,i)\) entry \(1/c\)

Check of the first inverse. For \(i \neq j\), \(e_i e_j^\top e_i e_j^\top = (e_j^\top e_i)\, e_i e_j^\top = 0\), so

\[ (I - \ell e_i e_j^\top)(I + \ell e_i e_j^\top) = I - \ell^2\, e_i e_j^\top e_i e_j^\top = I . \]

4.5 Elimination as a Matrix Product

Forward elimination on a \(3 \times 3\) matrix (without row exchanges) is

\[ E_{32} E_{31} E_{21}\, A = U \quad (\text{upper triangular}). \]

\(E_{21}\) acts first, so it sits closest to \(A\). Inverting gives

\[ A = E_{21}^{-1} E_{31}^{-1} E_{32}^{-1}\, U = LU, \]

which is the starting point of the \(LU\) factorization.

5 Laws of Matrix Multiplication

Matrix multiplication obeys the familiar laws

\[ (AB)C = A(BC), \qquad A(B + C) = AB + AC, \qquad (B + C)D = BD + CD . \]

The law that fails is more interesting: matrix multiplication is not commutative. In general, \(AB \neq BA\).

5.1 Shape Condition

Let \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\), \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times p}\), \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{p\times q}\). The condition for the products to exist pairs up respectively:

Rows of Columns of Common length Product defined
\(A\) \(B\) \(n\) \(AB \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times p}\)
\(B\) \(C\) \(p\) \(BC \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times q}\)

Then both \((AB)C\) and \(A(BC)\) are \(m \times q\). No product such as \((AB)(BC)\) is involved; it would generally not even be defined.

5.2 Associativity via the Column Rule

Lemma. For any \(x \in \mathbb{R}^p\), \(A(Bx) = (AB)x\).

Proof. Let \(b_1,\dots,b_p\) be the columns of \(B\).

  • Left side: \(Bx = \sum_k x_k b_k\) by the column rule, so by distributivity \(A(Bx) = \sum_k x_k\,(Ab_k)\).
  • Right side: the columns of \(AB\) are \(Ab_1,\dots,Ab_p\), so by the column rule \((AB)x = \sum_k x_k\,(Ab_k)\).

The two expressions agree. \(\blacksquare\)

With \(A = E\) and \(B = A\), this is exactly the requirement \((EA)x = E(Ax)\) that motivated the multiplication rule.

Intuition. \(A(Bx)\) means “combine the columns of \(B\) first, then transform the result by \(A\).” \((AB)x\) means “transform each column of \(B\) by \(A\) in advance, then combine with the same weights.” These agree because \(A\) preserves linear combinations: combine-then-transform equals transform-then-combine. In fact, \(AB\) is defined so that this holds: it stores the operation “apply \(B\), then apply \(A\)” as a single matrix.

Theorem (associativity). \((AB)C = A(BC)\).

Proof. Let \(c_1,\dots,c_q\) be the columns of \(C\). Compare column \(j\):

\[ \big[(AB)C\big]_{:,j} \overset{\text{column rule}}{=} (AB)\,c_j \overset{\text{lemma}}{=} A\,(Bc_j) \overset{\text{column rule}}{=} A\,\big[BC\big]_{:,j} \overset{\text{column rule}}{=} \big[A(BC)\big]_{:,j}. \]

All columns agree. \(\blacksquare\)

In words: if \(C\) is a single vector, associativity is the lemma; if \(C\) has several columns, place them side by side and apply the lemma to each.

The row view gives the mirror-image argument. With \(a_i^\top\) the \(i\)-th row of \(A\),

\[ \big[A(BC)\big]_{i,:} = a_i^\top (BC) = (a_i^\top B)\, C = \big[(AB)C\big]_{i,:}, \]

where the middle step is the row version of the lemma, \(y^\top(BC) = (y^\top B)C\).

Entrywise check. Associativity is just an interchange of finite sums:

\[ \big[(AB)C\big]_{ij} = \sum_l \Big(\sum_k a_{ik} b_{kl}\Big) c_{lj} = \sum_k \sum_l a_{ik} b_{kl} c_{lj} = \sum_k a_{ik} \Big(\sum_l b_{kl} c_{lj}\Big) = \big[A(BC)\big]_{ij}. \]

This is a valid proof, but it shows that the identity holds, not why. The column-rule argument shows why.

TipOne-line summary

Acting on a vector, \(ABC\) means “apply \(C\), then \(B\), then \(A\).” Parentheses only decide which two consecutive steps are pre-combined into one matrix; they never change the order of action. So we simply write \(ABC\).

Law What changes Result
Commutativity: \(AB\) vs \(BA\) order of action generally different
Associativity: \((AB)C\) vs \(A(BC)\) grouping only always equal

The practical payoff for elimination is that

\[ E_{32}\big(E_{31}(E_{21}A)\big) = (E_{32}E_{31}E_{21})\,A , \]

so all elimination steps can be collected into a single matrix acting on \(A\).

5.3 Distributivity

\[ A(B + C) = AB + AC, \qquad (B + C)D = BD + CD, \]

whenever \(B\) and \(C\) have the same shape and \(A\), \(D\) have the right sizes for multiplication on the left and right. Entrywise, it is just \(\sum_k a_{ik}(b_{kj} + c_{kj}) = \sum_k a_{ik}b_{kj} + \sum_k a_{ik}c_{kj}\).

5.4 Non-Commutativity: Composition of Operations

Read as operations, \(AB\) means “do \(B\) first, then \(A\).” Reversing the order of two operations generally changes the outcome. Shapes alone can already prevent commuting: if \(A\) is \(m \times n\) and \(B\) is \(n \times m\) with \(m \ne n\), then \(AB\) is \(m\times m\) while \(BA\) is \(n \times n\).

Even among square matrices, only very special ones commute with everything.

Claim. If \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\) satisfies \(AB = BA\) for every \(B\), then \(A = cI\).

Proof. Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\). With \(B_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \[ AB_1 = \begin{bmatrix} a & 0 \\ c & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B_1A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; b = c = 0 . \] With \(B_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), \[ AB_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B_2A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & d \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; a = d . \qquad \blacksquare \]

6 Why Order Matters for Elimination Matrices

6.1 The Setup

Matrix Operation Outer-product form
\(E\) row 2 \(\leftarrow\) row 2 \(- 2\cdot\) row 1 \(I - 2 e_2 e_1^\top\)
\(F\) row 3 \(\leftarrow\) row 3 \(+\) row 1 \(I + e_3 e_1^\top\)
\(G\) row 3 \(\leftarrow\) row 3 \(+\) row 2 \(I + e_3 e_2^\top\)

Read \(e_i e_j^\top\) as “read row \(j\), write into row \(i\).”

6.2 When Do Two Row Operations Commute?

Multiply two row-addition matrices:

\[ (I + a\, e_i e_j^\top)(I + b\, e_k e_l^\top) = I + a\, e_i e_j^\top + b\, e_k e_l^\top + ab\, e_i \underbrace{(e_j^\top e_k)}_{\delta_{jk}} e_l^\top . \]

The cross term survives only when \(j = k\), that is, when the row read by the later operation is the row written by the earlier one. Then information propagates from one operation into the other.

  • \(EF = FE\). Both \(E\) and \(F\) only read row 1, and neither writes into row 1. Neither sees the other’s output, so the order is irrelevant. In either order, rows 2 and 3 are changed using row 1, and the product does both steps at once: \[ EF = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = FE . \]
  • \(GE\) (\(E\) first). \(G\) reads row 2, which \(E\) has already changed to \(r_2 - 2r_1\). The term \(-2r_1\) propagates into row 3.
  • \(EG\) (\(G\) first). \(G\) adds the original \(r_2\) to row 3. When \(E\) later modifies row 2, row 3 is already finished, so it feels no effect from row 1.

\[ GE = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{-2} & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \qquad\text{but}\qquad EG = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{0} & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Random matrices also fail to commute. What these examples add is the reason, read off from the flow of information between rows.

6.3 All Three at Once: \(GFE\) versus \(EFG\)

\(GFE\): the true order of elimination (\(E \to F \to G\)). Tracking rows:

  • \(E\): \(r_2' = r_2 - 2r_1\)
  • \(F\): \(r_3' = r_3 + r_1\)
  • \(G\): \(r_3'' = r_3' + r_2' = r_3 + r_1 + (r_2 - 2r_1) = -r_1 + r_2 + r_3\)

\[ GFE = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{-1} & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The \((3,1)\) entry \(-1 = \underbrace{1}_{F} + \underbrace{1}_{G}\cdot\underbrace{(-2)}_{E}\) is a mixed term created by the cross term. \(GFE\) is the matrix that takes the original \(A\) to the upper triangular \(U\).

\(EFG\): the reverse order (\(G \to F \to E\)). The candidate cross terms are

\[ E\cdot G:\ e_2 \underbrace{e_1^\top e_3}_{0} e_2^\top, \qquad E\cdot F:\ e_2 \underbrace{e_1^\top e_3}_{0} e_1^\top, \qquad F\cdot G:\ e_3 \underbrace{e_1^\top e_3}_{0} e_2^\top , \]

and all of them vanish. Hence

\[ EFG = I - 2e_2e_1^\top + e_3e_1^\top + e_3e_2^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{1} & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The numbers \(-2\) from \(E\) and \(1\) from \(F\) and \(G\) go straight into the product, undisturbed. A row-by-row trace confirms this: \(G\) gives \(r_3 + r_2\), \(F\) gives \(r_3 + r_2 + r_1\), and \(E\) changes only row 2, so row 3 is \(r_1 + r_2 + r_3\).

6.4 Reversing Elimination Gives the Clean Order

\(GFE\) \(EFG\)
Meaning actual elimination order; sends \(A \to U\) wrong order for elimination
Entries multipliers get mixed multipliers appear unchanged

Undoing elimination reverses the order automatically:

\[ (GFE)^{-1} = E^{-1} F^{-1} G^{-1}. \]

This is exactly the order in which cross terms vanish. Each inverse simply flips a sign (\(E^{-1} = I + 2e_2e_1^\top\), \(F^{-1} = I - e_3e_1^\top\), \(G^{-1} = I - e_3e_2^\top\)), so

\[ L = E^{-1}F^{-1}G^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The multipliers \(\ell_{21} = 2\), \(\ell_{31} = -1\), \(\ell_{32} = -1\) are recorded directly in \(L\). The forward elimination matrix is messy, but its inverse is clean. This is why elimination is best viewed as the factorization \(A = LU\).

6.5 Products of Lower Triangular Matrices

Claim. If \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) are lower triangular, then so is \(L_1 L_2\).

Proof. Take \(i < j\). Then

\[ (L_1 L_2)_{ij} = \sum_k (L_1)_{ik} (L_2)_{kj}. \]

A nonzero \((L_1)_{ik}\) requires \(k \le i\), and a nonzero \((L_2)_{kj}\) requires \(k \ge j\). Since \(j > i\), no \(k\) satisfies both, so the sum is zero. \(\blacksquare\)

Row-operation view. Each lower triangular elimination step adds a multiple of a higher row to a lower row. Information flows only downward, so any composition of such steps stays lower triangular.

7 Summary

  • \(Ax\) is a combination of the columns of \(A\); \(y^\top A\) is a combination of the rows. The column view governs existence (\(b \in C(A)\)) and uniqueness (\(N(A)\)).
  • The solution set of \(Ax = b\) is \(x_p + N(A)\), an affine translate of the nullspace.
  • Left multiplication performs row operations; right multiplication performs column operations. Elementary matrices encode single row operations and are trivially invertible.
  • Associativity follows from the lemma \(A(Bx) = (AB)x\), which holds because \(A\) preserves linear combinations. Parentheses change grouping, not order of action.
  • Commutativity fails because order of action matters. For row-addition matrices, a cross term appears exactly when a later step reads a row that an earlier step wrote.
  • Elimination in its true order (\(GFE\)) mixes the multipliers, while the reversed order (\(E^{-1}F^{-1}G^{-1} = L\)) records them cleanly. This leads to \(A = LU\).
